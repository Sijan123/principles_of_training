Fibre Types & Motor Units

Slow, fast, and the recruitment order that decides who works

Your eyes and your calves are both controlled by motor neurons, but a single neuron running to an eye muscle might command as few as ten muscle fibres, while a single neuron running to the calf's gastrocnemius can command close to two thousand. That mismatch is not an accident of wiring — it is a direct readout of what each muscle is for. Fine, instantly adjustable control needs small commands over small groups of fibres. Bulk, economical force needs one nerve impulse to move a small army at once. Before any discussion of training can mean anything, it has to start here: with the unit the nervous system actually controls, and the strict, orderly rule it uses to decide which ones to switch on.

5.1The Motor Unit: One Neuron, Many Fibres

A motor unit is a single alpha motor neuron together with every muscle fibre it connects to. This is the true all-or-nothing unit of voluntary muscle control: the nervous system cannot fire half of a motor unit. An impulse down that neuron either arrives or it doesn't, and when it does, every fibre belonging to that unit contracts together, essentially simultaneously. You never choose to contract "some fibres" in a muscle directly — you choose how many whole motor units to switch on, and how fast to re-fire each one.

How many fibres one neuron commands — the innervation ratio — varies enormously by muscle, and it is a clean proxy for how fine or how coarse that muscle's control needs to be. The extraocular muscles that aim your eyes have innervation ratios in the region of 10–20 fibres per neuron C, enough for the minute, continuous adjustments that keep your gaze locked on a moving target. The gastrocnemius, by contrast, averages roughly 1,000–2,000 fibres per neuron C — a single nerve impulse recruiting an entire battalion at once, because walking and jumping need bulk force delivered economically, not minute gradation.

In plain terms

Think of a motor unit as a single light switch wired to a cluster of bulbs, not a dimmer wired to one bulb.

Flip the switch and every bulb on that circuit comes on at full brightness together — there is no partial setting. A room wired with many small circuits, a few bulbs each, gives you fine control over total brightness: flip one switch, a small change. A room wired with a few enormous circuits, hundreds of bulbs each, only gives you big jumps: flip one switch, the whole room changes. Your eye muscles are wired like the first room. Your calves are wired like the second. Both are reasonable wiring plans for what each room is actually used for.

5.2Three Fibre Types, One Spectrum

Every fibre within a single motor unit is the same type, and muscle fibres themselves come in a small number of recognisably different types, classified mainly by which myosin heavy chain (MHC) isoform they build their thick filaments from — a molecular detail that happens to predict almost everything else about how the fibre behaves. The three types that matter for training are usually labelled type I, type IIa and type IIx (an older scheme also described a type IIb, now understood to be the human equivalent of IIx in most tissue).

Treat type IIx as the extreme sprinting end of a spectrum rather than a wholly separate category: a fibre's actual myosin content often blends two isoforms, which is why biopsy samples regularly contain measurable numbers of IIa/IIx hybrid fibres rather than everything sorting neatly into three bins. This blending turns out to matter a great deal for how training reshapes fibre composition, in section five.

5.3Henneman's Size Principle: Smallest First, Every Time

Given a muscle built from a mix of all three fibre types, which motor units does the nervous system actually switch on for a given task? The answer, established by Elwood Henneman in 1957 and confirmed many times since, is startlingly orderly: motor units are recruited from smallest to largest, in a fixed sequence, regardless of the task. This is the size principle. Small motor neurons, attached to small numbers of type I fibres, have the lowest electrical threshold and fire first for any contraction, however light. As the nervous system demands more force, it recruits progressively larger neurons, attached to progressively larger, faster, more fatigable fibre populations, strictly in order of size — never the reverse, and never skipping ahead to a big unit while leaving a smaller, lower-threshold one unrecruited.

The direct consequence is one of the most useful ideas in this book: a light contraction physically cannot recruit your biggest, strongest, most powerful motor units, no matter how much you want it to. Lifting a pencil recruits a handful of small, slow units and stops there, because that is already enough force; the large, fast, type IIx-dominant units simply are not called on, because the nervous system never needs to go that far up the queue.

Key idea

There are two genuine exceptions to strict smallest-first ordering, and both are directly useful for training. First, explosive intent — deliberately trying to move a light or moderate load as fast as possible — compresses the normal recruitment thresholds, bringing large, fast units into play earlier than a slow, calm contraction of the same external force would. Second, as a set continues toward fatigue, the small units recruited first begin to tire and produce less force each, and the nervous system compensates by recruiting additional, higher-threshold units it would not otherwise have needed — which is exactly why the last few repetitions of a demanding set reach fibres the first few reps never touched, even at a load far below maximum.

Worked example
Marcus, 29, performs a set of 12 leg-press reps at 65% of his one-rep max, taken to the point where rep 12 is a genuine struggle.
  1. At 65% of 1RM, the force demanded at the very start of the set is well below maximum, so the size principle predicts recruitment of only low- and mid-threshold motor units — mostly type I and the lower end of type IIa.Reps 1–3: low–mid threshold units only
  2. Those units begin to fatigue as the set continues; each one produces progressively less force per fibre even though it is still firing.Reps 4–8: force per already-recruited unit falling
  3. To keep moving the same 65% load, Marcus's nervous system must recruit additional, higher-threshold units to replace the force the fatiguing units can no longer supply.Reps 8–10: progressively higher-threshold units join
  4. By reps 10–12, with the lighter units substantially fatigued, the set can only continue by calling on the largest, fastest, most fatigable type IIx-dominant units — the ones a single easy rep at this load would never have reached.Reps 10–12: near-maximal units recruited for the first time this set
So: a 65% load reaches Marcus's highest-threshold motor units only in the last three reps of the set — not because the load changed, but because fatigue forced the nervous system up the recruitment queue. Those final three reps are doing almost all of the work that matters for growth and strength in those fibres; the first nine are largely the cost of admission.

5.4Rate Coding: More Force Without More Units

Recruiting an additional motor unit is not the only way to add force. Once all the motor units relevant to a task are already switched on, further increases in force come from firing each one faster — a mechanism called rate coding. A single twitch from one stimulus produces a brief, small pulse of force; fire the same motor unit repeatedly in quick succession and the individual twitches begin to overlap and summate before the previous one fully decays, building toward a smooth, sustained, substantially larger force called a tetanic contraction. Recruitment decides which units are contributing; rate coding decides how hard each contributing unit is pulling. Near-maximal voluntary efforts in large muscles rely on both simultaneously — the full roster recruited, each member firing as fast as it can.

5.5Composition Varies by Muscle — and by Person

Fibre-type proportion is not fixed across the body; it tracks each muscle's habitual job. The soleus, active almost continuously during standing and walking (see chapter 3), is commonly 80–90% type I in untrained adults B — about as slow-twitch-dominant as human muscle gets. Muscles built for short, powerful bursts rather than sustained postural work, such as the triceps brachii, typically run closer to an even split, or even a fast-twitch majority, though reported values vary considerably between studies and sampling sites C.

Within any one muscle, fibre-type proportion also varies substantially between individuals, and twin studies attribute a large share of that variation to genetics: heritability estimates for the proportion of type I fibres cluster in the region of 40–50%, based on correlations that run noticeably higher in identical twins than in fraternal twins or non-twin siblings B. This is a real, substantial genetic contribution — and, exactly as with the heritability of VO2max discussed in chapter 1, it describes the spread across a population, not a ceiling on what training can change in any one person.

What training reliably does change is the balance within the fast-twitch population, not the broad I-versus-II split. Essentially any form of resistance training shifts a measurable fraction of type IIx fibres toward the type IIa profile — more oxidative capacity, better fatigue resistance, while remaining fast and capable of high force. Reported shifts vary by study and training type, but the direction is remarkably consistent: hybrid IIa/IIx fibres, and pure IIx fibres, both tend to shrink as a proportion of the total after even a few weeks of regular training, with pure IIa fibres taking up the difference. Genuine interconversion between type I and type II — a fibre switching its fundamental speed category rather than shifting within the fast-twitch spectrum — appears to be minimal in adult human muscle under ordinary training conditions; most of the evidence for it comes from extreme interventions such as chronic low-frequency electrical stimulation in animal models, not from normal resistance or endurance programmes C.

The paper
Henneman E. Relation between size of neurons and their susceptibility to discharge. Science 1957;126(3287):1345–7.
Who

Cat spinal motor neurons and their associated muscle fibres, studied via direct electrophysiological recording.

What

Recorded the order in which individual motor axons began firing as the strength of an electrical stimulus to the nerve was gradually increased.

Found

Axons were recruited in a strict order correlated with their size, from smallest to largest, as stimulus strength rose — and de-recruited in the reverse order as it fell. This orderly relationship became the size principle.

Why it matters: it gave training science its single most load-bearing idea about who actually does the work during a set — recruitment order is not random or task-specific, it is size-ordered and predictable. Limitation: direct electrical stimulation of an isolated nerve in an anaesthetised cat is not a voluntary human contraction; the exceptions covered above (explosive intent, fatigue) were established by later human work, not by this original experiment.

The paper
Plotkin DL, Roberts MD, Haun CT, Schoenfeld BJ. Muscle fiber type transitions with exercise training: shifting perspectives. Sports 2021;9(9):127.
Who

A synthesis of human resistance- and endurance-training studies measuring myosin heavy chain isoform distribution before and after training.

What

Reviewed fibre-type transitions reported across training modalities, including hybrid-fibre data and detraining and cross-education findings.

Found

Resistance training consistently shifts the fast-twitch population from type IIx and IIa/IIx hybrid fibres toward pure type IIa; true type I–II interconversion in adult humans under normal training is weakly supported at best, with most of that evidence instead coming from extreme experimental models.

Why it matters: it corrects a common oversimplification — that training "converts" slow fibres to fast or vice versa — and replaces it with the better-supported picture of a fast-twitch spectrum shifting within itself. Limitation: a narrative review drawing on studies with different biopsy methods and training durations, not a single controlled trial; some disagreement between included studies is acknowledged in the original.

5.6What This Means for Programming

Put recruitment order and rate coding together and a question that sounds like a contradiction resolves cleanly: training guides claim that heavy loads, explosive intent, and sets taken near failure all reach fast, high-threshold fibres — so which one is actually true? All three are, because they are three different routes up the same recruitment queue, not three different queues.

  • Heavy load reaches high-threshold units directly: a load near maximum simply cannot be moved without recruiting almost the entire motor-unit pool from the first repetition, because nothing less is strong enough to move it.
  • Explosive intent reaches them by compressing recruitment thresholds: trying to move even a moderate load as fast as possible brings large, fast units into play earlier than the same load moved deliberately would.
  • Sets taken close to failure reach them by attrition: as demonstrated in Marcus's worked example above, fatigue in the early-recruited, low-threshold units forces the nervous system to call up higher-threshold units later in the same set, even though the external load never changed.
  • What does not reliably reach them is a light load, moved at a comfortable, unhurried pace, stopped well short of fatigue — none of the three routes above is open, so the high-threshold units simply never get recruited.
Myth vs fact
“Light weights can't build the same fibres as heavy weights — you need to lift heavy to reach fast-twitch muscle”

If the bar is light, the high-threshold motor units are never in the room.

True only if the set stays far from failure

A light load lifted for a handful of easy reps never reaches high-threshold units, exactly as the myth states. But a light load lifted to or near failure recruits the same full motor-unit pool as a heavy load does, through the fatigue route rather than the load route — a finding repeatedly confirmed by comparable hypertrophy outcomes between heavy low-rep and light high-rep training when both are taken close to failure. The variable that matters is how close to failure the set finishes, not the number on the bar by itself.

Activate it

Reaching high-threshold motor units through all three available routes across a week, rather than relying on just one.

  • Do Rotate a heavy compound lift, an explosive-intent lift, and a near-failure higher-rep lift across the week on the same movement pattern — e.g. heavy back squat, jump squat, and a leg-press set taken to RIR 0–1.
  • Load Heavy day: 85–92% of 1RM. Explosive day: 30–50% of 1RM, moved as fast as technique allows. Near-failure day: a load that allows 10–15 reps before failure.
  • Dose Heavy: 3–5 sets × 2–4 reps. Explosive: 4–6 sets × 3–5 reps, full recovery between sets. Near-failure: 2–3 sets to RIR 0–1.
  • When Spread across the week, 48 h apart on the same pattern, so no single session is asked to deliver all three stimuli at once.
  • Progress Add load on the heavy day only once bar speed there is unchanged week to week; add reps before load on the near-failure day.
  • On the bike A short, maximal standing sprint (8–15 s, full recovery) is the explosive-intent route on a bike — high force, high velocity, compressed recruitment thresholds — while a long, grinding, low-cadence seated interval near failure reaches high-threshold units by the fatigue route instead; the two feel very different and are training the same motor units through different doors.
  • Signal You're doing it right when the explosive day feels fast and fresh, the heavy day feels controlled and grinding, and the near-failure day leaves the last few reps visibly slower than the first.
A That heavy load, explosive intent and near-failure effort each recruit high-threshold motor units is well established individually; the specific weekly split above is a reasonable synthesis rather than a single tested protocol.

Five things to carry forward

  1. A motor unit — one neuron plus every fibre it commands — is the true unit of muscular control, and how many fibres each neuron commands scales with how fine or coarse that muscle's job requires control to be.
  2. Type I, IIa and IIx fibres differ in speed, force, fatigue resistance and metabolic machinery, but IIa/IIx hybrid fibres show this is a spectrum, not three hard categories.
  3. Henneman's size principle recruits motor units strictly smallest-to-largest for any task, which is why a light, easy contraction physically cannot reach your biggest, fastest motor units — except through explosive intent or fatigue, the two genuine exceptions.
  4. Once a motor unit is recruited, further force comes from firing it faster (rate coding), not from recruiting it harder.
  5. Heavy load, explosive intent and sets taken near failure are three different routes to the same high-threshold fibres, not three competing methods, and true type I–II conversion is minimal — training mainly shifts the fast-twitch IIx-to-IIa balance.
