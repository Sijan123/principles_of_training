Hydration & Electrolytes

Sweat rate, sodium, and both ends of the fluid error

A marathon runner who finishes four hours and twenty minutes behind the winner has spent roughly twice as long sweating, drinking and making fluid decisions as the winner did — and that exposure time, not fitness, is the strongest predictor of who ends up in the medical tent with dangerously low blood sodium. Hydration science spent decades assuming fluid balance could only go wrong in one direction: too little water. The rarer failure runs the other way, it is caused by drinking, and it kills people the common failure almost never does.

32.1Fluid Balance Basics

About 60% of an adult's body mass is water — roughly 42 litres in a 70 kg person — split between two compartments. Two-thirds sits inside cells (intracellular fluid); the remaining third sits outside them (extracellular fluid), which itself splits into the fluid bathing tissues and the liquid part of blood, plasma, at around 3–3.5 litres. Plasma is the compartment that matters most during exercise, because it is the compartment the heart is pumping.

Sweat is drawn predominantly from plasma and the fluid immediately around cells, not evenly from all 42 litres. Lose a litre of sweat and plasma volume takes a disproportionate hit relative to its small total size. A smaller plasma volume means a smaller amount of blood returning to the heart each beat (stroke volume), which the heart compensates for by beating faster to hold cardiac output steady — the mechanism covered in full in chapter 9. This is why heart rate at a fixed, easy pace climbs measurably over a long hot session even when nothing else about the effort has changed: it is the direct, visible fingerprint of shrinking plasma volume, often called cardiovascular drift.

The same shrinking plasma volume competes against a second, simultaneous demand: skin blood flow needs to rise to carry heat to the surface for evaporative cooling. Early dehydration makes the body choose between delivering blood to working muscle, to the skin for cooling, and to the heart's own filling — a three-way competition for a shrinking pool, and the reason mild fluid deficits raise perceived effort and core temperature before they measurably slow you down.

In plain terms

Picture a delivery company with one van and three customers who all need stock replenished constantly: the muscles, the skin, and the heart's own fuel tank.

When the van is full (plasma volume normal), every customer gets what they need on time. Lose a fifth of the van's capacity and the dispatcher has to start rationing — sending slightly less to the skin (so you cool less efficiently and get hotter), slightly less to the muscle (so it works slightly harder for the same pace), and running more trips to keep the heart's own filling topped up (so heart rate climbs). None of the customers run out completely at first. They all just get served a little worse, which is exactly what "it feels harder for no obvious reason" actually is.

32.2Sweat — Two Numbers That Vary Enormously

Sweat has two independent properties, and conflating them is the single commonest confusion in this chapter. One is how much you produce (sweat rate); the other is how salty it is (sweat sodium concentration). They are controlled by largely different physiology, and an athlete can be high on one and low on the other.

Sweat rate ranges from roughly 0.5 litres per hour in a small, unfit person exercising gently in a cool room to over 2.5 L/h in a large, heat-acclimatised athlete working hard in heat and humidity — a five-fold range driven by body mass, fitness, heat acclimatisation, clothing, intensity and, dominantly, ambient temperature and humidity A. The same athlete's own sweat rate can roughly double between a cool training run and a hot, humid one, which is the single most important practical fact in this chapter: one fluid number does not serve one athlete all year.

Sweat sodium concentration ranges from around 200 mg/L in a well-adapted, sodium-conserving sweater to over 2000 mg/L in a genuine "salty sweater" B — a ten-fold range that is substantially genetic, reflects how efficiently the sweat duct reabsorbs sodium before it reaches the skin, and changes only modestly with heat acclimatisation or diet. This is why the visible white salt rings some athletes get on dark kit are a real and useful clue — they mean genuinely salty sweat, not simply a lot of it — but their absence does not rule out high losses in someone sweating heavily, because a moderate concentration multiplied by a large volume still adds up to serious sodium loss.

Multiply the two together and the range of plausible hourly sodium loss across real athletes spans roughly a twenty-fold difference — from under half a gram to several grams per hour. No single sports-drink formulation or electrolyte-tablet dose can be correct for that entire range, which is the practical argument, developed later in this chapter, for testing your own numbers rather than trusting a label.

32.3How Much Dehydration Actually Matters

The honest answer has a threshold in it, and the threshold is doing most of the work. Body-mass losses under roughly 2% have little measurable effect on endurance performance in most conditions B — thirst, comfort and pacing feel are affected well before any objective output measure is. Beyond roughly 2–3%, endurance performance and thermoregulation both start to decline measurably, and the effect is amplified sharply in heat B. Self-paced events seem more forgiving than fixed-intensity laboratory tests — some meta-analytic work finds self-paced performance holding up until losses reach 3–4%, because athletes who are dehydrating simply, often unconsciously, slow down rather than failing outright.

That last point is the centre of a real, ongoing argument, stated fairly rather than resolved by fiat. One camp argues for programmed drinking: calculate your sweat rate and drink to stay close to it on a schedule, because waiting for thirst in a hot, long, high-intensity event risks a deficit large enough to hurt both performance and safety margins. The other camp argues for drinking to thirst: thirst is a well-tuned signal, programmed drinking is the single biggest modifiable risk factor for the hyponatraemia discussed next, and much of the older research "proving" dehydration ruins performance used designs that do not resemble a real event — dehydrating participants before exercise with diuretics or saunas, then forcing them to hold a fixed, externally imposed pace in a lab, unable to self-pace the way they would in a race. Critics of that literature, most prominently exercise physiologist Éric Goulet, argue the field overstated dehydration's cost by generalising from that design to the self-paced question athletes actually face.

The practical synthesis, and the one this book uses: for sessions under about 60–90 minutes in cool conditions, drinking to thirst is almost certainly adequate. For longer, hotter, harder, or competitively important efforts — where the cost of discovering your sweat rate was higher than you guessed is a blown race, or worse — a fluid plan built from your own measured sweat rate, used as a ceiling rather than a rigid target, out-performs pure improvisation without tipping into the overdrinking that causes the next section's problem.

32.4Hyponatraemia — Rarer, And Far More Dangerous

Exercise-associated hyponatraemia (EAH) is a fall in blood sodium below the normal range (conventionally under 135 mmol/L), arising during or within 24 hours of prolonged exercise. It is rarer than clinically meaningful dehydration — reported in roughly 1–5% of finishers in studied marathons and ultramarathons, higher in some hot, slow, back-of-pack cohorts B — but severe cases cause cerebral and pulmonary oedema, and EAH has killed otherwise healthy endurance athletes. It is the one failure mode in this chapter that is genuinely a medical emergency rather than a performance problem.

The mechanism is simple once stated: sodium concentration in blood is a ratio, not an absolute amount. Drink more plain water (or any very low-sodium fluid) than you are losing in sweat, over several hours, and you dilute the sodium that remains — even though you have lost some sodium in sweat too, you have added disproportionately more water than salt. The kidneys can normally excrete several hundred millilitres of excess water per hour, but prolonged exercise itself blunts that excretion (partly via a hormone called arginine vasopressin, released under physical and psychological stress), which is why the same overdrinking that would be harmless at rest becomes dangerous mid-event.

Who develops exercise-associated hyponatraemia, and the mechanism behind each risk factor
Risk factorWhy it raises risk

Symptoms progress roughly in order: bloating and puffiness (a genuine clue, since true dehydration does not cause swelling), nausea and vomiting, headache, confusion and disorientation, and in severe cases seizure or loss of consciousness as brain swelling develops. The practical trap is that early EAH symptoms — nausea, feeling awful, wanting to stop — look identical to ordinary exertional distress or heat illness, and the instinctive response to all three is often "drink more," which is the one thing that makes EAH worse.

Where this goes wrong

Exercise-associated hyponatraemia is a medical emergency, and it is not something to self-diagnose or self-treat on the course. The single most useful field clue is weight gain during an endurance event, or visible swelling of hands, face or abdomen — true dehydration never does this, so gained weight plus confusion, vomiting or disorientation in anyone racing more than a couple of hours should be treated as suspected EAH until a clinician says otherwise. Event medical teams measure blood sodium directly when they suspect it; severe cases are treated with controlled hypertonic saline, administered only under medical supervision, because correcting sodium too fast carries its own serious risk. If you suspect EAH in yourself or someone else, stop, alert event medical staff immediately, and do not give the person more plain water or sports drink "just in case" — that is very often how it got dangerous in the first place.

The paper
Hew-Butler T, Rosner MH, Fowkes-Godek S, et al. Statement of the Third International Exercise-Associated Hyponatremia Consensus Development Conference, Carlsbad, California, 2015. Clin J Sport Med 2015;25(4):303–320.
Who

An international multidisciplinary panel of clinicians and researchers with direct experience managing EAH in endurance and ultra-endurance events.

What

A consensus synthesis of the pathophysiology, risk factors, field diagnosis and treatment protocol for exercise-associated hyponatraemia, updating two earlier conferences.

Found

EAH is overwhelmingly a disorder of excess water relative to sodium and training-associated changes in kidney water handling, not simply inadequate salt intake; overdrinking beyond thirst — often from well-intentioned programmed-drinking advice — was identified as the dominant modifiable cause, and weight gain during an event was confirmed as a reliable field red flag.

Why it matters: it is the document that shifted mainstream race-medicine guidance away from blanket "drink as much as you can" advice and toward thirst-guided intake for most participants, precisely because the alternative was causing a rarer but more lethal problem. Limitation: a consensus statement synthesises expert judgement and the available case literature rather than being a single controlled trial, and incidence estimates vary widely by event, climate and the sodium content of fluids provided on course.

32.5Measuring Your Own Sweat Rate

The calculation is simple arithmetic once you have the inputs. Weigh yourself nude (or in identical, wrung-dry kit) immediately before and after a session of known duration, add back any fluid you drank during it, and subtract urine if you went. The result, divided by session duration, is your sweat rate in that specific condition — and the condition matters as much as the number, because the same athlete's rate can double between a cool morning and a hot afternoon.

  1. Weigh before

    Nude or in dry kit, immediately before starting, bladder emptied.

  2. Train as normal

    Track exactly how much fluid you drink (weigh the bottle before and after, or use marked volumes) and note duration.

  3. Weigh after

    Same conditions as before — towel off sweat first, don't let it evaporate before the second weigh-in.

  4. Do the sum

    Sweat loss (L) = weight lost (kg) + fluid drunk (L) − urine passed (L). Divide by hours for a rate.

  5. Repeat across conditions

    Once in cool, easy conditions and once in your hottest, hardest representative session — a single measurement describes a single day, not a season.

Two everyday markers extend this between formal tests. Morning body mass, taken under consistent conditions (same time, after emptying your bladder, before eating or drinking), is a reasonable week-to-week hydration and recovery monitor — a single day's reading is noisy, contaminated by glycogen and gut contents, but a sustained unexplained downward drift of more than about 1–2% over several days is worth investigating, alongside the broader fatigue monitoring covered in chapter 35. Urine colour, checked first thing and occasionally through a heavy training day, is a cheap and fast proxy for hydration status: pale straw suggests adequate fluid, dark amber suggests a deficit — though it is confounded by B-vitamin supplements (which turn urine bright yellow regardless of hydration) and by the first urine of the morning, which is naturally more concentrated.

Worked example
Freya, 29, 61 kg triathlete, wants a fluid plan that actually matches her two most different training environments: an easy indoor trainer session in a cool room, and a hot, humid outdoor long ride.

  2. Session 2 — hot, humid 3 h outdoor ride, race-pace effort. Weighs 61.2 kg before, 59.6 kg after, drank 1800 mL.Loss = (61.2−59.6) + 1.8 = 3.4 L over 3 h → 1.13 L/h

32.6Sodium, Potassium, Magnesium — And The Cramp Story

Sodium is the electrolyte that actually matters during exercise, for the reasons already covered: it is lost in meaningful, individually variable amounts, and both too little attention to it (in a salty, heavy sweater over a long hot event) and too much dilution of it (overdrinking low-sodium fluid) cause real problems. Potassium and magnesium are lost in sweat too, but in much smaller amounts relative to the body's total stores, and ordinary food — fruit, vegetables, dairy, wholegrains, nuts — comfortably replaces what training removes for the overwhelming majority of athletes B; this overlaps with the broader micronutrient picture in chapter 31. Specific potassium or magnesium supplementation during exercise has not been shown to improve performance or reliably prevent cramp in athletes who are not frankly deficient.

Which brings up the claim this section exists to grade honestly. Exercise-associated muscle cramping (EAMC) has been blamed on electrolyte depletion and dehydration for decades, and the marketing built on that story is enormous. The evidence does not support it as the primary mechanism for most cramping athletes C leaning toward D for the electrolyte-depletion theory specifically. Cramping athletes are often no more dehydrated or depleted than non-cramping finishers of the same event, cramps cluster in specific, previously injured or fatigued muscles rather than symmetrically across the body, and a cramp can sometimes be stopped within seconds by contracting the opposing muscle group — far faster than any ingested electrolyte could plausibly act.

The leading alternative is altered neuromuscular control: sustained or unaccustomed muscular work alters the balance of excitatory and inhibitory signals reaching the spinal reflex arc, making a muscle fire involuntarily. This fits the pattern far better than depletion does, and tracks fatigue and unaccustomed load more reliably than it tracks sweat loss. A small subset of extreme, very salty, heavy sweaters in prolonged heat events may have a genuine electrolyte contribution C — but for the ordinary athlete cramping in a club 10K, the fix most likely to work is training the muscle to tolerate the load, not drinking more electrolyte mix.

Myth vs fact
“My calves cramped at the end of the race — I must be low on electrolytes”

Cramp means salt or fluid deficiency, so the fix is a sports drink or an electrolyte tablet.

Most cramp is neuromuscular fatigue, not depletion

Cramping athletes typically show no greater fluid or electrolyte deficit than non-cramping finishers of the same event. The stronger pattern is localised, fatigue-driven, altered reflex activity in a specific overworked muscle. Pickle juice and other sharp-tasting remedies may work partly through a reflex triggered by the mouth and throat, not through delivering electrolytes fast enough to matter — the relief, when it happens, is often too quick to be chemical.

32.7Hyperhydration And Sodium Loading — Narrow Tools, Narrow Uses

Hyperhydration — drinking beyond normal fluid balance before exercise, usually with glycerol to help retain the extra water, or with extra sodium to retain fluid without simply diluting it — can modestly expand plasma volume going into an event C. The appeal is narrow: events where fluid will not be available for a long stretch after the start, in significant heat — military deployment, some open-water swims, a long trail stage with no aid access. For most training and racing, where fluid is accessible throughout, the benefit over simply starting hydrated and drinking to plan is marginal, gastrointestinal discomfort from the extra load is common, and getting the sodium dosing wrong recreates the same dilution risk this chapter has spent most of its length warning about.

Sodium loading alone, without the glycerol, in the day or two before a long hot event, is a gentler version of the same idea — eating saltier than normal to pre-emptively top up what will be lost. It carries less risk and is a reasonable, low-cost tactic for long, hot races in athletes who already know, from testing, that they are moderate-to-heavy sodium sweaters; it is not useful for short or cool events, where normal eating already covers it.

Activate it

Building and rehearsing a hydration plan rather than improvising one on the day.

  • Test Measure your sweat rate once in cool, easy conditions and once in your hottest, hardest representative session, using the before/after weigh-in method above.
  • Dose For sessions under 60–90 min in mild conditions, drink to thirst. For longer, hotter or race-important efforts, drink to a programmed target set 10–20% below your measured sweat rate — close enough to protect performance, short of the overdrinking that causes hyponatraemia.
  • Eat If you sweat heavily or saltily, add salted food (pretzels, salted rice, electrolyte drink mix) rather than relying on plain water to meet the whole fluid target.
  • Timing Rehearse your race-day fluid and sodium plan in training at race intensity and duration — exactly the same logic as the gut-training principle in chapter 27.
  • On the bike An indoor trainer is the single best place to run a sweat-rate test: controlled temperature, no wind to mask sweat loss, and an easy weigh-in before and after a fixed-duration session at a fixed power.
  • Signal You're doing it right when body mass after a session is within about 1–2% of where you started, heart rate drift across a steady effort is modest for the conditions, and you finish without the bloating or puffiness that signals overdrinking.
B The 2% threshold and the overdrinking-hyponatraemia link are well supported; the exact fluid percentage to target below measured sweat rate is a reasonable synthesis, not a single tested number.

Five things to carry forward

  1. Sweat rate (0.5–2.5+ L/h) and sweat sodium concentration (200–2000 mg/L) are independently variable, several-fold-wide ranges — a single fluid plan or electrolyte dose cannot be correct for every athlete or every condition.
  2. Body-mass losses under about 2% have little measurable performance effect in most conditions; losses beyond 2–3% impair both performance and thermoregulation, and the cost is amplified sharply in heat.
  3. Exercise-associated hyponatraemia is rarer than dehydration but far more dangerous, is caused mainly by drinking beyond thirst relative to sweat losses over several hours, and weight gain during an event is the clearest field red flag that it is happening.
  4. Most exercise-associated muscle cramping tracks localised neuromuscular fatigue far better than it tracks electrolyte depletion or dehydration, which is why electrolyte supplements reliably fail to prevent it in most athletes.
  5. Measuring your own sweat rate in both cool and hot/hard conditions, then building a programmed plan only for the longer or hotter sessions where drinking to thirst is riskier, beats both blanket advice and pure improvisation.
