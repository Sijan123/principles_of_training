From Plate to Mitochondrion

Digestion, absorption, transport — and training the gut

A gel takes about ten seconds to swallow and roughly ninety minutes to become the glucose that lights up a mitochondrion in your calf. In between, that spoonful of maltodextrin crosses a stomach that may or may not let it through quickly, a gut wall with a weighing-room strict about how much sugar it admits per hour, a portal vein that routes it straight through the liver before it ever reaches the heart, and a bloodstream that has to out-compete your skin and your muscles for the blood supply doing the carrying. Fuelling is not "eating enough." It is logistics, with real bottlenecks, real ceilings, and real rules for widening them.

27.1The Journey — Mouth To Mitochondrion

Follow one carbohydrate gel from the moment it is swallowed. It spends seconds in the mouth, where salivary amylase barely begins breaking starch apart before you swallow. It then sits in the stomach, which is not a digestive organ so much as a metering valve: it has essentially no capacity to absorb carbohydrate itself and simply controls how fast the gel's contents trickle into the small intestine, a process called gastric emptying. For a dilute, low-energy drink at rest, that trickle empties at roughly 1–1.5% of stomach volume per minute; a more concentrated, energy-dense gel empties more slowly, typically over 20–40 minutes depending on what else is in the stomach.

Once in the small intestine — about six to seven metres of tubing folded into your abdomen, its inner surface thrown into millions of finger-like villi that multiply its absorptive area roughly thirtyfold — carbohydrate is broken into single sugars (glucose, fructose, galactose) and carried across the gut wall by specific transporter proteins embedded in the villi. This absorption step is fast once the sugar reaches the wall: single sugars can cross within minutes. From there, essentially all the blood draining the gut collects in one vessel, the hepatic portal vein, and is delivered directly to the liver before it reaches general circulation — a one-way customs checkpoint that lets the liver top up its own glycogen, buffer a surge of fructose (which the liver, not muscle, is primarily built to handle), and smooth out blood glucose before the rest of the body sees it. Transit from gut wall to liver takes roughly a minute; liver to working muscle, carried by a heart now pushing several times its resting output, another few seconds.

Add it up and a sensibly formulated carbohydrate drink, taken on an already-moving gut, can have measurable amounts of its sugar being oxidised in exercising muscle within about 10–15 minutes, rising for the next 60–90 minutes as absorption catches up with intake. That lag is the entire reason race-fuelling plans start early and little, not late and large: a gel swallowed at the 35 km marker of a marathon mostly serves the last six kilometres, not the one you are running when you feel the dip.

In plain terms

Think of fuelling like posting a parcel through a chain of sorting offices, each with its own maximum throughput.

The stomach is the first office: it only forwards parcels at a fixed rate, faster for small light ones, slower for big dense ones. The small intestine is the second office, and it is the strict one — it has exactly two conveyor belts for sugar, one for glucose and one for fructose, and each belt has a top speed. The liver is customs: everything gets searched there before it is allowed into general circulation. None of this is optional and none of it responds to how much you want the parcel to arrive faster. The only way to speed the whole chain up is to use both conveyor belts at once, and to have trained the offices to handle a bigger volume — which is exactly what the rest of this chapter is about.

27.2The Carbohydrate Ceiling — Why One Sugar Is Never Enough

The single most important number in sports fuelling is a transporter protein's maximum speed. Glucose crosses the gut wall mainly via SGLT1 (sodium-glucose cotransporter 1), a protein that drags one glucose molecule in alongside a sodium ion. SGLT1 behaves like any pump: there is a maximum rate at which it can turn over, and that ceiling is reached at a glucose intake of roughly 60 g per hour A. Drink more glucose, maltodextrin, or sucrose (which splits into glucose plus fructose, so only half saturates this route) beyond that point, and the extra sugar simply isn't absorbed fast enough to help — it sits in the gut, ferments, and contributes to bloating and cramping rather than fuel.

Fructose, however, does not queue for the same pump. It crosses mainly via a separate, sodium-independent transporter called GLUT5, which has its own, lower, independent ceiling, generally cited around 20–30 g/h for fructose taken alone. The practical consequence is one of the cleaner wins in exercise nutrition: because glucose and fructose use different doors, ingesting them together does not simply split one ceiling in two — it adds a second ceiling on top of the first. This is the entire basis for the modern recommendation to use multiple transportable carbohydrates, usually a 2:1 or roughly 1:0.8 glucose(or maltodextrin):fructose blend, to reach total exogenous carbohydrate oxidation rates of 90–120 g/h rather than being capped at 60 g/h A.

The win is purely about getting more total sugar across the gut wall per hour. This is also why plain fructose alone is a poor single fuel: it is capped lower than glucose and, taken in large single doses, is a reliable way to cause gastrointestinal distress.

27.3Protein And Fat — Slower Doors, Different Rules

Protein and fat do not face a sodium-cotransporter ceiling the way carbohydrate does, but they are absorbed far more slowly, and that slowness is sometimes the entire point. Dietary protein is first unfolded by stomach acid and pepsin, then cut into peptides and amino acids by pancreatic enzymes in the small intestine, then absorbed by a battery of specific amino-acid transporters. The practical difference between protein sources is less about the ceiling and more about the shape of delivery over time.

Whey protein is soluble, empties from the stomach quickly, and delivers amino acids into the blood fast — typically appearing within 30–45 minutes and peaking around 60–90 minutes, at an effective digestion-and-absorption rate often quoted around 8–10 g/h B. Casein, by contrast, curdles into a dense clot in the acidic stomach on contact — the same reaction that curdles milk with lemon juice — and that clot is digested slowly from the outside in, trickling amino acids into the blood for 6–8 hours. Neither is "better"; a fast rise suits the period right after training when muscle is primed to use amino acids quickly, and a slow trickle suits an overnight fast, which is why casein before bed is one of the few timing recommendations in this book with decent mechanistic support (more in chapter 29).

Fat is the slow one by a wide margin. It is not water-soluble, so it must be emulsified by bile before pancreatic lipase can act on it, then repackaged inside the gut wall into particles called chylomicrons that enter the lymphatic system rather than the portal vein — a detour that adds hours, not minutes, to its appearance in blood. The practical consequence for anyone training is immediate: a meal with a meaningful amount of fat in the stomach slows everything behind it, including the carbohydrate you may be relying on. This is also the mechanism, not a coincidence, behind "why I felt sluggish on the start line" after a pre-race breakfast with too much butter or cheese in it.

27.4What Actually Controls Gastric Emptying

Four variables govern how fast your stomach lets food through, and all four are things you control.

  1. Volume

    A fuller stomach empties faster in absolute terms — stretch itself is a signal to empty — which is the physiological case for taking a reasonably sized bolus rather than constant tiny sips, within comfort.

  2. Energy density

    Calorie-for-calorie, dilute drinks empty faster than concentrated gels. A 6–8% carbohydrate solution (6–8 g per 100 mL) is close to the sweet spot used in most sports drinks; much higher concentrations slow emptying and, combined with high osmolality, can draw water into the gut rather than out of it.

  3. Osmolality

    This is the concentration of dissolved particles in a fluid relative to blood. A drink markedly more concentrated than blood plasma (hypertonic) delays gastric emptying and can pull fluid into the gut lumen, which is part of why some concentrated gels taken without water cause a sloshing, nauseous feeling.

  4. Exercise intensity

    Below roughly 70% of VO2max, gastric emptying is barely affected by exercise at all. Above that, and sharply above ~80%, blood is progressively redirected away from the gut (splanchnic circulation) toward working muscle and skin, and gastric emptying together with intestinal absorption both slow down. This is the physiological reason nobody can stomach a large meal during a hard interval session or a race-pace 10K, and why fuelling strategy has to change with intensity, not just duration.

Key idea

Gastric emptying and intestinal absorption are two separate bottlenecks in series, and high-intensity exercise narrows the first one deliberately — it is not a malfunction, it is your circulatory system correctly prioritising the muscles doing the work over the gut digesting breakfast.

27.5Gastrointestinal Distress — Causes And Fixes

Surveys of endurance events consistently find 30–50% of participants report some gut symptom, from mild bloating to the kind of cramping that ends a race B. The causes cluster into a short, fixable list.

27.6Gut Training — Absorption Is A Trainable Capacity

Here is the fact that changes how fuelling should be planned: SGLT1 density in the gut wall is not fixed. Feed the gut carbohydrate repeatedly over weeks, and transporter density and gastric comfort both improve — exogenous carbohydrate oxidation rises and gut symptoms at a given intake fall, in both lab feeding studies and field reports from endurance athletes who deliberately practise high intake B. The mechanism looks like ordinary training specificity applied to an organ most athletes never think to train: the gut adapts to the load you give it, the same way a muscle does, just on its own clock.

The practical programme is simple and should run for 4–8 weeks ahead of any event where you plan to fuel above roughly 60 g/h: in a subset of your long training sessions, progressively increase carbohydrate intake, using exactly the products and the multiple-transportable-carbohydrate ratio you intend to race with, and track symptoms honestly rather than pushing through nausea.

Worked example
Owen, 32, training for a 100 km mountain ultramarathon. Comfortable at 60 g/h for years; when he tried to push to 90 g/h for a faster finish, he got nauseous and walked for 20 minutes mid-session. Six weeks to his key long run before taper.
  1. Identify the ceiling that failed: single-source maltodextrin drink, no fructose, taken cold at race intensity.90 g/h glucose-only > ~60 g/h SGLT1 ceiling → unabsorbed sugar ferments
  2. Switch product to a 2:1 maltodextrin:fructose blend and rebuild the dose from below his old comfortable rate, in his long runs, every week.Week 1: 65 g/h (43 g maltodextrin + 22 g fructose) over a 2 h run
  3. Add 10 g/h per week, holding pace at long-run effort (65–75% VO2max), and log symptoms on a 0–4 scale.Week 2: 75 g/h (50+25 g) over 2.5 h — symptom score 1/4
  4. Cross his old failure point deliberately, with the blend doing the work the single sugar couldn't.Week 3: 85 g/h (57+28 g) over 2.5 h — 0/4. Week 4: 95 g/h (63+32 g) over 3 h — 1/4, the first time past 90 g/h without distress
  5. Rehearse the exact race products in the exact race format at race-representative effort.Week 5: 105 g/h (70+35 g) over 3 h on race-day gels and drink — 0/4. Week 6: 100 g/h over 3.5 h at race pace, final dress rehearsal
So: Owen races at 100 g/h — up from a hard ceiling of 60 g/h six weeks earlier — by crossing his failure point gradually, in training, on the exact products, rather than discovering the ceiling on race day.
Activate it

The gut-training block to run before any event where you plan to exceed ~60 g/h.

  • Eat A 2:1 glucose(maltodextrin):fructose blend, matched to your actual race products wherever possible.
  • Dose Start at your current comfortable rate; add ~10 g/h per week.
  • Timing One long aerobic session per week, carbohydrate from the first 15 minutes, at the intensity you expect to race or ride at.
  • On the bike An indoor trainer session works just as well for this — 2–3 h at 60–70% FTP is ideal because pace, and therefore gut blood flow, stays controllable while you escalate the dose.
  • Signal You're progressing correctly when the symptom score at a given g/h falls week to week, not just when you can tolerate more — comfort, not willpower, is the metric.
B Consistent direction across feeding studies and field practice; optimal weekly increment and total block length are not tightly established.

27.7The Microbiome — What's Plausible, What's Hype

Your gut hosts on the order of 1013–1014 bacteria, and it is tempting to assume that optimising this community is the next frontier in fuelling. The honest picture is more modest. A widely cited 2019 study found marathon runners' guts were enriched, post-race, in Veillonella atypica — a species that happens to metabolise lactate — and that dosing mice with it improved treadmill run time, a genuinely interesting mechanistic lead C. Small trials of specific probiotic strains show modest reductions in GI symptom severity and, in a few cases, respiratory infection rates during heavy training blocks C. Both are plausible and worth watching.

What is not supported is the leap from these findings to commercial claims of personalised performance-boosting probiotics, "gut-healing" powders, or microbiome tests that promise a bespoke supplement stack. The between-person variability in gut microbial composition is enormous, the strains and doses used in positive trials rarely match what is sold over the counter, and no study has shown that manipulating the microbiome outperforms the two interventions already proven to work — training the gut's absorptive capacity, and simply eating enough fibre and variety to keep the resident community diverse D for the commercial extrapolation specifically. Fermented foods and a fibre-diverse diet are a reasonable general-health habit; they are not, on current evidence, a fuelling strategy with a dose you can prescribe.

Where this goes wrong

The most common fuelling mistake is not choosing the wrong product — it is using a product on race day that was never used in training. The gut's ceiling, its comfort with a given osmolality, and its tolerance for a given volume while running at pace are all specific adaptations. A gel that feels fine eaten at your desk can be genuinely different at 85% of max heart rate three hours into a race. Rehearse everything, including the brand, the flavour, and the exact timing you intend to use.

The paper
Podlogar T, Cirnski S, Bokal Ž, et al. Increased exogenous but unaltered endogenous carbohydrate oxidation with combined fructose-maltodextrin ingested at 120 g/h versus 90 g/h at different ratios. Eur J Appl Physiol 2022;122(11):2393–2401.
Who

Highly trained competitive cyclists, used to moderate-to-high carbohydrate intakes during exercise.

What

Three hours of cycling comparing 120 g/h (fructose:maltodextrin roughly 0.8:1) against 90 g/h (roughly 1:2), measuring exogenous and endogenous (the body's own stored glycogen) carbohydrate oxidation.

Found

The 120 g/h trial produced higher exogenous oxidation (~1.51 vs ~1.29 g/min) but did not spare any more of the athletes' own glycogen — endogenous oxidation was essentially identical between trials.

Why it matters: it shows that pushing intake from 90 to 120 g/h genuinely raises how much external fuel you can burn, extending the ceiling first mapped by Jeukendrup's multiple-transportable-carbohydrate work in the 2000s — but it did not prove that doing so saves stored glycogen, which is a separate question performance studies have to answer on their own. Limitation: highly trained cyclists already accustomed to large carbohydrate intakes; results in untrained or gut-untrained athletes would likely be worse on both counts.

Five things to carry forward

  1. Fuel is only useful once it clears four bottlenecks in series — gastric emptying, intestinal transporters, the liver's portal filter, and the bloodstream — and measurable oxidation starts roughly 10–15 minutes after ingestion, which is why fuelling plans must run early and often, not late and large.
  2. Glucose absorption caps near 60 g/h through the SGLT1 transporter; fructose uses a separate transporter (GLUT5), which is why glucose+fructose blends, not more of one sugar, unlock 90–120 g/h.
  3. Protein and fat are absorbed far more slowly than carbohydrate — whey delivers amino acids over roughly an hour, casein over six to eight, and fat delays gastric emptying by hours, which is why a high-fat pre-race meal can sabotage same-day carbohydrate intake.
  4. High exercise intensity actively shuts the gut's blood supply down, which is a feature of circulatory prioritisation, not a malfunction — fuelling strategy has to change with intensity, not just duration.
  5. Absorption capacity itself is trainable: raising carbohydrate intake progressively in long sessions over 4–8 weeks, using the exact race products, reliably pushes an athlete's practical ceiling well past where it started.
