Muscle Architecture

Whole muscle to myofibril — and why shape dictates function

Lay a soleus and a sartorius side by side and you would struggle to believe they are the same kind of tissue. One is a short, dense, almost woody slab buried under the calf; the other is a long, thin ribbon that spirals across the entire thigh. Both are skeletal muscle, built from the same contractile protein, activated by the same kind of nerve signal. The difference between them — and the difference between a sprinter's hamstring and a marathoner's — is almost entirely a matter of architecture: how the fibres inside are arranged, how long they run, and at what angle they pull. Shape is not decoration. Shape is a trade-off, deliberately made, between force and speed.

3.1The Nested Tube

Pick up a whole muscle and the first thing you would find, wrapping the outside, is a tough sheath called the epimysium. Slice into the muscle and you find it is not one solid block but a bundle of smaller bundles — fascicles, each wrapped in its own sheath, the perimysium. Slice a fascicle open and inside are the individual muscle cells, called fibres, each wrapped in a thinner layer still, the endomysium. At the end of the muscle, all three layers do not simply stop; they braid together and continue on as the tendon, which in turn blends into the fibrous covering of the bone. From the outside of the whole muscle to the inside of the bone it anchors to, it is one continuous sheet of collagen, never interrupted, like a single garment tailored with three nested layers rather than three separate pieces of cloth.

This continuity matters for a reason that is easy to miss if you think of a muscle as a rope with both ends tied down. A rope only pulls along its own length. A muscle, because its connective-tissue wrapping is continuous sideways as well as along its length, also transmits force laterally — sideways, into the perimysium around neighbouring fascicles, and from there into adjacent muscles and the sheet of fascia surrounding the whole limb. Cut a single fibre's tendon in an animal experiment and the fibre still produces force at the joint, because enough of that force escapes sideways through the endomysium and perimysium network to reach the skeleton anyway. The practical upshot is one you already met in chapter 1: no exercise isolates a muscle as cleanly as its name implies, because the tissue carrying the load was never built as an isolated cable in the first place.

In plain terms

Think of a bunch of drinking straws, wrapped individually in tissue paper, bundled into groups wrapped in a second layer of tissue paper, and the whole bundle wrapped a third time in cling film that continues straight on into a leather strap.

That is a muscle: fibre, wrapped in endomysium; fascicle, wrapped in perimysium; whole muscle, wrapped in epimysium; all three layers running on, uninterrupted, into the tendon. Squeeze one straw and some of that squeeze is felt by its neighbours, because the wrapping paper is shared. That is lateral force transmission, and it is why pulling on a tendon is really pulling on a whole woven structure, not a single strand.

3.2Parallel or Pennate: The One Design Decision

Inside that nested tube, fascicles can be arranged in only a few basic patterns, and almost all of the functional variety between muscles comes down to where each one sits between two extremes.

In a parallel (or fusiform) muscle, fascicles run roughly along the muscle's whole length, parallel to its line of pull, like the sartorius or the biceps brachii. In a pennate muscle — from the Latin penna, feather — fascicles run at an angle to the line of pull, inserting into a central tendon the way the barbs of a feather insert into its shaft. The angle between the fascicle and the tendon's line of pull is the pennation angle. A muscle can be unipennate (feather barbs on one side only, like much of the tibialis posterior), bipennate (barbs on both sides of a central tendon, like the rectus femoris), or multipennate (several tendon sheets converging from different directions, like the deltoid, which lets it pull a limb in three different directions from one muscle belly).

Why would angling the fibres matter at all? Because the amount of force a muscle can produce is set by how many fibres pull in parallel across a slice cut through the muscle at a right angle to the tendon — not by how much tissue the muscle contains in total. A pennate arrangement lets a muscle fit more, shorter fibres into the same overall volume by stacking them at an angle into a shared tendon, the way more feather barbs fit onto a given length of shaft than would fit if they all had to lie end to end along it. The cost is paid in two currencies at once. First, each fibre contributes only the component of its force that lines up with the tendon's pull — a fibre pulling at a pennation angle θ contributes force × cos(θ) of useful pull, so very large pennation angles start giving back some of what they gained. Second, and more importantly, shorter fibres can only shorten by a shorter absolute distance, and they shorten no faster per sarcomere than a long fibre does — so a muscle built from short, pennate fibres has less reach (excursion) and a lower top speed of shortening than the same volume of tissue arranged in long, parallel fibres.

Key idea

Pennation is not a design flaw and it is not "better" or "worse" than a parallel arrangement — it is a specific trade of range and speed for force, bought by packing more fibres into less length. A muscle's job tells you which side of that trade it should be on, and evolution has placed almost every muscle in your body close to wherever its particular job needs it to sit.

3.3What Actually Predicts Force: PCSA, Not Length or Volume

It is tempting to guess that a bigger muscle — more total volume, a longer belly — produces more force. It doesn't, not directly. The number that predicts a muscle's maximum force is its physiological cross-sectional area (PCSA): the total cross-sectional area of all its fibres, measured on a plane that cuts across the fibres rather than across the whole muscle belly. For a pennate muscle, that plane is tilted relative to the muscle's long axis, which is precisely why anatomical cross-section (a simple slice straight across the belly) systematically underestimates how much force-producing tissue is actually present.

PCSA is calculated as muscle volume divided by fibre length, then corrected for pennation angle: PCSA = (volume ÷ fibre length) × cos(θ). Two muscles can have identical volume — the same total amount of contractile tissue — and wildly different PCSA, simply because one has packed that volume into long fibres and the other into short, angled ones. Multiply PCSA by a muscle's specific tension — the force each square centimetre of fibre can produce, estimated at roughly 20–40 N/cm² depending on measurement method and whether it is corrected for pennation C — and you get an estimate of a muscle's maximum isometric force, independent of how long the muscle happens to be.

Worked example
Two muscles, A and B, each built from exactly 200 cm³ of contractile tissue — identical volume, arranged differently.
  1. Muscle A is parallel-fibred: fascicle length equals the whole muscle's working length.Fibre length = 20 cm, pennation = 0°
  2. Its PCSA is volume divided by fibre length, with no pennation correction needed.PCSAA = 200 ÷ 20 = 10 cm²
  3. Muscle B is pennate: the same 200 cm³ is packed into fibres a quarter as long, angled into a central tendon.Fibre length = 5 cm, pennation = 20°
  4. Its PCSA is volume divided by the shorter fibre length, corrected for the angle.PCSAB = (200 ÷ 5) × cos(20°) = 40 × 0.94 ≈ 37.6 cm²
  5. Using the same specific tension for both (same tissue, just arranged differently), compare maximum force.Force ratio ≈ 37.6 ÷ 10 ≈ 3.8×
  6. Now compare excursion — how far each can shorten. Excursion scales with fibre length, not muscle volume.Excursion ratio ≈ 5 ÷ 20 = 0.25×
So: for identical muscle volume, Muscle B produces roughly 3.8 times the force of Muscle A, but can only shorten through about a quarter of the distance, and does so at a correspondingly lower top speed. Neither muscle "has more muscle." They have the same amount, arranged for different jobs.

3.4Motor and Spring, Wired in Series

A muscle never acts alone; it acts as a muscle–tendon unit, and the tendon is not a passive attachment cable — it is a spring, wired in mechanical series with the muscle's contractile machinery. Tendon is made predominantly of collagen, and like any spring it stretches under load and gives that energy back on release, with only a small fraction lost as heat. How stiff or compliant that spring is changes what the muscle fibres attached to it actually have to do.

Here is the consequence that matters most for anyone who runs: a sufficiently compliant tendon can stretch and recoil while the muscle fibres in series with it barely change length at all — the muscle works near-isometrically (producing force without shortening much), while the tendon does almost all of the lengthening and shortening. This matters because a muscle fibre contracting isometrically uses far less metabolic energy than one actively shortening and lengthening through a full range, for the same force. During running, the Achilles tendon stretches on landing and recoils on push-off like a loaded catapult, storing and returning a substantial share of the mechanical energy of each stride while the calf muscles themselves do comparatively little active shortening. This tendon-spring behaviour is one of the central mechanical reasons two runners with identical VO2max can have measurably different running economy — the oxygen cost of covering the same ground at the same pace — a subject picked up properly in chapter 14.

In plain terms

Think of a pogo stick strapped to a small motor. The motor's only job is to keep enough tension in the spring; the spring does the actual bouncing.

A stiff pogo stick barely compresses and gives a harsh, energetic-feeling bounce back quickly. A soft one compresses a long way and returns energy slowly. Your Achilles tendon is closer to the soft end of that spectrum than most people guess, and the calf muscle sitting above it is closer to the small motor than to the bouncing part. Train the motor and you get stronger. Train the spring's stiffness and you can change how efficiently the whole system bounces — which is a different adaptation, covered in chapter 19.

3.5Architecture Changes With Training — Two Different Knobs

Architecture is not fixed at birth. Training turns two separate knobs, and they do not move together.

Fascicle length increases most reliably in response to training that loads a muscle through a long range under high tension, especially eccentrically — while the muscle is lengthening under load. Sprinters show longer fascicles in the hamstrings and calf than endurance runners of similar size, and the gap is large enough to measure on ordinary ultrasound: in one comparison of elite sprinters, distance runners and untrained controls, the sprinters' vastus lateralis and gastrocnemius fascicles were measurably longer, relative to limb length, than either of the other groups, with the distance runners and controls similar to each other. Training studies confirm the direction runs both ways: several weeks of the Nordic hamstring exercise — a bodyweight eccentric exercise that loads the hamstrings through a long, slow lengthening range — have repeatedly produced roughly a 20–24% increase in biceps femoris fascicle length within six to nine weeks B, an adaptation that is thought to add contractile units (sarcomeres) in series along the fibre, and one that reverses within about two weeks of stopping the exercise even though the strength gained is retained for longer.

Pennation angle, by contrast, increases most reliably with traditional heavy resistance training performed through a shorter, concentric-biased range, and tracks fairly closely with gains in whole-muscle cross-sectional area. Classic hypertrophy training — think heavy back squats, leg press, hack squats — raises pennation angle alongside PCSA, which is part of why resistance-trained limbs can gain measurable strength from muscle growth that looks, on ultrasound, architecturally different from the muscle growth driven by a programme built around long-range eccentric work.

Hypertrophy also distributes unevenly along a single muscle — regional hypertrophy — because different exercises load different fascicle regions of the same muscle to different degrees depending on joint angle and moment arm, a topic you have already met for force in chapter 2. The quadriceps, hamstrings and deltoid are the clearest examples: a leg extension and a squat both grow the quadriceps, but ultrasound studies consistently find they do not grow it evenly along its length, and programming that varies exercise selection across a training block is partly an attempt to manage this unevenness rather than leave any one region undertrained.

Activate it

Targeting fascicle length — the architectural change most linked to high-speed running capacity and to a lower risk of hamstring strain.

  • Do Nordic hamstring curl, kneeling, controlled lowering, assisted push back up.
  • Load Bodyweight; emphasise a slow, resisted lengthening phase of 3–4 seconds.
  • Dose 2–3 sets × 4–8 reps, twice weekly.
  • When Year-round at low dose for maintenance; higher dose in the 6–9 weeks before a season with sprinting demands.
  • Progress Slow the lowering phase further, or reduce assistance on the way back up, before adding external load.

3.6Reading a Muscle's Job From Its Shape

Once you know the trade-off, a muscle's architecture stops being trivia and starts being a label that tells you what the muscle is for.

The soleus, buried under the gastrocnemius in the calf, is short, densely pennate and built from fibres only a few centimetres long packed at a steep angle — commonly cited in the region of 25–35° C, with substantial variation between measurement methods and joint angle. That architecture gives it a very large PCSA for its size and correspondingly large force output, with modest excursion. It is also, not coincidentally, overwhelmingly slow-twitch (see chapter 5) and active almost continuously during standing and walking: a muscle built to produce large, sustained, low-velocity force. The gastrocnemius sitting over it has a shallower pennation angle, longer fascicles and crosses two joints rather than one, trading some of that raw force for more excursion and speed — useful for the sudden push-off of a sprint start or a jump, where the soleus alone would be too slow.

The deltoid is multipennate, with anterior, lateral and posterior fibre groups converging on a shared tendon from three different directions, which is exactly why one muscle belly can flex, abduct and extend the shoulder depending on which portion is driving. The sartorius, by contrast, is the longest muscle in the body, a narrow parallel-fibred strap running from the outside of the hip to the inside of the knee, built almost entirely for excursion and range rather than force — it assists hip flexion, abduction and lateral rotation, and knee flexion, but could never produce the force per unit volume that the soleus does, because it was never built to.

The paper
Wickiewicz TL, Roy RR, Powell PL, Edgerton VR. Muscle architecture of the human lower limb. Clin Orthop Relat Res 1983;(179):275–83.
Who

Three unembalmed human cadavers, dissected in full.

What

Direct measurement of fascicle length, pennation angle, muscle mass and physiological cross-sectional area for every major muscle of the hip, knee and ankle.

Found

Fascicle length was remarkably consistent within a given muscle and within synergistic groups, while PCSA varied enormously between muscles of similar mass — the first systematic demonstration that architecture, not size alone, sets a muscle's force-generating capacity.

Why it matters: this dataset underlies most of the muscle-architecture values used in biomechanics and musculoskeletal modelling to this day. Limitation: three cadavers, typically elderly at time of death, measured at a single joint position — later ultrasound studies in living, younger people have refined some values, and architecture measured at one joint angle does not describe the whole range of motion.

Five things to carry forward

  1. A muscle is a continuous sheet of collagen from epimysium to tendon to bone, which is why force travels sideways as well as along the fibre and no exercise isolates a muscle as cleanly as its name suggests.
  2. Pennation packs more, shorter fibres into the same volume of tissue, trading excursion and shortening speed for force — it is a deliberate trade, not an inferior design.
  3. Physiological cross-sectional area, not muscle length or total volume, predicts maximum force; two muscles of identical volume can differ several-fold in force depending purely on how their fibres are arranged.
  4. A compliant tendon lets the muscle fibres in series with it work near-isometrically while the tendon does the lengthening and shortening, storing and returning energy like a spring — a major contributor to running economy.
  5. Training turns two separate architectural knobs: long-range eccentric loading lengthens fascicles over six to nine weeks, while heavy concentric-biased training raises pennation angle alongside ordinary hypertrophy.
