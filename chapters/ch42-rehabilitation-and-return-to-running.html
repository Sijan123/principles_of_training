Rehabilitation & Return to Running

Tendon, muscle and bone injuries — the loading progressions that work

The oldest piece of advice for an overuse injury is also the most reliably wrong: rest it. Rest removes pain and removes almost nothing else — the tendon that was too weak for the load returns from two weeks off exactly as weak as it left, and the load has not gone anywhere. The physiotherapy and sports-medicine literature of the last twenty years has converged on an unglamorous opposite: in nearly every overuse injury, controlled, progressive loading is the treatment, not the thing to avoid. This chapter sets out how that works for tendon, bone and muscle, where the one real exception sits, and exactly what a loading plan looks like week by week. It is written to educate, not to diagnose — the first section is about where this chapter's authority ends and a clinician's begins.

42.1Principles That Apply To Every Tissue

Four rules recur across every condition in this chapter, regardless of which tissue is involved.

  1. Load is the medicine

    Tendon, muscle and bone all adapt to the mechanical load placed on them (chapter 13). An injured tissue did not fail because it was loaded; in almost every case it failed because the load rose faster than the tissue's capacity to adapt to it. The fix is not the absence of load. It is the right dose, rebuilt from below the level that caused the problem.

  2. Rest is rarely the answer

    Complete rest reliably removes pain and reliably fails to rebuild capacity. The tissue that comes back from two weeks of rest is barely stronger than the tissue that went into it, while the training error that caused the injury — the mileage jump, the missing strength work — is still sitting there unaddressed. Relative rest — removing the specific provocative load while continuing to load the tissue in ways it can tolerate — is almost always the better default.

  3. Progress on next-morning symptoms, not during-session pain

    Some discomfort during a rehabilitation exercise is normal and does not mean damage. What matters is whether the tissue has settled back to its baseline by the next morning. This is the single most useful self-check in this entire chapter, and it is referred to throughout simply as the next-morning test.

  4. The pain-monitoring model

    Pain during a loading session may reach roughly 3 out of 10, and it must have returned to its pre-session baseline within 24 hours. If either condition is not met, the previous session was too much — not a sign that loading is the wrong approach, but a sign that the dose needs to come down a step B.

In plain terms

Think of an injured tendon like a rope that has frayed slightly from being used for a job slightly too heavy for it. Hanging the rope up and never using it again does not re-weave the fibres — it just stops you finding out how weak it still is. What actually rebuilds a rope's strength (if rope could rebuild itself, which tendon can) is steady, controlled use at a load it can just about manage, repeated often enough and long enough for new fibre to be laid down along the line of the pull.

Total rest is taking the rope out of service. Rehabilitation is putting it back to light duty, then medium duty, then its old job — and only increasing the load once the current one causes no trouble the next day.

Before you use any of this chapter

Everything that follows describes general loading principles for common, straightforward presentations. It is education, not a diagnosis, and it is not a substitute for a clinician who can examine you, rule out the conditions below, and confirm what you actually have. See a clinician promptly — and do not wait out the self-tests in this chapter first — if any of the following apply:

  • Focal, sharply localised bony tenderness, especially with night pain or pain that is present at rest or walking.
  • Pain that is no longer load-dependent — constant, present at night, or waking you from sleep.
  • A sudden, sharp pain with an audible pop, a palpable gap in a muscle or tendon, or a sudden inability to bear weight or push off — possible complete rupture.
  • Calf swelling, warmth and tenderness that is diffuse rather than localised to the tendon — rule out deep vein thrombosis before assuming it is Achilles pain.
  • Numbness, pins and needles, burning, or any measurable weakness.
  • Known low energy availability, disordered eating, menstrual disturbance, or a previous stress fracture, combined with any new bony pain — treat as a bone stress injury until proven otherwise.
  • Fever, unexplained weight loss, or any systemic symptom.
  • No meaningful improvement after 6–8 weeks of correctly executed loading.

None of this chapter should ever be read as a reason to avoid or delay seeing a clinician. Loading is powerful medicine, but it is only the right medicine once you know what you are treating.

42.2Tendinopathy

For decades, painful tendons were called tendinitis — "-itis" meaning inflammation. Biopsy studies overturned that: a painful chronic tendon shows little or no inflammatory cell infiltrate. What it shows instead is disorganised, thinned collagen fibres, an excess of ground substance between them, and new blood vessels and nerve endings growing in alongside — a failed healing response, not an inflamed one. The modern term, tendinopathy, deliberately avoids claiming a mechanism the tissue doesn't show.

A useful model (Cook & Purdam's tendon continuum) describes three overlapping states: a reactive tendon that has swollen protectively after a sudden load spike and can still recover quickly; tendon dysrepair, where the matrix has started to disorganise under sustained overload; and degenerative tendinopathy, longstanding disorganisation with areas of cell death, usually in older tendons with a long history. All three can hurt identically. What differs is how much load they tolerate right now — their irritability — and that, not the biopsy stage, is what should set the starting dose.

Tendon irritability and what it means for the starting load
IrritabilityWhat it looks likeWhere to start
HighPain at rest or overnight, pain within seconds of light load, slow to settleIsometrics only, reduce aggravating activity, relative rest from impact
ModeratePain only with loading, settles within the same day, no morning stiffness increaseIsometrics progressing quickly into heavy slow resistance
LowPain only with sport-specific or high-speed loading, settles well within 24 hoursHeavy slow resistance, moving on to energy-storage and plyometric loading

From whichever starting point, the progression runs the same four stages for every major tendon in a runner:

  1. Isometrics

    A static hold against resistance, no joint movement. 5 sets of 45 seconds at roughly 70% effort, 2 minutes rest between. Isometrics load the tendon without the stretch-shortening cycle that provokes it, and in several trials produce measurable, if short-lived, pain relief within minutes — useful both as treatment and as a same-day way to calm a flare before the next loading session.

  2. Heavy slow resistance (HSR)

    3–4 sets of 6–8 reps, 3 seconds up and 3 seconds down, three times a week. This is the best-supported stage for driving genuine tendon adaptation — increased collagen synthesis and tendon stiffness — and it should occupy most of the programme's middle weeks.

  3. Energy storage and plyometric loading

    Once heavy slow work is pain-free, reintroduce the elastic, rapid loading that running itself demands — pogo hops progressing to bounding (see chapter 41). Skipping this stage is the commonest reason rehabilitation "works in the gym" and then fails the moment running resumes.

  4. Return to sport

    A graded return to full training load (§42.6), with the heavy slow resistance work continued at a lower maintenance dose indefinitely — stopping it is a common cause of recurrence.

Three tendons, specifically

  • Achilles (midportion) — heel raises through full range, both knee angles (gastrocnemius straight, soleus bent); avoid aggravating with excessive uphill or speed work until HSR is pain-free.
  • Achilles (insertional) — the same progression, but keep the heel-raise range short of full dorsiflexion at the bottom, since over-stretching at the insertion compresses the tendon against the heel bone and can provoke rather than help it.
  • Patellar ("jumper's knee") — the same isometric-to-HSR sequence using a split squat or leg-press pattern; decline-board loading is a well-established variant once irritability is low.
  • Gluteal — the same structure, with one extra rule: avoid compressive positions during the irritable phase — crossing the legs, lying directly on the affected hip, deep hip-flexed stretching — which load the tendon against the greater trochanter in exactly the way a tight band compresses the structure in chapter 43. A large trial found education plus this kind of progressive loading outperformed a corticosteroid injection at one year, even though the injection looked better in the first few weeks.

Its five drills are staged exactly as above: isometric calf hold when the tendon is irritable, heavy slow resistance once it has settled, then single-leg calf raise and pogo hops to rebuild capacity and elasticity, with the calf stretch kept for maintenance afterwards. The routine note is explicit that progress is judged on next-morning pain, never on how a set feels while you are doing it.
The paper
Beyer R, Kongsgaard M, Hougs Kjær B, Øhlenschlæger T, Kjær M, Magnusson SP. Heavy slow resistance versus eccentric training as treatment for Achilles tendinopathy: a randomized controlled trial. American Journal of Sports Medicine 2015;43(7):1704–11.
Who

58 patients with chronic midportion Achilles tendinopathy.

What

Randomised to 12 weeks of heavy slow resistance training (3×/week) or the traditional twice-daily eccentric heel-drop protocol.

Found

Both groups improved significantly. The heavy-slow-resistance group showed a larger improvement in symptom and function scores at 12 weeks (41% versus 24%) with noticeably better session compliance (92% versus 78%), and both groups maintained their gains at 52 weeks with reduced tendon thickness and neovascularity.

Why it matters: it showed a three-times-weekly, gym-based protocol can match or beat the demanding twice-daily eccentric programme that preceded it, with much better adherence — which matters because a protocol nobody completes is not actually more effective in practice. Limitation: one tendon, one centre, and the eccentric comparison group's lower compliance may itself have narrowed the gap between methods.

Worked example
Freya, 38, 61 kg, three weeks into midportion Achilles pain. Pain is present after runs, settles by the next day, and is absent at rest — moderate irritability.
  1. Weeks 1–2: isometric calf holds, 5 × 45 s at ~70% effort, daily, plus continued easy running only if it stays under 3/10 and passes the next-morning test.If running fails the test twice, switch to cycling until isometrics alone are comfortable
  2. Weeks 2–3: introduce heavy slow resistance, bent-knee and straight-knee, starting light.3 × 8, bodyweight only, 3 s up / 3 s down, 3×/week
  3. Weeks 4–5: add load as 8 reps becomes easy; progress toward true heavy resistance.3 × 8 → 4 × 6 with a loaded vest or barbell, enough that rep 6 is hard
  4. Weeks 5–6: if HSR has been pain-free for two full weeks, add pogo hops.2 × 15, low amplitude, judged the next morning like everything else
  5. Weeks 6–7: progress elastic work to short bounding efforts, keep HSR at maintenance dose.2 × 20 m bounding; HSR drops to 2×/week
  6. Week 8: begin the graded return-to-run table in §42.6 from week 1, regardless of what volume she was running before — not from where she left off.Start point is set by the protocol, not by memory of her old mileage
So: by week 8 Freya has never once trained through rising next-day pain, and every stage advanced only once the previous one had been pain-free for roughly two weeks — the single biggest determinant of whether this becomes an eight-week problem or a recurring one.
Activate it

The adaptation this targets is tendon load tolerance — collagen synthesis and stiffness — rebuilt in a strict order that respects current irritability.

  • Do Isometric calf hold (or equivalent isometric for the tendon involved) while irritable; heavy slow resistance once settled; pogo hops and bounding once HSR is pain-free.
  • Load Isometrics at ~70% effort; HSR heavy enough that the top rep of each set is genuinely hard.
  • Dose Isometrics 5 × 45 s; HSR 3–4 × 6–8, 3 s up / 3 s down, 3×/week.
  • When Daily for isometrics while irritable; HSR on non-consecutive days to allow adaptation between sessions.
  • On the bike The default way to hold cardiovascular fitness while a lower-limb tendon settles: keep cadence high (90–100 rpm) and resistance low to minimise ankle and knee torque, ride zone 2 for 45–75 min, and add sweet-spot or threshold intervals once comfortable — none of it loads the tendon the way running does, so fitness and rehabilitation can run in parallel rather than in sequence.
  • Progress Move to the next stage only after the current one has passed the next-morning test for roughly two weeks running, not two good days.
  • Signal You're doing it right when the load you can tolerate before pain appears keeps rising week on week, even if some discomfort during a session never fully disappears.
B The staged structure is well supported; the exact week-by-week timing is individualised, not a fixed protocol.
Myth vs fact
“My tendon is inflamed, so I should ice it and rest until the inflammation goes”

Anti-inflammatories and rest should settle it like a sprained ankle would.

Chronic tendinopathy usually isn't an inflammatory condition

Biopsies of painful chronic tendons typically show disorganised collagen and new vessel growth, not an inflammatory infiltrate. Anti-inflammatory measures may dull pain briefly; they do not rebuild collagen organisation, and rest removes the loading stimulus that actually does. The exception is the first days after a sudden, large load spike — a genuinely reactive tendon — where a short period of reduced load before starting isometrics is reasonable.

42.3Bone Stress Injuries

Bone is living tissue on a constant repair cycle: cells called osteoclasts remove old or microdamaged bone, and osteoblasts lay new bone down behind them. Normal training damages bone a little and repairs it with a margin to spare — that margin is what gets you a slightly stronger bone than you started with. A bone stress injury is what happens when loading outpaces that repair for long enough that damage accumulates faster than it can be cleared.

It exists on a spectrum, not as a single event:

  • Stress reaction — bone marrow oedema on MRI, no visible fracture line. The earliest, most reversible stage.
  • Stress fracture — a visible fracture line, still usually without displacement.
  • Complete fracture — full cortical break, sometimes displaced, occasionally needing surgical fixation.
Bone stress injury risk factors, graded

This is the one category in this chapter where relative rest with genuinely reduced impact loading is required, not optional, and the reason is mechanistic: during active bone remodelling, the resorption phase runs slightly ahead of the repair phase, leaving a brief window of relative weakness at the damaged site. Continuing full-impact loading through that window does not give the tissue a training stimulus — it risks completing the fracture B. This is different from tendon and muscle, where some continued loading at a tolerable dose is almost always part of the solution.

Site matters — and some sites need imaging and specialist input before any loading plan
Risk categoryTypical sitesWhy
High-riskFemoral neck (tension side), anterior tibial cortex, navicular, fifth metatarsal (Jones fracture), medial malleolus, sesamoidsPoor blood supply, high risk of displacement, delayed union or nonunion; these need prompt imaging and specialist management, sometimes non-weight-bearing or surgical

Recovery timelines vary widely by site and grade of injury, but typically run from roughly 8–12 weeks for a low-risk site caught early to well over six months for a high-risk site or a complete fracture C. The graded return follows the same walk-run structure in §42.6, but started later, progressed more slowly, and — for high-risk sites — only after a clinician confirms healing on repeat imaging.

42.4Muscle Strains

A strain is a tear within the muscle or at the muscle-tendon junction, graded by how much tissue is involved:

Muscle strain grading
GradeWhat's tornTypical presentation
I — mildA small number of fibresTightness or a twinge, minimal strength or range loss, often able to continue at reduced effort
II — moderatePartial tearSudden pain, noticeable strength loss, often bruising over the following days
III — severeComplete ruptureImmediate loss of function, often a palpable gap; needs urgent clinical assessment

The hamstring, usually the biceps femoris at the musculotendinous junction, is the archetype because it is injured in a specific, repeatable way: at high running speed, in terminal swing, the muscle is lengthening eccentrically to decelerate the shin at the exact moment it is also closest to its longest length (chapter 41). That mechanism dictates the rehabilitation.

Modern management starts controlled loading early rather than immobilising. Within pain limits, gentle isometric and then concentric work begins within the first days, not weeks, because early mobility and blood flow support healing better than stillness does. The specific element that most changes outcomes is eccentric loading at long muscle length — exercises performed in a lengthened hip-flexed, knee-extended position, such as a controlled lunge-and-reach pattern or a progression toward the Nordic curl itself — because that is the exact position and contraction type the original injury occurred in, and strength built anywhere else transfers to it poorly.

The number worth remembering here is the re-injury rate: hamstring strains recur in a substantial minority of cases, and the single strongest modifiable predictor is returning to sport before eccentric strength at long length has been restored, rather than before pain has resolved B. Pain-free is not the same as ready, and rushing the gap between the two is the commonest reason a six-week hamstring problem becomes a recurring one for a whole season.

Key idea

A strained hamstring can feel entirely normal walking, jogging, and even striding well before the eccentric strength at long length that caused the injury has actually been rebuilt. Clear the strength deficit, not just the symptom, before returning to full-speed running.

42.5Plantar Heel Pain and Medial Tibial Stress Syndrome

Plantar heel pain (plantar fasciopathy)

Despite the common name "plantar fasciitis," biopsy findings here mirror tendinopathy rather than inflammation: a degenerative, disorganised matrix at the fascia's attachment to the heel bone, usually from cumulative overload rather than a single injury. The best-tested loading plan is a high-load progressive heel raise, performed with a rolled towel under the toes so the foot's arch-supporting "windlass" mechanism is loaded along with the calf: a 3-second lift, a 2-second hold at the top, and a 3-second lower, every other day.

High-load heel-raise progression for plantar heel pain, after Rathleff et al.
WeeksSets × repsNotes
1–23 × 12RMTowel under the toes; every other day; a heel insert or supportive shoe reduces load in the early, irritable phase
3–44 × 10RMLoad should rise as reps-in-reserve increase, not on a fixed calendar

A trial comparing this protocol with plantar-specific stretching found the loading group had meaningfully better function at three months; by twelve months both groups had converged, suggesting loading mainly buys a faster recovery rather than a different ceiling B.

Medial tibial stress syndrome ("shin splints")

This sits mechanistically between a tendon problem and a bone stress injury: repetitive bending load on the tibia combined with traction from the soleus and deep calf fascia produces pain along the lower, inner shin — broad and diffuse, unlike the pinpoint tenderness of a tibial stress fracture, which is the key feature that distinguishes the two and the reason a clinician's examination matters here specifically. Management is relative rest from the aggravating impact volume, progressive calf and tibialis posterior/anterior strengthening, and the same load-error correction that underlies most of this chapter — a mileage jump, a surface change, or worn-out footwear are the usual culprits. Where tenderness is focal rather than diffuse, treat it as a possible bone stress injury (§42.3) until a clinician says otherwise.

42.6A Generic Return-To-Run Protocol

Whatever the tissue, the return to running follows the same logic: start well below the level that caused trouble, advance only on the next-morning test, and treat a setback as information rather than failure. The table below is a generic starting template — slow it down for bone stress injuries and for anything still moderately irritable, and skip ahead only from a stage that has already passed cleanly.

Two adjustments are worth stating explicitly. For a bone stress injury, do not enter this table until a clinician has confirmed sufficient healing, start at least one full stage earlier than symptoms alone would suggest, and extend each stage to two weeks rather than one. For a tendon, keep the heavy slow resistance work running in parallel throughout this table at a maintenance dose — stopping it the moment running resumes is one of the most common, and most avoidable, causes of recurrence.

42.7Preventing Recurrence

Every condition in this chapter has the same two-part prevention plan, and both parts were usually missing before the injury happened in the first place.

  1. The strength work that should have been there first

    Calf and Achilles capacity, Nordic hamstring curls, hip abductor and gluteal strength — the exact exercise set in chapter 41, continued as ongoing training rather than dropped once symptoms resolve.

  2. Load management that respects the slow tissues

    Roughly 10% weekly progression as a default, one new stimulus at a time, a planned lighter week every third or fourth, and particular caution around the known precipitants — a return from time off, a sudden terrain or surface change, a jump in intensity sessions. The full framework is in chapter 39.

Nearly every injury in this chapter was, at its root, a mismatch between how fast the engine improved and how fast the tendon, bone or muscle could keep up (chapter 1). Fixing the symptom without fixing that mismatch is treating the alarm, not the fire.

Five things to carry forward

  1. Across tendon, muscle and most bone injuries, controlled progressive loading is the treatment, and progress is judged by next-morning symptoms, not by pain during the exercise itself.
  2. Tendinopathy is a failed-healing, disorganised-collagen problem rather than inflammation, and responds to a four-stage ladder: isometrics, heavy slow resistance, energy-storage and plyometric loading, then return to sport.
  3. Bone stress injuries are the one category where genuine relative rest from impact is required rather than optional, because active bone remodelling runs through a brief window of real weakness; high-risk sites need imaging and specialist input before any loading plan begins.
  4. Hamstring and other muscle strains need eccentric strength at the lengthened position restored, not just pain resolved, before return to sport — rushing that gap is the main driver of the high re-injury rate.
  5. Every condition here has the same two-part prevention plan: the strength work in chapter 41 that should have been there already, and the load management in chapter 39 that prevents the next tissue from falling behind the engine driving it.
