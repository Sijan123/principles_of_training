Carbohydrate

Glycogen, intake rates, loading and train-low

A marathon run at a steady effort burns through roughly 2,500–3,000 kilocalories' worth of carbohydrate-equivalent fuel. Your entire carbohydrate storage system — every granule of glycogen in every muscle fibre, plus the liver's separate reserve — holds about 2,000–2,400 kilocalories, full to the brim, the morning of the race. The arithmetic does not close. Somewhere between kilometre 25 and the finish, the tank that started full is running on fumes, and what happens next depends on which tank it was and how well you have prepared to bridge the gap. This chapter is about reading that gauge, and about the limits — real, physical, measurable — on how fast you can refill it while still moving.

28.1Two Tanks, Not One

Glycogen is the body's way of storing glucose compactly: thousands of glucose molecules strung together into a branched, water-soluble granule, stored mainly in two places that do very different jobs. Muscle glycogen sits inside the fibres that are doing the work — roughly 400–500 g in an adult of moderate size, more in a well-trained, well-fed endurance athlete, less in someone untrained or under-fuelled B. Liver glycogen is a separate, smaller reserve of about 80–100 g, and its job is not to fuel muscle directly at all — it is to hold blood glucose steady for every tissue that depends on it, the brain above all.

Do the energy arithmetic and the numbers are blunt. Carbohydrate yields about 4 kcal per gram, so 450 g of muscle glycogen is roughly 1,800 kcal, and 90 g of liver glycogen is roughly 360 kcal. Call it 2,000–2,200 kcal of total carbohydrate energy on board for most people, closer to 2,400–2,500 kcal for a large, well-glycogen-loaded endurance athlete. Set beside the 2,500–3,000 kcal of carbohydrate-equivalent fuel a marathon run at a demanding pace actually requires, the shortfall is obvious before the gun even goes. It is covered three ways: by burning more fat relative to carbohydrate as the pace allows (chapter 30), by eating carbohydrate during the event, and, if neither covers the gap, by slowing down.

In plain terms

Think of the body as running two fuel tanks with two different gauges.

The muscle tank is like the fuel in a car's own cylinder — local, immediate, and only useful to the engine it sits in. If your calf runs out, your calf slows down; your arms are unaffected. The liver tank is more like a reservoir feeding the whole city's water supply — small, but critical, because it is the only thing keeping the pressure (blood glucose) steady for every downstream user, including an organ, the brain, that has almost no fuel of its own and cannot simply wait for a delivery. Running the muscle tank dry is a local breakdown. Running the liver tank dry is closer to a citywide outage.

28.2Bonking, The Wall, And Why They Feel Different

Cyclists call it bonking; marathon runners call it hitting the wall, usually somewhere around kilometre 30–32. Both names describe the same underlying event — glycogen running critically low — but the two tanks fail in distinguishable ways, and knowing which one is emptying tells you what to do about it.

Muscle glycogen depletion is local and mechanical. A fibre running low on its own glycogen cannot resynthesise ATP fast enough through glycolysis to sustain the same power output, so that muscle, specifically, loses force and coordination. The sensation is heavy, stiff, uncooperative legs, a pace that will not hold no matter how hard you try, and a stride or pedal stroke that starts to look wrong on video before it feels wrong to you. It is not dangerous. It is simply a local fuel shortage in the tissue doing the work, and the fix — slow down, let fat oxidation and whatever blood glucose is available carry a larger share of the load — is self-limiting.

Liver glycogen depletion is a different order of problem, because it threatens blood glucose directly, and the brain depends on blood glucose almost exclusively; it cannot use fat as fuel and can only switch to ketones after days of adaptation that a single long run does not provide. The result is hypoglycaemia: confusion, irritability, poor decision-making, dizziness, and in the extreme case a genuinely dangerous collapse. This is why the worst-feeling moments late in an ultramarathon are often as much cognitive as muscular — missed turns, sudden tearfulness, an inability to do simple maths at an aid station — and why they usually resolve within minutes of taking on sugar, because topping up blood glucose restores brain function almost immediately even though it does nothing to refill the muscle tank that triggered the slowdown in the first place.

This distinction is also the real reason carbohydrate eaten mid-exercise works at all. A gel swallowed at 90 minutes does not meaningfully refill muscle glycogen while you are still running — muscle glycogen resynthesis needs the insulin response and the relative rest that only come after you stop. What it does is defend blood glucose and supply a steady stream of fuel the muscle can oxidise directly from the blood, alongside whatever glycogen remains. You are not refilling the local tank; you are keeping the citywide reservoir topped up and feeding the engine from a separate hose.

Key idea

“Running out of carbohydrate” is not one failure, it is two, and they feel different on purpose: muscle glycogen depletion slows you down locally and safely, liver glycogen depletion threatens the brain and ends the event. Fuelling during exercise mainly defends the second one.

28.3Daily Intake Targets — Match The Tank To The Training

How much carbohydrate to eat on an ordinary training day is not a single number; it scales with how much glycogen that day's training actually drains, which is itself a function of duration and intensity together, not either alone. The widely used bracket system below, derived from sports-nutrition position statements A, sets the daily target by training load rather than body weight alone, because body weight alone says nothing about whether today was a rest day or a six-hour ride.

These are ranges, not prescriptions handed down from a lab bench to your plate; where an individual sits within a bracket depends on body size, training efficiency, heat, and simple appetite. The number is also meant to move: a rest day inside a high-volume week should sit at the bottom of the High bracket or even in Moderate, not stay pinned to a flat daily average, because glycogen that is not being drained does not need refilling at the same rate (chapter 34 covers the day-to-day playbook in full).

28.4During Exercise — The Ceiling, The Ratio, And Why It Exists

Fuelling needs during a session scale almost entirely with duration, and there is a hard physiological ceiling involved that no amount of willpower moves.

  1. Under ~60–75 minutes

    Nothing is needed from a fuel-supply standpoint; muscle and liver glycogen, plus a normal pre-session meal, cover it. (Carbohydrate mouth rinsing, covered below, is the exception for short, maximal efforts.)

  2. 1–2.5 hours

    30–60 g/h, taken from the first 15–20 minutes rather than saved for when you feel the dip — absorption lags intake by roughly that long, as chapter 27 details.

  3. Beyond 2.5 hours

    Up to 90 g/h, but only by using multiple transportable carbohydrates — glucose (or maltodextrin) plus fructose together, not more of one sugar.

  4. Trained, gut-trained athletes, long or very hard events

    Up to 120 g/h, at a glucose:fructose ratio of roughly 1:0.8, is now the contemporary ceiling cited for elite and gut-trained endurance athletes B, up from the 90 g/h figure that stood as the working limit through the 2010s.

The reason a single sugar caps out near 60 g/h, and a blend does not, is a plumbing problem covered in detail in chapter 27's discussion of gut transporters: glucose crosses the intestinal wall mainly through a carrier protein called SGLT1, which saturates — reaches its maximum turnover rate — at a glucose intake around 60 g/h. Push more glucose, or more maltodextrin or sucrose (which is half glucose), past that point and the surplus is not absorbed any faster; it sits in the gut, ferments, and causes the bloating and cramping that athletes blame on “my stomach,” when the real cause is a saturated transporter. Fructose, however, crosses through a separate carrier, GLUT5, with its own independent ceiling. Because glucose and fructose use different doors, taking them together does not split one ceiling in two — it stacks a second ceiling on top of the first, which is the entire mechanism behind reaching 90–120 g/h rather than being capped at 60 A.

Mouth rinsing — fuelling the brain, not the muscle

For efforts under roughly an hour — a hard time trial, a parkrun, a race-pace 5K — there usually isn't time for swallowed carbohydrate to be absorbed and matter metabolically at all. Yet simply swishing a carbohydrate solution around the mouth for five to ten seconds and then spitting it out, with no sugar reaching the gut, has repeatedly improved short, high-intensity performance by a small but real margin B. The mechanism is not metabolic; it is in the head. Carbohydrate-sensing receptors in the mouth and throat signal reward and pre-motor regions of the brain, lowering perceived effort and nudging the brain's pacing decisions, entirely independent of blood glucose. It is one of the few genuinely free interventions in sports nutrition: it costs nothing, carries no gut risk, and only works for exactly the efforts — short, hard, under about 45–60 minutes — where swallowing the same sugar would do almost nothing.

28.5Carbohydrate Loading — The One-Day Version That Replaced The Week-Long One

The original 1970s loading protocol asked athletes to deplete muscle glycogen with an exhausting training session, follow it with three to four days of near-zero carbohydrate (and the fatigue, irritability and poor sleep that come with it), then gorge on carbohydrate for the final three days before racing. It worked, but it also wrecked the final week of taper for an uncertain extra margin, and it has been abandoned by virtually everyone who understands the physiology, including the researchers who first proposed it.

The modern version, built on the finding that trained muscle's glycogen-storing enzyme (glycogen synthase) is already primed by training itself and does not need a depletion phase to supercompensate, is far simpler: eat 10–12 g/kg/day of carbohydrate for just 1–2 days before the event, while tapering training volume right down so the carbohydrate is not immediately burned off A. Done this way, trained muscle can push stored glycogen from a resting baseline of roughly 100–120 mmol/kg wet muscle to 180–220 mmol/kg or higher — a 50–100% increase in the local tank — without a single depleted, miserable day beforehand.

Loading is worth doing for continuous efforts of roughly 90 minutes or longer where glycogen availability is plausibly the limiting factor — marathons, long triathlons, road races over about 90–120 km. It does essentially nothing useful for a 20-minute 5K or a strength session, where glycogen was never going to be the bottleneck, and the extra 2–3 kg of water that typically comes with the stored glycogen (each gram of glycogen is stored with roughly 3 g of water) is simply unhelpful weight to carry for no benefit.

Myth vs fact
“I need to deplete myself with a brutal session and then starve for three days before I can load”

The idea that supercompensation requires first running the tank to empty.

The depletion phase was abandoned decades ago

Sherman, Costill and colleagues showed in the 1980s that trained athletes achieve essentially the same glycogen supercompensation from 1–3 days of high carbohydrate intake and reduced training alone, with no depletion phase required. The week-long protocol persists mainly in folklore, not in current sports-nutrition guidance.

28.6Train-Low, Sleep-Low — Training The Signal, Not The Race

If low glycogen is a problem to be solved before a race, it is deliberately created as a training tool in a different context. The idea behind train-low (and its best-studied variant, sleep-low: a glycogen-depleting session in the evening, an overnight fast with no carbohydrate refeeding, then an easy fasted session the next morning) is that low glycogen itself is a signal, and amplifying that signal on purpose might amplify the adaptation that follows it.

The mechanism has real support. Low muscle glycogen increases the activity of two energy-sensing proteins already met in chapter 12 — AMPK and p38 MAPK — above what the same session produces with normal glycogen on board, and that amplified signal drives greater expression of the genes behind mitochondrial biogenesis and fat-oxidation enzymes. Several training studies built around this logic report meaningfully higher whole-body fat oxidation after a train-low block — increases on the order of 25% in some trials — alongside measurable rises in markers like PGC-1α and the enzymes that shuttle fat into the mitochondrion B for the signalling and enzyme changes specifically.

Where the evidence gets honest and uncomfortable is performance. The adaptation markers move; whether that translates into faster racing is a different question, and the trials that have actually measured race-relevant performance disagree. A single well-known sleep-low study in triathletes found a worthwhile time-trial improvement; several others, including systematic reviews pooling multiple protocols, find no consistent performance advantage over training with normal, well-periodized carbohydrate availability C. There is also a real cost that is easy to underweight: training with low glycogen measurably impairs the ability to hit the power output or pace a high-intensity interval session demands, which means doing it on the wrong day quietly degrades the very sessions that build the fitness you actually race on.

Where this goes wrong

Train-low sessions belong on easy, aerobic days — never immediately before a key interval or threshold session, and never as a daily habit. Running every session glycogen-depleted does not multiply the benefit; it mostly produces chronic low energy availability, poor-quality key sessions, and, in susceptible athletes, the hormonal and bone consequences covered in chapter 34. This is a tool used once or twice a week in a base-training phase by athletes who already have solid everyday fuelling habits, not a default way to eat.

Grade the honest verdict overall: B for the molecular and fat-oxidation adaptations, C for whether it reliably moves race performance. It is worth doing — sparingly, in a base or early-build phase, on sessions that are aerobic rather than high-intensity, for an athlete who has the fuelling fundamentals of the rest of this chapter already in place. It is not worth doing as a replacement for them.

Worked example — fuelling four hours on a bike

Worked example
Tomás, 36, 74 kg, riding a 4-hour gravel race with rolling climbs. Gut-trained over the previous eight weeks up to 90 g/h; plan is to hold 80–90 g/h throughout rather than push to his absolute ceiling on race day.

Activate it

Matching intake to the fuelling rules above, in training and on race day.

  • Eat On high and very-high training days, hit the top of your g/kg bracket from the daily-targets table — rice, pasta, bread, fruit, dairy, not supplements.
  • Dose During sessions over 2.5 h, build toward 90–120 g/h using a 2:1 or 1:0.8 glucose:fructose product, never a single sugar past 60 g/h.
  • Timing Start fuelling in the first 15–20 minutes of anything over an hour — absorption lags intake, so feeding the dip is always too late.
  • On the bike Rehearse the exact race-day blend and rate on an indoor trainer: 2–3 h at 60–70% FTP with carbohydrate from minute one, because pace, and therefore gut comfort, stays controllable while you push the dose up.
  • Signal You're fuelling correctly when effort feels flat across the back half of a long session rather than falling off a cliff in the final third.

28.8The Fuel Mix, And Where This Evidence Comes From

Fat vs carbohydrate oxidation by intensity

Interactive
Drag the intensity slider to see how the fuel mix shifts from fat-dominant at an easy jog to almost entirely carbohydrate at threshold — the shift that decides how fast your glycogen tank empties at any given pace.
The paper
Jeukendrup AE. Carbohydrate and exercise performance: the role of multiple transportable carbohydrates. Curr Opin Clin Nutr Metab Care 2010;13(4):452–457.
Who

A synthesis of feeding and performance trials in trained cyclists and runners, not a single new experiment.

What

Reviewed exogenous carbohydrate oxidation rates across single-sugar and glucose-plus-fructose feeding protocols during prolonged cycling.

Found

Single-sugar feeding plateaus around 1 g/min (~60 g/h) regardless of how much more is ingested; glucose+fructose blends raise exogenous oxidation by up to roughly 65% over glucose alone, reaching 1.2–1.75 g/min (~70–105 g/h) in the trials reviewed.

Why it matters: this review consolidated a decade of feeding trials into the practical rule that now underpins every major sports-nutrition body's advice on fuelling beyond 60 g/h. Limitation: it is a review of lab-based cycling trials in already carbohydrate-tolerant athletes; it establishes the absorption ceiling convincingly but says less about gut comfort in untrained or gut-untrained athletes attempting the same rates, which is why gut training (chapter 27) is a separate, necessary step.

Five things to carry forward

  1. Total carbohydrate storage — roughly 400–500 g of muscle glycogen plus 80–100 g of liver glycogen, around 2,000–2,400 kcal — is smaller than the demand of a hard marathon or longer effort, which is why fuelling strategy exists at all.
  2. Muscle glycogen depletion is a local, self-limiting slowdown; liver glycogen depletion threatens blood glucose and therefore the brain, which is why mid-exercise carbohydrate mainly defends the second tank, not the first.
  3. Carbohydrate absorption is capped near 60 g/h through a single transporter (SGLT1), and only combining glucose with fructose — which uses the separate GLUT5 transporter — unlocks the 90–120 g/h rates now used by trained, gut-trained endurance athletes.
  4. Modern carbohydrate loading is 1–2 days at 10–12 g/kg with a taper, not a week of depletion and starvation, and is only worth doing for continuous efforts of roughly 90 minutes or more.
  5. Train-low and sleep-low sessions genuinely amplify fat-oxidation signalling, but the performance evidence is mixed and the sessions impair high-intensity quality — use them sparingly, on easy days, never before a key interval session.
