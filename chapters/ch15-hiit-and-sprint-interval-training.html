HIIT & Sprint Interval Training

Protocol design, time near VO2max, and what intensity buys you

In 1996 a Japanese physiologist asked seven cyclists to ride twenty seconds at an intensity they could not possibly sustain, rest ten seconds, and repeat until they failed — which took about four minutes. Six weeks later their maximal oxygen uptake had risen 14% and their anaerobic capacity by 28%. The protocol took his name, became the most misused word in fitness, and is now performed almost nowhere as published. The interesting question is not whether intensity works. It is which intensity, for how long, how often, and at what cost — because unlike easy training, hard training has a bill attached.

15.1What HIIT Is, and What It Isn't

"HIIT" has become a label for any workout that makes you breathe hard, which has made it nearly useless as a term. In the research literature it means something specific, and the distinctions matter because the protocols drive genuinely different adaptations.

Two things follow immediately. First, a circuit of burpees and kettlebell swings may be hard, but it is usually not HIIT in the physiological sense — the limiter is local muscular fatigue and technique, not oxygen delivery, and oxygen uptake rarely gets near maximal. Second, HIIT and SIT are not the same intervention. They produce overlapping results by substantially different routes, which is why the evidence for them diverges.

In plain terms

Imagine filling a bath from a tap.

Easy training makes the bath bigger — more mitochondria, more capillaries, more blood volume, more places to put oxygen and more machinery to use it. You do that by running the tap for a long time at a comfortable rate, and you can do it almost every day because it costs you little.

Hard intervals work on the tap itself. The only way to find out how fast water can flow is to open it all the way, and the only way to make the pipe wider is to keep demanding that maximum. That is what a four-minute interval does: it drags your oxygen uptake up to its ceiling and holds it there, which is the one stimulus easy riding and jogging never provide.

And here is the part people skip. You cannot open the tap fully for very long, and doing it often damages the plumbing. Easy volume is cheap and you buy a lot of it. Intensity is expensive and you buy a little. Almost every training mistake in this chapter comes from getting that budget wrong.

15.2Why Intensity Works — Two Separate Routes

High-intensity training drives adaptation through two mechanisms that operate on different timescales and in different tissues. Confusing them is why people argue about protocols.

  1. The central route: demanding maximal oxygen delivery

    To reach your oxygen-uptake ceiling, your heart must move the most blood it can — so a session that holds you at that ceiling repeatedly is a direct stimulus to the left ventricle: more filling, greater compliance, larger stroke volume. This is what raises VO2max, and it needs time spent at or near the ceiling. Oxygen uptake takes 60–120 seconds to get there, which is why work bouts under 30 seconds accumulate almost none of it. See chapter 9.

  2. The peripheral route: molecular alarm signals

    Very hard work empties phosphocreatine and glycogen, floods the cell with calcium, and sends the AMP:ATP ratio sharply upward. That activates AMPK and calcium-dependent kinases, which converge on PGC-1α and switch on mitochondrial biogenesis. This route does not require time near VO2max at all — which is exactly why 4 × 30 seconds of all-out sprinting, totalling two minutes of work, measurably raises VO2max in untrained people. See chapter 8.

Key idea

Long intervals and all-out sprints both work, but not for the same reason. Long intervals buy you cardiac adaptation by accumulating time at your oxygen ceiling. Sprints buy you peripheral adaptation by creating a large metabolic alarm in very little time. If you are already well trained, the central route is usually the one with headroom — which is why trained athletes get more from 4 × 8 minutes than from Wingate sprints, while a sedentary beginner improves on either.

15.3The Currency: Time Near VO2max

If the central route is what you are after, the thing to count is not how hard the session felt but how many minutes you spent above about 90% of your maximal oxygen uptake. That is the accepted proxy for the stimulus, and it changes how you read a session.

A recent systematic review and meta-analysis pooled 239 HIIT protocols from 86 articles and asked which designs accumulate the most time near the ceiling. The answer was consistent: work intervals of two minutes or longer accumulate more time at and above 90% of VO2max than either very short (≤30 s) or moderate-duration bouts, and protocols using variable intensity within the work bout also performed well. The review was appropriately careful to note that the link between accumulated time near VO2max and long-term adaptation still needs direct investigation — it is a well-reasoned proxy, not a proven dose–response. B

There is one important nuance. Short intervals with incomplete recovery — the 30-seconds-hard, 15-seconds-easy format — behave differently from isolated sprints with long rests. Because the brief recovery never lets oxygen uptake fall far, repeated short efforts can keep you near the ceiling while blood lactate stays far more tolerable than in a single long block. In a 2025 crossover study of well-trained cyclists and triathletes, 3 × 13 × 30/15 produced more time above 90% of VO2max than 6 × 5 minutes when the intensity was fixed — though not when both were self-paced maximally. The paper
Helgerud J, Høydal K, Wang E, Karlsen T, Berg P, Bjerkaas M, Simonsen T, Helgesen C, Hjorth N, Bach R, Hoff J. Aerobic high-intensity intervals improve V̇O2max more than moderate training. Medicine & Science in Sports & Exercise 2007;39(4):665–71.
Who

40 moderately trained men, randomised to four training groups.

What

Eight weeks, three sessions a week, with total work matched across groups: long slow distance, lactate-threshold training, 15/15 intervals, and 4 × 4 min at 90–95% of maximal heart rate with active recovery.

Found

The high-intensity interval groups raised VO2max significantly more than long slow distance or threshold training (P<0.01). The 4 × 4 group improved VO2max by roughly 7% — despite doing the same total work as groups that barely improved at all.

Why it matters: because the work was equated, the result isolates intensity as the active ingredient for raising VO2max. This is the study that made 4 × 4 the default VO2max protocol worldwide, and the finding has broadly replicated: typical gains of 7–10% over 8 weeks. Limitation: moderately trained men, eight weeks, VO2max as the outcome. It does not show that intensity-dominant training produces better race performance over a season — and the descriptive evidence on how elite endurance athletes actually train points firmly the other way, toward large volumes of easy work with a small fraction of intensity (chapter 14). Both things are true: intervals are the most efficient way to raise the ceiling, and they are a small minority of a good endurance programme.

Two other studies belong beside it. Tabata and colleagues (1996) established how much adaptation very brief supramaximal work can produce — 8 × 20 s at about 170% of VO2max to exhaustion, raising VO2max around 14% and anaerobic capacity around 28%. And Seiler and colleagues (2013) compared 4 × 4, 4 × 8 and 4 × 16 minutes in trained cyclists over seven weeks and found the middle option best: 4 × 8 min produced roughly a 10% rise in VO2peak and about 16% more power at 4 mmol/L lactate. The hardest protocol was not the best one. That is the most useful single fact in this chapter.

15.4Choosing Your Session

Worked example
Dan, 36, runs four times a week, 45 km. Recent 5 km: 22:00. He has one hard session a week to spend and wants to raise his ceiling.
  1. Convert his 5 km to pace per kilometre.22:00 ÷ 5 = 4:24 per km
  2. Interval (I) pace sits close to 3–5 km race pace — for a runner at this level, roughly 5–10 s/km faster than 5 km pace.4:24 − ~7 s ≈ 4:17 per km → ~64 s per 250 m
  3. Option A, long intervals: 5 × 3 min at I pace with 2 min jog.5 × 3 = 15 min of work; total block 23 min
  4. Estimated time above 90% of VO₂max for a long-interval format — roughly half the work time once uptake has risen.15 × ~0.5 ≈ 7–8 min near the ceiling
  5. Option B, short intervals: 2 × 10 × (30 s at I pace / 15 s jog), 3 min between sets.20 × 0.5 = 10 min of work; total block 18 min
  6. Short intervals with incomplete recovery hold uptake high for a greater fraction of the work.10 × ~0.55 ≈ 5–6 min near the ceiling, at much lower blood lactate
  7. Weekly placement: one hard session, three easy runs. Easy pace from the same 5 km time is roughly 1:15–1:30/km slower.4:24 + 80 s ≈ 5:45 per km easy
So: both sessions are defensible, and Option A buys slightly more time at the ceiling. Dan should start with 5 × 3 min at 4:17/km, 2 min jog, and keep Option B in reserve for when the long intervals become psychologically intolerable — it delivers a similar stimulus at noticeably lower discomfort. What he must not do is add a second hard session: with 45 km a week, one is his budget.

If your sport is cycling, or you are using a trainer as a low-impact engine-builder, the equivalent library of sessions is laid out in chapter 44, where the trainer's precision makes interval targets far easier to hit than on the road.

15.5The Fat-Loss Claim, Examined

HIIT's popular reputation rests heavily on "afterburn" — the idea that a short intense session keeps your metabolism elevated for many hours, making it a uniquely efficient fat-loss tool. The phenomenon is real and the magnitude is routinely overstated.

Excess post-exercise oxygen consumption (EPOC) is the elevated metabolic rate after exercise, spent restoring phosphocreatine, clearing metabolites, replenishing oxygen stores, and dealing with a raised body temperature and catecholamine levels. It is larger after intense exercise than after moderate exercise. But in absolute terms it typically amounts to a small fraction of the session's own energy cost — on the order of 6–15% — which for most sessions means a few dozen kilocalories, not a few hundred. B

Myth vs fact
“Twenty minutes of HIIT burns more fat than an hour of steady cardio”

Because of the afterburn, so steady-state work is a waste of time.

Per unit of energy expended, they are close to equivalent — and HIIT's real advantage is time, not magic

When total energy expenditure is matched, HIIT and moderate continuous training produce broadly similar fat loss. HIIT's genuine advantage is that it achieves comparable cardiometabolic benefit in substantially less time, which for a busy person is a real and important advantage. But a 20-minute session does not out-burn a 60-minute one, and the afterburn does not close that gap. If fat loss is the goal, the dominant variables are total energy expenditure over weeks and energy intake — see chapter 34.

Where intensity genuinely shines is in cardiorespiratory fitness and metabolic health per minute invested. VO2max is one of the strongest available predictors of all-cause mortality, and interval training is the most time-efficient way to raise it. For a sedentary adult with twenty minutes, three times a week, HIIT is not a fashionable option — it is close to the optimal one.

15.6Programming It: The Budget

Intensity is the one training variable where more is reliably worse past a low threshold. The limit is not willpower; it is autonomic and neuromuscular recovery.

Activate it

The adaptation targeted here is maximal oxygen delivery — a larger, better-filling left ventricle and a higher VO2max. This is the block, not the year.

  • Do 4 × 4 min at 90–95% of maximum heart rate (or ~105–110% of threshold pace/power), with 3 min of easy active recovery. Running, cycling, rowing or uphill — the mode matters less than the intensity.
  • Warm up 12–15 min easy with 2–3 progressive 1-min efforts. Going into interval one cold wastes it.
  • Dose 1–2 sessions a week for 3–4 weeks, then back off to one or none for a fortnight.
  • Pace it Set the target from what you can hold on interval four, not interval one. 290-275-260-250 is one good interval and three compromised ones.
  • On the bike 4 × 8 min at ~106% of FTP with 3 min easy — the protocol that beat both 4 × 4 and 4 × 16 in trained cyclists. Or 5 × 4 min at ~113% FTP, 4 min easy. Indoors, in ERG or slope mode, with a fan running.
  • Stop when You cannot hold the target in the current interval, or your heart rate stops reaching the zone despite maximal effort. Both mean the session is finished, whatever the plan said.
  • Signal You're doing it right when the same interval target becomes achievable at a lower perceived effort over three weeks, and when a retested 5-minute maximal effort improves. If nothing has moved in four weeks, the problem is recovery or total volume, not the intervals.
A Replicated in multiple controlled trials, including work-matched designs; typical VO2max gains 5–10% over 6–8 weeks in moderately trained people, smaller in the already highly trained.

15.7Six Ways People Get It Wrong

  1. Doing it too often

    Three or four hard sessions a week produces an athlete who is permanently flat and cannot reach the required intensity in any of them — which is the definition of wasted work. Hard sessions have to be hard, and that requires easy days around them.

  2. Starting too fast

    The most common error in the room. Interval one at a pace you cannot repeat turns a VO2max session into a glycolytic one, and you will spend intervals three to five accumulating fatigue rather than stimulus.

  3. Recovering too hard, or not enough

    In long-interval work the recovery should be genuinely easy — its job is to let you produce the next interval. In short 30/15 formats the recovery is deliberately incomplete, because keeping oxygen uptake high is the entire point. Using the wrong recovery for the format changes what the session does.

  4. Using heart rate to pace short intervals

    Heart rate lags 30–60 seconds. In a 30-second effort it tells you about the effort you have already finished. Use pace, power, or honest perceived effort.

  5. Confusing "hard" with "high oxygen uptake"

    A metabolic conditioning circuit can be brutal and still never take you near your oxygen ceiling, because the limiter is local muscle fatigue, grip, or technique. If you want the central adaptation, use a mode you can drive hard with large muscle groups and good economy — running, cycling, rowing, uphill walking.

  6. Running it year-round

    VO2max work is a block stimulus. Three to four weeks, then reduce. Keeping it in permanently produces accumulated fatigue that the athlete then interprets as a plateau and treats with more intervals.

Before you start intervals

High-intensity training is safe and well tolerated for the great majority of healthy people, and it is used therapeutically in cardiac and metabolic rehabilitation under supervision. But the risk profile is not identical to walking, and two groups should take advice first:

  • Anyone with known or suspected cardiovascular disease, uncontrolled hypertension, chest pain on exertion, unexplained breathlessness, palpitations or syncope — get assessed before doing maximal work.
  • Anyone returning from a long layoff, an illness, or a musculoskeletal injury. Intervals multiply tissue loading rates, and connective tissue adapts far more slowly than your engine (chapter 19). Build four to six weeks of easy volume first; this is where runners most often break.

If you are over 40, sedentary, and planning to start sprinting — start with one-minute efforts, not thirty-second all-out ones, and build for a month before you go near maximal.

15.8Where Intervals Sit in a Whole Programme

The honest summary of this chapter has two halves that sound contradictory and are not.

Intensity is the most potent and time-efficient stimulus available for raising maximal oxygen uptake. Work-matched trials make this unambiguous: at the same total work, the interval groups improve and the continuous groups largely do not.

And yet elite endurance athletes spend roughly 80% of their training below their first lactate threshold, with only 15–20% hard. That is not a contradiction, because the two statements answer different questions. Intervals are the best way to raise the ceiling. Easy volume is the best way to build the peripheral machinery, the fat oxidation and the durability that let you use a high fraction of that ceiling for hours — and to do so at a fatigue cost low enough to be repeated daily for years.

A programme that is all intensity raises the ceiling and then stalls, because it cannot accumulate enough volume to develop anything else and the athlete is too tired to execute. A programme that is all easy volume builds a wide base under a low ceiling. The resolution is not a compromise in the middle — the middle is the grey zone that serves neither purpose. It is a large base of genuinely easy work with a small, well-executed dose of genuinely hard work, and almost nothing in between.

The hardest session is not the best session. In seven weeks of trained cyclists, 4 × 8 minutes beat both 4 × 4 and 4 × 16 — the optimum was an interior point, and it always is. Seiler et al., Scand J Med Sci Sports 2013

Five things to carry forward

  1. "HIIT" covers several different interventions: long intervals drive cardiac adaptation by accumulating time at your oxygen ceiling, while all-out sprints drive peripheral adaptation through a large molecular alarm signal from very little work.
  2. The currency for raising VO2max is minutes spent above about 90% of it — and work bouts of two minutes or more accumulate the most, which is why 4 × 4 and 4 × 8 remain the default protocols.
  3. Work-matched trials show intensity is the active ingredient for raising VO2max, with typical gains of 5–10% in 6–8 weeks — but the optimum is an interior point, not the hardest protocol you can survive.
  4. The "afterburn" is real and small; HIIT's genuine advantage for health is fitness gained per minute invested, not a metabolic loophole for fat loss.
  5. Intensity is a budget, not a volume: one to two hard sessions a week for most people, run as 3–4 week blocks, inside a programme that is otherwise overwhelmingly easy.
