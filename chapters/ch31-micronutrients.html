Micronutrients

Iron, vitamin D, calcium — and the antioxidant paradox

Finish a hard interval session and something happens that has nothing to do with sweat or sore legs. Within three to six hours, a hormone made by the liver rises several-fold and tells the gut to stop letting iron through the door. The harder the session, the bigger the rise. This is not a malfunction — it is the same alarm the body uses to withhold iron from an infection, repurposed by exercise. But an athlete training six days a week trips that alarm six days a week, and the gut obeys it every time. That one mechanism explains more about endurance-athlete iron deficiency than diet ever could, and it is the reason the right fix is almost never “just take more iron.”

31.1Training Raises The Bill, Not The Case For A Multivitamin

Training increases the turnover of several micronutrients at once. More red blood cells are made and destroyed (running in particular crushes red cells against the ground with every foot strike — foot-strike haemolysis). More mitochondrial enzymes are built, and many of them use iron, magnesium and B vitamins as working parts, not just ingredients. Collagen remodelling in tendon and bone needs vitamin C and zinc as cofactors. Bone itself remodels faster under load and needs calcium, vitamin D and vitamin K on tap. Sweat carries out small but non-zero amounts of several minerals. None of this is controversial, and all of it is true.

What does not follow from any of it is that a harder-training person needs a broader-spectrum supplement. A multivitamin adds a little of everything whether or not that nutrient is the one actually running short, and for the two nutrients in this chapter with real downside risk — iron and high-dose antioxidants — more is not automatically better and can measurably be worse. The useful question is never “should I take a supplement” but “which specific nutrient, in which specific athlete, is there good reason to think is actually short — and how do I find out, rather than guess.”

In plain terms

Imagine a factory that switches from one shift a day to three.

It burns through specific spare parts faster — a particular bolt, a particular filter, a particular lubricant used in the machines running the extra shifts. The sensible fix is to order more of those parts, in the quantity the machines actually use. Ordering a bigger box of every part in the catalogue, including hundreds the factory's machines never touch, fixes nothing and costs money for no return. Worse, overstocking one particular part — the one this chapter spends most of its time on — can jam the machine it was meant to help.

31.2Iron — The One That Actually Matters Most

Iron's job is oxygen handling at two levels: haemoglobin carries it in blood, myoglobin stores and shuttles it inside the muscle fibre. Roughly two-thirds of the body's iron sits in haemoglobin alone. Endurance training raises the demand for both — total haemoglobin mass expands as part of the plasma-volume and red-cell adaptations covered in chapter 11 — while simultaneously increasing the rate of iron loss, through foot-strike haemolysis, small amounts of exercise-induced gastrointestinal blood loss, sweat, and, in women, menstruation on top of all of it.

The test panel, not the guess

Three blood markers, read together, tell the real story. Ferritin reflects iron stores — the warehouse, not the shop floor — and is the first thing to fall as deficiency develops. Transferrin saturation reflects how much iron is actually available in circulation right now. Haemoglobin is the functional endpoint: anaemia is what happens only once the first two have been low for a while. The complication is that ferritin is also an acute-phase protein — it rises with inflammation, and hard training itself is mildly inflammatory — so a low-ish ferritin after a heavy block is more meaningful than a borderline one taken the morning after a hard race, and a C-reactive protein (CRP) measurement alongside the panel helps tell the two apart.

Athletes can be short of iron at three depths, and the depth changes what is needed. Depleted stores with normal haemoglobin — low ferritin, everything else normal — may already blunt training adaptation and is worth correcting. Non-anaemic iron deficiency adds a low transferrin saturation. Iron-deficiency anaemia adds low haemoglobin and is a different order of problem, with fatigue, breathlessness and a measurable drop in aerobic capacity. All three need the same panel to diagnose and the same clinical oversight to treat — the difference between them is not visible from how tired you feel.

Myth vs fact
“My performance has plateaued and I feel tired all the time — I must be low in iron, I'll start taking a supplement”

Iron is the first thing athletes reach for because it's the nutrient most associated with endurance fatigue.

Fatigue has many causes; only a test tells you which one is yours

Low energy availability, poor sleep, overreaching, anaemia from other causes, thyroid dysfunction and depression can all produce the same symptoms. Supplementing iron you don't need does not fix any of those, and chronic iron overload causes its own damage — oxidative stress, and in people with an undiagnosed predisposition such as haemochromatosis, organ damage. Test first.

The absorption window hepcidin controls

Hepcidin is the master regulator of iron absorption: made by the liver, it shuts the intestinal door that lets dietary iron through. Prolonged or intense exercise raises interleukin-6, and interleukin-6 drives hepcidin up, peaking roughly 3–6 hours after a hard session and settling back toward baseline by about 24 hours B. Take an iron dose into that window and a meaningful fraction of it is wasted at the gut wall before it ever reaches the blood.

The same hormone creates a second, less obvious problem. Taking an oral iron dose itself raises hepcidin for roughly 24 hours, which blunts absorption of a second dose given the next day. The practical fix follows directly from the mechanism: alternate-day dosing absorbs iron more efficiently per dose than daily dosing, because it gives hepcidin time to fall back down between doses — the subject of this chapter's paper, below.

Who is actually at elevated risk

Three groups deserve specific attention, not blanket supplementation. Female athletes combine menstrual losses with, on average, lower total energy and iron intake than male athletes of equal training load. Plant-based athletes rely on non-haem iron, which is absorbed at roughly 2–10% compared with 15–35% for the haem iron in meat and fish, and plant diets are also higher in phytates, which further inhibit absorption. Distance runners of either sex carry the added load of foot-strike haemolysis. None of this means every runner, woman or vegan needs an iron supplement — it means these are the groups worth testing proactively rather than waiting for a collapse in performance to prompt it.

Where this goes wrong

Self-supplementing iron without a blood test is the single most common mistake in this chapter, in both directions. Taking iron when you don't need it does not raise performance in an iron-sufficient athlete, risks gastrointestinal side effects, and in rare cases of undiagnosed hereditary haemochromatosis causes genuine organ damage through iron overload. Equally, assuming fatigue is anaemia and never getting tested leaves the real cause — which might be low energy availability (chapter 34), a different nutrient deficiency, or simply too much training — untouched. This is one of the places in this book where the right next step is a GP or sports physician, not a supplement aisle.

Worked example
Elena, 27, trains 70–90 km a week for the marathon, reports flattening performance and heavier legs on easy days over six weeks.
  1. Her GP orders the three-marker panel plus CRP to rule out an inflammation confound.Ferritin 18 µg/L (low) · transferrin saturation 14% (low) · haemoglobin 125 g/L (low-normal) · CRP normal
  2. Pattern read together: depleted stores with early functional impairment, not yet frank anaemia — exactly the stage most likely to be quietly limiting her training adaptation.
  3. Her clinician prescribes oral ferrous sulfate, roughly 60–80 mg elemental iron, on alternate days rather than daily — following the absorption logic above.Dose on Mon/Wed/Fri/Sun, nothing Tue/Thu/Sat
  4. The paper
Stoffel NU, Zeder C, Brittenham GM, Moretti D, Zimmermann MB. Iron absorption from supplements is greater with alternate day than with consecutive day dosing in iron-deficient anemic women. Haematologica 2020;105(5):1232–1239.
Who

Iron-depleted and iron-deficient anaemic women, studied with labelled-iron absorption tests.

What

Compared fractional and total iron absorption from supplemental doses given on consecutive days versus every other day.

Found

An iron dose raises serum hepcidin for roughly 24 hours, which suppresses absorption of a dose given the next day. Giving the same total iron on alternate days instead produced meaningfully higher absorption per dose than daily dosing.

Why it matters: it gave a mechanistic, hepcidin-based reason to abandon the reflexive “take it every day” instruction that still appears on most supplement labels. Limitation: the study measured absorption, not training performance outcomes directly, and was conducted in iron-deficient anaemic women rather than athletes specifically — the exercise-hepcidin interaction layered on top is drawn from separate sports-science work and is itself an active research area.

31.3Vitamin D — Bone, Muscle, Immune Function

Vitamin D is made in skin exposed to UVB light and converted in the liver and kidney to its active form, which then governs calcium absorption in the gut, supports normal muscle contractile function through receptors in muscle tissue, and modulates immune signalling. Deficiency is common in two groups with otherwise excellent training habits: indoor athletes — swimmers, track cyclists, gymnasts — who simply see little direct sun, and athletes training through autumn and winter at higher latitudes, where UVB intensity from October to March is too low for meaningful skin synthesis regardless of time spent outdoors.

Testing measures serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D; as a rough guide, below about 50 nmol/L (20 ng/mL) is widely treated as deficient and 50–75 nmol/L as insufficient, though exact cut-offs vary between guidelines and laboratories C. The evidence for supplementing a genuinely deficient athlete is reasonably solid for bone and muscle outcomes B. The evidence for supplementing an already-sufficient athlete, hoping for extra performance on top, is not — trials in sufficient athletes show no further benefit. This is a correction-of-deficiency nutrient, not a performance-enhancer, and the only way to know which category you are in is the blood test.

31.4Calcium, Bone, And The Energy-Availability Trap

Bone is not inert scaffolding; it remodels continuously, and the raw material for that remodelling is calcium, with vitamin D governing how much of it gets absorbed and vitamin K involved in directing it into bone rather than soft tissue. Dietary calcium requirements for athletes are not dramatically different from the general population — roughly 1000–1200 mg/day covers most adults — and are easily met through dairy, fortified plant milks, tinned fish eaten with bones, and leafy greens.

The interaction that matters more than the raw number is with energy availability — the balance between what you eat and what training and basic bodily function together cost, covered in full in chapter 34. Chronic low energy availability suppresses the reproductive hormones that normally support bone formation, and the result is impaired bone density regardless of how much calcium is on the plate. An athlete can meet every calcium target in this chapter and still develop a bone stress injury if she or he is under-fuelled overall. Calcium and vitamin D are necessary but not sufficient; energy availability is the variable that decides whether the raw materials actually get used.

31.5Magnesium, Zinc, B Vitamins — What's Real And What's Marketing

Magnesium is a cofactor for several hundred enzyme reactions, including ones involved in muscle contraction and relaxation, which is exactly why it is marketed hard against cramp. The evidence for magnesium supplementation preventing exercise-associated muscle cramps in people who are not actually magnesium-deficient is weak D — see the cramp discussion in chapter 32. True dietary magnesium deficiency is uncommon in athletes eating a varied, sufficient-calorie diet; nuts, wholegrains, legumes and leafy greens cover it easily.

Zinc supports immune function, wound healing and is a cofactor in testosterone synthesis. Endurance athletes lose a little extra through sweat and urine, but dietary deficiency is uncommon where meat, shellfish or legumes feature regularly, and high-dose zinc supplementation can impair copper absorption over time — another case where more is not simply better.

B vitamins (B12, folate, B6, thiamine) are genuine cofactors in the pathways that turn food into ATP, which is the entire basis of the “energy vitamin” marketing built around them. Real deficiency risk exists for B12 in athletes eating no animal products at all — it is essentially absent from plant foods — and for folate in very restrictive diets generally. Supplementing B vitamins in someone who already has enough does nothing measurable for energy, performance or fatigue D, no matter how the label reads.

31.6The Antioxidant Paradox

This is the one piece of advice in this chapter that runs directly against supplement-aisle instinct. Exercise generates reactive oxygen species — by-products of high metabolic turnover, discussed more fully in chapter 12 — and for decades those were treated as pure damage to be mopped up with antioxidant supplements. The more complete picture is that a controlled burst of reactive oxygen species is part of the signal that tells the cell to build more mitochondria. It is not noise in the system; it is partly the message.

Trials giving large, isolated doses of vitamin C and vitamin E around training sessions — well above what food alone would provide — have found blunted markers of the redox signalling that drives mitochondrial biogenesis, and in some studies a smaller improvement in aerobic capacity over a training block compared with training without the supplement B for the signalling markers, C for whether this reliably costs real-world performance in every athlete, since not every trial agrees on the size of the functional effect. The practical takeaway is nonetheless clear enough to act on: get antioxidants from food, where they arrive in modest doses alongside fibre, polyphenols and everything else a vegetable or piece of fruit brings with it, and avoid large isolated-nutrient doses of vitamin C or E specifically scheduled around training sessions during a block where the whole point is to drive adaptation.

Key idea

The redox signal that makes training work and the oxidative damage antioxidants are sold to prevent are not two different things — they are, to a first approximation, the same molecules doing two different jobs depending on dose and timing. Blunting one with a megadose risks blunting the other.

31.7Sweat Losses — Do They Actually Matter?

Sweat does carry out small amounts of iron, calcium, magnesium and zinc alongside the sodium that dominates chapter 32. For the overwhelming majority of athletes, these losses are trivial relative to total body stores and are comfortably replaced by ordinary food intake B. The exception sits at the extreme end of the distribution — very high sweat-rate individuals doing very large training volumes in consistent heat — and even there, the dominant concern is sodium and fluid balance, not trace minerals. If fatigue, plateauing performance or recurring minor illness appear, a blood panel is a far more informative next step than trying to measure or replace what sweat has taken out directly.

Activate it

The practical sequence for any athlete wondering whether a micronutrient is the missing piece.

  • Eat Build meals around the food-atlas table above before considering any supplement — haem iron with vegetables, dairy or fortified alternatives daily, oily fish weekly.
  • Test If you're in a risk group — female endurance athlete, plant-based, distance runner, indoor or high-latitude winter training — ask for the three-marker iron panel (plus CRP) and a 25-hydroxyvitamin D level before symptoms force the issue.
  • Dose Only on a confirmed, clinician-guided deficiency: iron on alternate days, vitamin D per blood level, neither as a daily habit “just in case.”
  • Timing Take iron fasted, away from training-hard-session hepcidin spikes, away from tea/coffee/calcium, with a vitamin-C source.
  • On the bike Micronutrient status is set by diet, not by a session — but indoor riding is where the losses are largest. Sweat rates of 1.5–2.5 L/h are routine on a trainer, so if most of your volume is indoors, pay closer attention to sodium and to iron status (see chapter 32).
  • Signal You're on the right track when a repeat blood test at 8–12 weeks shows the number moving, not when you simply feel different — feeling different is too easy to talk yourself into.

Five things to carry forward

  1. Training genuinely raises the turnover of several micronutrients, but that almost never justifies a broad-spectrum multivitamin — it justifies finding the one or two nutrients actually running short and fixing those specifically.
  2. Iron is the nutrient that matters most for endurance athletes: diagnose with ferritin, transferrin saturation and haemoglobin together, never with how tired you feel.
  3. Exercise and iron supplementation both raise hepcidin for roughly 24 hours and block further absorption, which is why alternate-day dosing works better than daily dosing and why timing iron away from hard sessions matters.
  4. Vitamin D and calcium matter for bone and muscle, but vitamin D only helps when correcting a real deficiency, and calcium cannot protect bone on its own if energy availability is chronically low.
  5. High-dose isolated antioxidant supplements around training sessions can blunt the very redox signalling that builds mitochondria — get antioxidants from food, and never self-supplement iron without a blood test and a clinician.
