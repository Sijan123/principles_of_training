Indoor Cycling — The Workout Library

Every turbo session, what it changes, and the plan it belongs in

A turbo trainer is the most honest piece of training equipment ever built. On the road you coast, you brake, you get stopped at junctions, you sit up for a descent; your "two-hour ride" contains maybe eighty minutes of actual work. Indoors there is nowhere to hide. Every second is the intensity you chose, every session is repeatable to the watt, and the only variable left is you. That control is exactly what makes the trainer the best laboratory an amateur athlete can own — and exactly why it is so easy to ruin yourself on it.

44.1What the Trainer Gives You, and What It Takes Away

Indoor cycling is not a worse version of outdoor cycling. It is a different tool with a different profile, and knowing the trade is the whole game.

What it gives

  • No coasting. On a hilly road ride, 20–35% of elapsed time is often zero-power. Indoors, 60 minutes of riding is 60 minutes of pedalling, which is why a 60-minute turbo session can equal a 90-minute road ride in training stress.
  • Exact repeatability. Same resistance, same cadence, same duration, every time. If you are 8 watts better in six weeks, that is a real 8 watts, not a tailwind.
  • Interval precision. You cannot hold 110% of threshold accurately on a road with corners and traffic lights. You can indoors, to the second.
  • Safety and availability. No cars, no ice, no darkness, no excuse.
  • Zero impact. The reason it is the default engine-builder for injured runners — see chapter 42.

What it takes

  • Cooling. No airflow means core temperature climbs, heart rate drifts upward at the same power, and sweat rates can double. A serious fan is not an accessory; it is part of the equipment.
  • Terrain variation. Constant cadence, constant position, constant muscle recruitment. Over months this is a real monotony cost for the tissues.
  • Bike-handling skill. None of it is trained indoors.
  • Psychological tolerance. Boredom is a legitimate limiter. The best session is the one you will actually complete.
  • Bone loading. Cycling is almost non-osteogenic. Covered in §44.7 — this matters more than most cyclists realise.
In plain terms

Think of outdoor riding as cooking by taste and indoor riding as cooking by scales.

Both make dinner. But if you are trying to work out whether a recipe is actually improving, you need the scales. The road gives you a hundred uncontrolled variables — wind, gradient, traffic, who you rode with — and any one of them can hide a 5% change in your fitness. The trainer removes all of them, so a 5% change shows up as a 5% change.

The catch is the one every cook knows: weighing everything precisely does not tell you whether the recipe was any good. You still have to pick the right session. The trainer makes you accurate, not wise.

44.2Before the First Session: Setting the Numbers Up

Every workout in this chapter is written as a percentage of one number, so that number has to mean something.

FTP — functional threshold power
The highest power you could hold in a quasi-steady state for roughly an hour. The common estimate is a 20-minute maximal effort × 0.95. It is a convenient approximation, not a physiological constant.
CP and W′ — critical power and anaerobic work capacity
CP is the asymptote of the power–duration curve: the boundary above which fatigue becomes inexorable. W′ is the finite, rechargeable "battery" of work available above CP, measured in kilojoules; trained amateurs typically sit between 15 and 25 kJ. You get both from a 3-minute all-out test (CP ≈ mean power of the final 30 seconds; W′ ≈ total work done above it) or from a 3-minute and 12-minute pair. CP is better grounded physiologically than FTP; FTP is more widely supported by software. See chapter 35.
ERG mode vs slope mode
In ERG mode the trainer holds your target power regardless of cadence — useful for steady intervals, but it will also drag you through a session you should have abandoned, and it punishes you if your cadence drops. In slope mode the trainer simulates a gradient and you control power with gears and cadence, which is more like riding and better for anything requiring surges.

Set your zones

Interactive
Heart-rate zones are included here for context, but for everything in this chapter use power, not heart rate. Heart rate lags by 30–60 seconds, drifts upward with core temperature, and is therefore useless for intervals shorter than about three minutes — precisely the sessions the trainer is best at.
The three things that ruin indoor sessions
  • No fan. Without airflow, core temperature rises, heart rate climbs 10–20 beats at the same power, and the session feels — and becomes — harder than prescribed. If you are using heart rate at all indoors, an uncooled room makes the numbers meaningless. Use a proper floor fan at chest height, and a second one if you can.
  • Under-drinking and under-fuelling. Indoor sweat rates of 1.5–2.5 L/h are routine; measure yours with the calculator in chapter 32. And because indoor sessions are so dense, a 90-minute turbo session genuinely needs fuelling where a 90-minute road ride with coasting might not.
  • An FTP that is a fantasy. If minute 18 of a 2 × 20 is a fight, your FTP is set too high, and every "threshold" session you do is really a VO2max session you cannot recover from. Being honest about this number is the highest-value thing in this chapter.

Indoor cycling: the complete workout library

Interactive
Set your own FTP with the slider and every profile converts to watts. Each workout shows its power profile, time-in-zone breakdown, approximate training stress, the adaptation it targets and when to use it. The lower panel places them into six four-to-six week plans — click any session in a plan to jump to its profile.

A few notes the widget cannot easily say:

  • Sweet spot is the trainer's signature session. At 88–93% of FTP you are high enough to drive threshold adaptations but low enough to accumulate 36–60 minutes of work and ride again tomorrow. For a time-constrained rider this ratio of adaptation to fatigue is unbeaten, and it is far easier to hold indoors than out.
  • Over-unders are the most specific session in the library. The "overs" accumulate lactate and hydrogen ions; the "unders" force you to clear them while still working hard. That is precisely the demand of a steep pitch mid-race or a hard attack you have to absorb.
  • Low-cadence torque work is strength-adjacent, not strength training. At 50–60 rpm each pedal stroke needs roughly double the force for the same power, which recruits higher-threshold motor units while breathing stays moderate. It is a useful stimulus; it is not a substitute for a squat, and it should be skipped by anyone with patellofemoral or lateral knee pain.
  • The 20-minute test is a measurement, not a workout. Its value is that it is the same flawed protocol each time, so changes are real. Same trainer, same fan, same time of day, rested and fed.
The paper
Seiler S, Jøranson K, Olesen BV, Hetlelid KJ. Adaptations to aerobic interval training: interactive effects of exercise intensity and total work duration. Scandinavian Journal of Medicine & Science in Sports 2013;23(1):74–83.
Who

35 trained recreational cyclists (29 male, 6 female; VO2peak 52 ± 6 mL/kg/min), already training ~6 h/week.

What

Seven weeks. Three groups did two interval sessions a week — 4 × 4 min, 4 × 8 min, or 4 × 16 min — plus low-intensity riding. A fourth group did low intensity only. The sessions were performed at 94%, 90% and 88% of peak heart rate, with blood lactate of 13.2, 9.6 and 4.9 mmol/L respectively.

Found

4 × 8 min produced the largest average gains — roughly a 10% rise in VO2peak and about 16% more power at 4 mmol/L lactate. Not the hardest protocol, and not the longest.

Why it matters: it is the cleanest demonstration that interval adaptation is a product of intensity and accumulated duration, and that the optimum is an interior point. The hardest session is not the best session; 4 × 4 was more intense and 4 × 16 accumulated more work, and the middle option beat both. Limitation: one population, one duration, nine riders per group, and group averages conceal individual variation — some riders will respond better to 4 × 4. Treat it as a strong prior, not a prescription.

Worked example
Tom, 41, 78 kg, rides 5–6 h/week mostly indoors. Last 20-minute test: 262 W. He has a hilly 100 km sportive in ten weeks and wants his threshold higher.
  1. Convert the 20-minute test to FTP.262 × 0.95 = 249 W → round to 250 W
  2. Relative power, which is what decides climbing.250 / 78 = 3.21 W/kg
  3. Sweet-spot target, 88–93% of FTP.250 × 0.88 = 220 W  to  250 × 0.93 = 233 W
  4. Threshold target for 2 × 20.250 × 1.00 = 250 W
  5. VO₂max target for 4 × 8 min, ~106% FTP.250 × 1.06 = 265 W
  6. Six weeks on the FTP plan, progressing sweet spot by duration not power.3×12 min → 3×15 → 3×18 → 2×25 → 1×40
  7. Then a three-week VO₂max block, then a one-week taper into the event.6 wk FTP + 3 wk VO₂max + 1 wk taper = 10 wk
  8. Expected gain from a solid base with this structure, from the dose–response literature.250 W × 1.05 to 1.12 = 263–280 W
So: Tom's plan is six weeks of sweet spot progressed by duration, three weeks of 4 × 8 min, one week of taper — and a realistic target of 265–280 W FTP (3.4–3.6 W/kg) on event day. Note what he is not doing: no sprints, no anaerobic work, no Tabatas. None of them raise a hilly-sportive threshold, and all of them would cost him sweet-spot sessions.

44.5Choosing Between the Plans

The six plans in the widget are not interchangeable. The commonest mistake in indoor training is running a build plan all year because build plans feel productive.

Activate it

The highest-yield indoor block for most time-limited riders: raising sustainable power, progressed by duration rather than by watts.

  • Do Sweet spot: 88–93% of FTP, seated, cadence 85–95 rpm, in slope mode or ERG.
  • Dose Week 1–2: 3 × 12 min, 4 min easy. Week 3–4: 3 × 15 min. Week 5: 2 × 25 min. Week 6: 1 × 40 min.
  • When Twice a week, with one threshold session (2 × 20 at 100% FTP) and two zone-2 rides around it.
  • On the bike This is the bike session — but the equivalent off it is a 40–50 min continuous tempo run at roughly 80–85% of maximum heart rate, or 3 × 10 min at threshold pace for a runner.
  • Progress Add minutes, not watts. Re-test after six weeks and only then raise the power target.
  • Don't Add sprints or anaerobic work in this block. They will not raise your threshold and they will cost you a sweet-spot session.
  • Signal You're doing it right when the same sweet-spot session, at the same watts, costs you 3–5 fewer heartbeats than it did a fortnight ago — and when the last interval no longer feels different from the first.
B The specific sweet-spot progression is a coaching convention supported by the general interval dose–response literature rather than by trials of this exact protocol; the underlying principle — that accumulated time near threshold raises threshold — is well supported A.

44.6Six Ways People Waste Indoor Training

  1. Every ride becomes tempo

    Indoors there is no reason to go easy, so people don't. Three or four rides a week all landing at 75–85% of FTP produces a rider who is permanently slightly tired and never actually improving. If a ride is meant to be easy, make it genuinely easy — and if that feels pointless, see chapter 14 on why it isn't.

  2. Chasing training stress instead of adaptation

    A stress score is a bookkeeping device, not a goal. Two sessions with identical scores can drive completely different adaptations, and the number cannot tell you which one you needed.

  3. Letting ERG mode finish a session you should have abandoned

    ERG mode will hold the watts while your form collapses and your cadence falls to 60. There is no adaptive prize for completing a VO2max interval at 65 rpm with your shoulders rocking. If power quality is gone, the session is over.

  4. Testing too often

    A 20-minute test is a hard session that produces no adaptation. Every six to eight weeks is plenty.

  5. Doing VO2max work year-round

    It is a block stimulus, not a staple. Three to four weeks, then return to sweet spot, or you will simply accumulate fatigue and call it a plateau.

  6. Pacing interval one off interval one

    Set the target from what you can complete on the last rep. A set of five that reads 290-275-260-250-240 was one good interval and four compromised ones; 265 across the board is a better session in every way that matters.

Myth vs fact
“Indoor riding doesn't count — an hour on the turbo isn't a real hour”

So indoor volume should be scaled up to match outdoor volume.

It is the other way round

Because there is no coasting, no braking and no freewheeling, an indoor hour usually contains more pedalling and more training stress than an outdoor hour at the same nominal intensity. A reasonable rule of thumb is that 60 indoor minutes approximates 75–90 outdoor minutes of comparable work — which is why riders who transplant their full outdoor volume onto a trainer so often end up overcooked in three weeks.

44.7For Runners, Lifters and the Injured — and One Warning About Bone

Cycling's great virtue as cross-training is that it is concentric-dominant and impact-free. There is almost no eccentric muscle damage, which is why you can do a hard bike session and lift the next day, and why the measured interference between cycling and strength training is consistently smaller than between running and strength training (chapter 20).

That makes the trainer the right tool in four situations:

  • Building an engine while lifting for size. Cycling and rowing cost your hypertrophy less than running does.
  • Maintaining fitness through a running injury. Zone 2 and sweet spot, daily if pain-free. Runners who use the bike properly return with their aerobic fitness essentially intact.
  • Adding aerobic volume an aging or heavy runner's joints will not tolerate. The bike buys you hours that running cannot.
  • Heat acclimation. A warm room, reduced airflow, 60–90 minutes of zone 2 for five to ten days is the easiest heat-adaptation protocol available to an amateur — see chapter 37.
The bone problem, stated plainly

Cycling is non-weight-bearing and almost non-osteogenic. Bone responds specifically to high-magnitude, high-rate loading — impacts above roughly 4 g, of which cycling provides essentially none. Competitive cyclists have repeatedly been found to have lower bone mineral density than other athletes and sometimes than sedentary controls, and the risk compounds when low energy availability is also present.

If cycling is your main or only training, you must add the bone stimulus deliberately:

  • Impact: 50–60 landings above about 4 g on most days — skipping, pogo hops, low jumps, or simply some running.
  • Resistance training: twice a week, heavy, multi-joint, loading the hip and spine — squats, deadlifts, step-ups.
  • Enough food: you cannot build bone in an energy deficit. See chapter 34.

This is not an optional extra for cyclists. It is the single biggest long-term health cost of the sport, and it is entirely preventable.

Five things to carry forward

  1. The trainer's value is control, not intensity: no coasting and exact repeatability mean an indoor hour carries more training stress than an outdoor one and that real changes in fitness actually show up as changes in the numbers.
  2. Sweet spot — 88–93% of FTP, progressed by adding minutes rather than watts — is the highest adaptation-to-fatigue ratio available indoors, and the best default for a time-limited rider.
  3. The best interval session is an interior optimum, not the hardest one: in a seven-week trial of trained cyclists, 4 × 8 min beat both 4 × 4 and 4 × 16.
  4. Choose the adaptation before the session, and use VO2max and anaerobic work as short blocks rather than staples — year-round hard riding produces fatigue, not fitness.
  5. Cycling builds almost no bone, so any cyclist whose training is mostly indoors must add impact and heavy resistance work deliberately, and eat enough to support it.
