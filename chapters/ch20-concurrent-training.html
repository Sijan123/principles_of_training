Concurrent Training

Can you build an engine and a chassis at once?

In 1980, exercise scientist Robert Hickson took a group of healthy adults, had them lift heavy and run hard in the same programme for ten weeks, and found something that should not have surprised anyone but did: their legs got weaker even as their aerobic engine improved. He called it the interference effect, and the question it opened — can you build an engine and a chassis at the same time, or does training one quietly tax the other — has occupied exercise physiology for over four decades. The honest answer, four decades on, is smaller and more manageable than the alarm around it ever suggested.

20.1The Question, And Where It Came From

Hickson's original 1980 study is the reason "concurrent training" is a named problem rather than just a thing people do. He split participants into a strength-only group, an endurance-only group, and a combined group training both in the same ten-week block, and tracked leg strength and aerobic capacity throughout. The endurance-only and strength-only groups each progressed steadily on their own measure, as expected. The combined group's aerobic capacity rose in line with the endurance-only group — concurrent training did not cost them anything there — but their leg strength, after an initial rise alongside the strength-only group, plateaued around week seven and then declined for the remainder of the study, despite their continuing to train it.

That asymmetry — endurance holds up fine, strength and power are the ones that suffer — turned out to be the single most durable finding in this entire area, repeated in study after study since. It immediately raised the obvious next question: what, mechanically, makes adding a run cost a squat, when adding a squat does not seem to cost a run?

20.2The Proposed Molecular Mechanism

A cell cannot build new muscle protein and desperately conserve energy at the same time without one signal winning, and the two training modes send genuinely opposite instructions to the same machinery. Endurance exercise — especially when it is prolonged, glycogen-depleting or otherwise metabolically demanding — activates an energy-sensing enzyme called AMPK (AMP-activated protein kinase), the cell's low-battery warning light, which switches on when the ratio of spent to available energy (AMP to ATP) rises. AMPK's job once active is to shut down anything expensive and non-essential, and mTORC1 — the master growth switch from chapter 17 that drives muscle protein synthesis after resistance training — is exactly that kind of expensive, non-essential process from the cell's point of view during an energy crunch. AMPK suppresses mTORC1 partly by activating a protein called TSC2, which sits upstream of mTORC1 and restrains it; switch on enough AMPK and TSC2 puts the brakes on mTORC1 regardless of how much mechanical tension a subsequent resistance session produces.

The timing is what makes this a scheduling problem rather than a training-choice problem. AMPK activity rises quickly after an endurance bout and falls back toward its resting baseline within roughly three hours. mTORC1 activity after a resistance session, by contrast, rises more slowly and stays elevated for considerably longer — on the order of 18–24 hours. A hard endurance session performed shortly before a resistance session arrives while AMPK is still switched on and able to blunt the mTORC1 response the lifting is trying to produce; performed well clear of it, AMPK has already returned to baseline and the two signals barely interact.

In plain terms

Picture two building crews who share one supervisor. One crew is the demolition crew, called in whenever the site needs stripping down fast and cheaply to deal with an emergency. The other is the construction crew, building something new and expensive that takes all day to get going properly.

If the demolition crew is called in while the construction crew is mid-pour, the supervisor pulls resources toward the emergency and the pour suffers. Call demolition in hours before the pour starts, and it has already finished and gone home by the time construction gets going — no conflict at all. AMPK is the demolition supervisor, triggered by an endurance session; mTORC1 is the construction job a lifting session starts. Keep them apart in time and they barely notice each other.

20.3What The Evidence Actually Shows

The molecular story is tidy. The whole-body training evidence is messier, and considerably less alarming than the mechanism alone would suggest. The most-cited synthesis remains Wilson and colleagues' 2012 meta-analysis, pooling 21 studies and 422 effect sizes comparing concurrent training against resistance training alone.

The paper
Wilson JM, Marin PJ, Rhea MR, Wilson SMC, Loenneke JP, Anderson JC. Concurrent training: a meta-analysis examining interference of aerobic and resistance exercises. J Strength Cond Res 2012;26(8):2293–2307.
Who

21 studies, 422 effect sizes, pooling concurrent-training trials against resistance-training-only comparisons.

What

Meta-analysis and correlational analysis of how endurance-training frequency, duration and modality relate to strength, power and hypertrophy outcomes when combined with resistance training.

Found

A small-to-moderate impairment of lower-body strength and a moderate impairment of power, with hypertrophy largely unaffected. Endurance-training frequency and duration both correlated negatively with the size of strength and power gains — more endurance volume, more interference.

Why it matters: it is the number most coaches still quote, and it confirms Hickson's asymmetry at scale — strength and power are vulnerable, hypertrophy is comparatively robust. Limitation: it pools studies that differ hugely in training status, modality and session spacing, so the pooled effect size is a useful average, not a precise prediction for any one programme; more recent, more tightly controlled work has found the effect to be smaller, or absent, in some well-managed concurrent schedules.

That last point deserves its own sentence, because the literature has moved since 2012. More recent, better-controlled trials, including a 2024 systematic review and meta-analysis examining training status as a moderator, have found that the interference effect in previously untrained people is often negligible — two novel stimuli both drive adaptation with room to spare — while it remains real, if modest, in already-trained lifters. There is also a growing, legitimate question in the literature about whether the acute molecular signal described above — a real, repeatable, short-term blunting of mTORC1 activity measured in a muscle biopsy — actually adds up to the long-term strength and power differences measured over months of training, or whether those differences are better explained by the much more mundane moderators covered next: accumulated fatigue, reduced recovery, and simply less energy and attention available for the resistance session. C The acute biopsy data are solid; the straight line from acute signal to chronic outcome is less so.

20.4The Moderators That Do Matter

Whatever the exact molecular contribution, four variables consistently predict how much interference shows up in practice, and all four are things a programme can actually control.

What makes concurrent-training interference worse — and what makes it smaller
ModeratorWorse interferenceSmaller interference
ModalityRunning — its eccentric, impact-heavy stride damages muscle fibres directly, on top of any molecular signalCycling or rowing — concentric-dominant, far less muscle damage per unit of aerobic stress
ProximityEndurance and resistance sessions back-to-back, same day, short gapSessions separated by hours, or trained on different days entirely
Endurance volume & intensityHigh weekly volume, frequent high-intensity or glycogen-depleting sessions — the strongest AMPK triggersLower weekly volume, more sessions kept at an easy, low-AMPK intensity
RecoveryInadequate sleep and energy intake on top of a high combined training loadRecovery resourced to match the combined, not just the single-discipline, load

The modality finding is worth dwelling on, because it is the single easiest lever to pull. Running's interference problem is not mainly molecular; a hard run leaves the quadriceps and hamstrings with genuine microscopic damage from the repeated eccentric braking of landing, on top of whatever AMPK is doing. A cyclist's legs experience nothing comparable from an equally hard ride, because pedalling has nowhere near the eccentric loading of a running stride. This is why, swapped for cycling at a matched training stress, the same weekly endurance dose produces measurably less interference with a parallel strength programme — and why an athlete who does not specifically need running (anyone whose sport is cycling, triathlon with a flexible bike/run balance, or simply general fitness) has an easy win available simply by choosing the less damaging aerobic tool more often.

20.5Practical Rules For Sharing A Week

Translating the molecular timeline into a schedule gives two workable numbers: a minimum gap of around 3 hours between an endurance and a resistance session on the same day, and an ideal gap of 6 hours or more, which gives AMPK activity more room to fall back toward baseline before the resistance session's mTORC1 response begins. Where same-day training is unavoidable, 3 hours is a usable floor, not a guarantee of zero interference. C

Ordering within that gap carries an honest trade-off that is worth stating plainly rather than glossing over. Training endurance first and resistance second protects the molecular picture best, because the AMPK spike from the endurance session has longer to resolve before the lifting session begins. But it also means lifting on legs that are already somewhat fatigued, which costs bar speed, working load and set quality on the resistance session itself — a different, more immediate cost than the molecular one, and for some lifters the larger of the two in practice. Training resistance first avoids that fatigue cost but places the endurance session closer to the resistance session's long mTORC1 window. Neither order is free; the choice is about which cost a given athlete's priorities can better absorb, not about avoiding cost altogether.

Worked example
Jade, 29, training for an Olympic-distance triathlon while trying to keep the heavy squatting and deadlifting she has built over two years of lifting.

  1. Identify the collision: Thursday stacks the week's hardest, most AMPK-triggering endurance session directly before the week's heaviest lift, with only a 2-hour gap — below even the 3-hour floor.2 h gap < 3 h minimum
  2. Note the compounding problem: the run session chosen is interval running, the single worst combination of modality (eccentric-damaging) and intensity (strongly AMPK-activating) available in her week.
  3. Fix it with the cheapest available change first: move the Thursday lift to the morning, before the interval run, or swap it to a different day entirely with 24 h or more of separation — either removes the same-day proximity problem outright.
  4. If the schedule genuinely cannot move, substitute a cycling-based interval session for the running one on lift days, trading some running-specific adaptation for a large cut in interference, and keep interval running for a day with no lifting nearby.
So: Jade's week was not generally overloaded — it had one specific, fixable collision on Thursday. Separating that single pairing by a day, or swapping the modality on that one day, removes almost all of the interference risk without cutting a single hour of total training.

20.6The Hybrid Athlete — What's Actually Achievable

Done with reasonable scheduling, a surprising amount is genuinely trainable at once. Aerobic capacity, muscle hypertrophy, and moderate relative strength can all be built in parallel across most of a training year without one meaningfully blunting another, provided the moderators above are respected and total volume across both disciplines stays within what the athlete can actually recover from — a point chapter 36 covers in more depth.

What does not combine as cleanly, and is better sequenced into separate blocks rather than chased simultaneously, is anything sitting at the far ends of either quality: maximal strength or peak power expression on one side, and high-volume, high-intensity endurance work (the kind that specifically drives mitochondrial and capillary adaptation at its ceiling) on the other. An athlete chasing a new 1RM squat and a new 10 km personal best in the same eight-week block is asking both qualities to peak at once, which is a much harder ask than maintaining each while the other leads. The practical pattern used by most successful hybrid programmes is a rotating emphasis — a block that leads with strength while endurance is held at a maintenance dose, followed by a block that leads with endurance while strength is held at a maintenance dose — rather than maximal effort on both fronts simultaneously, every week, indefinitely.

20.7The Real Limiter Is Usually Energy And Sleep, Not AMPK

It is tempting, given how much of this chapter is built on a named enzyme and a named signalling pathway, to treat AMPK as the villain of concurrent training. In most real athletes training a realistic combined volume, it is not. The far more common cause of a hybrid programme stalling is simpler and less interesting: the athlete is eating enough to fuel one discipline well, not two disciplines well at once, and sleeping on a schedule built for a single-sport training load. Both resistance and endurance adaptation are expensive, and a calorie or protein deficit, or a sleep debt, suppresses the recovery and synthetic processes behind both kinds of training simultaneously — indistinguishable, from the outside, from molecular interference, but fixed with food and sleep rather than a stopwatch and a session order.

Activate it

A weekly structure that respects the scheduling evidence above for a lifter who also wants a meaningful aerobic base.

  • Do 2–3 resistance sessions and 3–5 aerobic sessions, with no more than one hard session of each per day.
  • Gap Minimum 3 hours, ideally 6+ hours, between an endurance and a resistance session on the same day.
  • Order Endurance first if protecting the molecular signal matters most (e.g. a strength peak is weeks away); resistance first if bar quality and load on that day matter more.
  • Modality Default hard intervals to the bike or rower rather than running whenever the sport allows it, to cut eccentric muscle damage stacking onto a lifting week.
  • Fuel Eat to support the combined weekly load, not either discipline alone — under-fuelling is a more common cause of stalled progress than session order.
  • On the bike Use the bike as the default hard-interval tool on lifting days — e.g. 5–6 × 3 min at 90–95% FTP, 3 min easy — delivering the same aerobic stimulus as an equivalent run with far less muscle damage carried into the gym.
  • Signal You're doing it right when squat and deadlift numbers hold flat or creep up across a block that also includes a rising aerobic volume; a steady decline in bar weight at unchanged reps is the sign to widen the gap, change the modality, or check sleep and food before blaming the programme.

Five things to carry forward

  1. Hickson's 1980 study established the interference effect: combined training protects aerobic gains almost completely but can blunt strength, which plateaued and then fell in his concurrent group.
  2. The proposed mechanism is AMPK, switched on by endurance exercise, suppressing the growth pathway mTORC1 via TSC2; AMPK fades back to baseline in about 3 hours while mTORC1 stays elevated for 18–24 hours, making timing the crux of the problem.
  3. Wilson et al.'s 2012 meta-analysis of 21 studies found a small-to-moderate impairment of lower-body strength and power with hypertrophy largely unaffected, and newer work questions how much of that long-term effect the acute molecular signal actually explains.
  4. Running interferes more than cycling because of eccentric muscle damage on top of any molecular signal, which is why swapping hard intervals from the road to the bike is one of the cheapest fixes available to a hybrid athlete.
  5. In most real programmes the practical limiter is energy intake and sleep, not AMPK — a hybrid athlete who stalls is more often under-fuelled or under-slept than molecularly blocked.
