Inside the Sarcomere

The cross-bridge cycle, and the two curves that govern force

Freeze a muscle fibre at the instant it is producing maximum force and, under an electron microscope, almost nothing is moving. Two sets of protein filaments are overlapping, locked together at thousands of points along their length, each contact point cycling through a tiny mechanical ratchet a few dozen times a second. There is no single "contraction switch." There is a repeating four-step cycle, powered one molecule of fuel at a time, multiplied across billions of contacts simultaneously. Understand that cycle and two curves that fall directly out of it, and you understand why a muscle that moves a huge load in one position can barely move a light one in another, and why it is strongest doing the one thing you'd least expect: resisting being stretched.

4.1The Sarcomere: Muscle's Repeating Unit

A muscle fibre is, among other things, a chain of thousands of identical mechanical units stacked end to end, each about two micrometres long — roughly a fiftieth the width of a human hair. Each unit is a sarcomere, and it is the smallest piece of muscle that can still generate force on its own. Line enough sarcomeres up in series and you get a myofibril; bundle enough myofibrils in parallel, wrapped in the fibre's own membrane, and you get a muscle fibre; bundle enough fibres, as chapter 3 described, and you get a muscle.

Inside a sarcomere there are two kinds of protein filament. The thick filament is made of myosin, a molecule shaped like a long golf club with a globular head that juts out sideways at intervals along its length — that head is the business end, the part that physically grabs and pulls. The thin filament is made mainly of actin, strung like a twisted double chain of beads, with two regulatory proteins, troponin and tropomyosin, wound along it controlling whether myosin is allowed to grab on. Thick and thin filaments interleave, each thick filament surrounded by six thin ones, like pencils packed into a hexagonal bundle.

The boundary of each sarcomere is a dense protein disc called the Z-disc, which anchors the thin filaments at both ends. Under a microscope the overlap pattern creates visible bands, and the names of those bands are used constantly in muscle physiology, so they are worth fixing once: the A band is the dark zone spanning the full length of the thick filaments; the I band is the lighter zone either side, where only thin filaments are present; the H zone is a narrower pale strip in the very centre of the A band, where thick filaments exist but no thin filament reaches; and the M-line is a thin structural line running through the dead centre of the H zone, cross-linking neighbouring thick filaments and keeping the whole lattice aligned. One more protein ties the structure together mechanically rather than contractively: titin, a single giant molecule that runs from the Z-disc to the M-line, acting as a molecular spring that keeps the thick filament centred and provides passive tension when the sarcomere is stretched beyond the point where actin and myosin overlap at all.

This picture is the basis of the sliding-filament theory, established independently by Andrew Huxley and Hugh Huxley's research groups in 1954: the filaments themselves do not shorten. A sarcomere shortens because the thin filaments are pulled further in between the thick ones, so the Z-discs are drawn closer together while the A band, the thick filament's own fixed length, stays exactly the same width throughout. Everything this chapter covers happens at, or because of, this sliding.

4.2From Decision to Calcium: Excitation–Contraction Coupling

A sarcomere cannot decide to contract on its own; it waits for a signal, and getting that signal from a decision in the brain to a calcium ion inside a specific fibre is a five-step relay called excitation–contraction coupling.

  1. Action potential arrives

    An electrical impulse, generated in the motor cortex and carried down the spinal cord and the alpha motor neuron (see chapter 6), reaches the neuromuscular junction and triggers an identical electrical impulse in the muscle fibre's own membrane.

  2. The impulse dives inward via the T-tubules

    The fibre's surface membrane folds inward at regular intervals to form tunnels called T-tubules, which carry the electrical signal deep into the fibre's interior so that every sarcomere, not just those near the surface, receives it at essentially the same instant.

  3. The sarcoplasmic reticulum releases calcium

    Wrapped around each myofibril is a calcium store called the sarcoplasmic reticulum (SR). The T-tubule signal triggers specialised channels in the SR membrane to open, and calcium, held at a far higher concentration inside the SR than in the surrounding fluid, floods out into the space around the filaments.

  4. Calcium binds troponin, and tropomyosin moves

    Calcium binds to troponin, which changes shape and physically drags the tropomyosin strand sideways, off the groove on actin where it had been sitting and blocking access.

  5. Binding sites are exposed

    With tropomyosin out of the way, the sites on actin that a myosin head can grab are now physically exposed. The fibre is now mechanically ready to contract — but whether it actually shortens depends on what happens next: the cross-bridge cycle.

In plain terms

Think of tropomyosin as a security barrier laid along a loading dock, and troponin as the gatekeeper holding the lever.

Normally the barrier sits across the dock and nothing can be loaded. A calcium ion arriving is like a key turning in the gatekeeper's lock: the gatekeeper (troponin) swings the barrier (tropomyosin) aside, and now the dock (the actin binding site) is open for business. No calcium, no key, no open dock — no matter how urgently the nervous system wants the muscle to contract.

4.3The Cross-Bridge Cycle: Four Steps, One Fuel

With binding sites exposed, the actual force-producing machinery is a repeating four-step cycle run independently by each myosin head, thousands of times per second across a whole fibre. This is the cross-bridge cycle, and ATP (adenosine triphosphate, the cell's universal energy currency) has an exact, specific job at two different points in it — not as a vague "fuel" but as a molecular switch.

  1. 1 · Attachment

    A myosin head, already carrying a spent ATP molecule as ADP plus a phosphate group, binds to the now-exposed site on actin, forming a cross-bridge.

  2. 2 · Power stroke

    Releasing the phosphate group causes the myosin head to pivot sharply — the power stroke — dragging the thin filament a few nanometres past the thick filament. ADP is released at the end of this stroke.

  3. 3 · Detachment

    A fresh ATP molecule binds to the myosin head. This is the step that makes the head let go of actin — ATP's role here is not to power the pull, but to force the release.

  4. 4 · Re-cocking

    Myosin splits that ATP into ADP and phosphate, and the energy released re-cocks the head back into its high-energy, ready-to-attach position, poised to repeat the cycle for as long as calcium and ATP are both available.

Key idea

ATP does two different jobs in this cycle and it is easy to swap them in your head. Splitting ATP re-cocks the head (step 4) — it loads the spring. Binding a new ATP is what makes an attached head let go of actin (step 3) — it releases the ratchet. This second fact is the one that explains rigor mortis: after death, cells stop producing ATP, myosin heads that are already attached have no fresh ATP to bind, and so they cannot detach. Actin and myosin lock together permanently, and the muscle goes rigid — direct, visible proof that ATP's job is to release the cross-bridge, not merely to "power" it.

Multiply this cycle across the roughly 300 myosin heads on a single thick filament, across the hundreds of thick filaments in one sarcomere, across the thousands of sarcomeres in series along one myofibril, across the hundreds of myofibrils in one fibre, and the aggregate force of enormous numbers of tiny, repeating, asynchronous pulls is what you feel as a smooth muscular contraction.

Worked example
Priya, 70 kg, performs a heavy set of 10 back-squat reps, roughly 2 s down and 2 s up each rep — 40 s of continuously loaded muscle.
  1. Active muscle mass driving a heavy squat — quadriceps, glutes, hamstrings, calves, combined — is roughly 8–12 kg of wet tissue in a lifter this size.Take 10 kg wet muscle
  2. Skeletal muscle is about 75% water, so convert to dry mass, which is what turnover rates are usually reported against.10 kg × 0.25 = 2.5 kg dry muscle
  3. Muscle biopsy studies during hard, near-maximal contraction report ATP turnover of roughly 4–15 mmol ATP per kg dry muscle per second C — take a mid-range figure for a heavy but sub-maximal squat.≈ 8 mmol · kg⁻¹ · s⁻¹
  4. Multiply rate × dry mass × time under tension across all 10 reps.8 × 2.5 × 40 = 800 mmol ATP
  5. Convert to mass, using ATP's molar mass of roughly 507 g/mol.0.8 mol × 507 g/mol ≈ 406 g of ATP turned over
  6. Compare that with how much ATP is actually sitting in the muscle at rest — roughly 5–8 mmol per kg wet muscle.10 kg × 6 mmol/kg ≈ 60 mmol ≈ 30 g stored
So: the set turns over on the order of 400 g of ATP while the working muscle holds perhaps 30 g at any instant — meaning that stored ATP is recycled roughly a dozen times over during those 40 seconds, entirely through phosphocreatine breakdown and glycolysis regenerating ADP back into ATP as fast as the cross-bridges consume it (the subject of chapter 7). Nothing here is precise — turnover rate varies several-fold between studies and individuals — but the order of magnitude is the point: cross-bridge cycling would exhaust the muscle's entire ATP store in a couple of seconds without continuous, high-rate resynthesis running alongside it.

4.4The Length–Tension Relationship

A muscle's maximum force depends not only on how many cross-bridges are cycling, but on how much actin and myosin overlap is available at a given sarcomere length for cross-bridges to form in the first place — and that overlap changes continuously as a muscle lengthens or shortens. The result, mapped out by stretching and stimulating single muscle fibres at fixed lengths, is the length–tension relationship, and it has three distinct regions.

The three regions of the length–tension curve
RegionSarcomere lengthWhat limits forcePractical example
Ascending limbShortThin filaments from opposite sides overlap each other, interfering with productive cross-bridge formation; force rises as the muscle lengthens toward optimumA biceps curl at full elbow flexion, muscle fully shortened — noticeably weaker than mid-range
PlateauOptimal (≈2.0–2.2 μm)Maximal, near-complete actin–myosin overlap with no interference; the most cross-bridges available at onceA squat or bench press near the joint angle that typically feels strongest
Descending limbLongFilaments are pulled apart far enough that overlap, and so the number of possible cross-bridges, falls directly; titin's passive tension begins contributing more of the total forceA deep Romanian deadlift or a Nordic curl at full stretch — muscle lengthened, cross-bridge force falling even as passive tension rises

This relationship is the biophysical reason a given muscle feels unmistakably weaker at some joint angles than others, independent of how motivated you are or how well you have warmed up — it is a direct consequence of filament geometry, the same geometry chapter 3 described in terms of architecture. It is also a central reason exercise selection and range of motion matter for training purpose: an exercise that loads a muscle on its descending limb, at long muscle length, combines a demanding mechanical position with (for most people) a position of relatively lower force capacity, which several studies link to a stronger hypertrophy stimulus per set than training the same muscle only through its stronger, shorter range — a theme returned to properly in chapter 17.

Myth vs fact
“Partial reps near full lockout build real strength”

Lifting a heavier weight through a short range must be more effective because the number is bigger.

Depends entirely on which partial

A partial rep performed near the plateau or short, strong end of the range lets you handle more load, but trains a narrow and already-strong part of the length–tension curve. A partial performed at long muscle length, on the descending limb, loads the muscle exactly where it is both weakest and architecturally most responsive to growth. "Partial reps" is not one training decision; it is at least two, with different purposes and different evidence behind each.

4.5The Force–Velocity Relationship

A second curve governs force independently of muscle length: how fast the muscle is shortening or lengthening at the moment force is measured. Each cross-bridge cycle takes a fixed minimum amount of time to complete its four steps. Ask the filaments to slide past each other faster than cross-bridges can attach, pull and detach in sequence, and fewer of them are attached and pulling at any given instant — so force falls as shortening velocity rises. At the other extreme, a muscle contracting with no shortening at all (an isometric contraction, same length throughout) gives cross-bridges unlimited time to attach, and produces more force than any shortening contraction can.

This single mechanical fact — fewer cross-bridges can stay attached at high sliding speed — is the entire explanation for why you cannot be maximally fast and maximally strong in the same instant. A heavy deadlift moves slowly because only a slow contraction keeps enough cross-bridges attached to produce the force the bar demands; a baseball pitch moves the arm explosively because the muscle has deliberately sacrificed most of its force capability for speed. Every point on a force–velocity curve is a different, equally valid trade between the two, and no training method moves a muscle off the curve entirely — it can only reshape how much force is available at each speed, the idea behind the force-velocity widget's athlete-profile comparisons.

Push shortening velocity to zero and beyond — into active lengthening, an eccentric contraction, where the muscle is activated but being forced to lengthen by an external load — and something different happens. Rather than falling further, force rises above the isometric maximum. The mechanism is still debated in detail, but the leading explanation involves titin: during forced lengthening, titin appears to engage and stiffen, bearing load passively in a way that adds to, rather than competes with, active cross-bridge force. In isolated animal muscle fibres under tightly controlled conditions, eccentric force can reach roughly 1.5–1.8 times the isometric maximum. In living humans performing whole-joint movements, the gap is far more modest and inconsistent — commonly in the region of 10–40% above isometric or concentric values, with substantial variation between joints, velocities and individuals, and some people showing little or no eccentric advantage at all C.

Activate it

Using the eccentric side of the curve deliberately — loading a muscle above its concentric maximum, which ordinary training with a single weight never does.

  • Do Accentuated eccentric loading on a main lift — e.g. a slow, resisted lowering phase on the barbell back squat or bench press, assisted or spotted back up.
  • Load 105–120% of concentric 1RM on the lowering phase; a partner, spotter arms or weight-release hooks handle the lighter concentric return.
  • Dose 2–4 sets × 2–4 reps, 3–4 s controlled lowering per rep, 2–3 min rest.
  • When Once weekly on a given lift, as an addition to — not a replacement for — ordinary concentric–eccentric training.
  • Progress Increase the eccentric load fraction or the controlled lowering time before adding reps; soreness runs high early on and should guide how quickly you progress.
  • On the bike A seated downhill or high-resistance low-cadence effort can bias load toward the lengthening phase of the pedal stroke, but it cannot approach the muscle-length and load control of a barbell eccentric, so treat any cycling version as a minor supplement, not an equivalent.
  • Signal You're doing it right when the controlled lowering phase feels like the harder part of the lift, not an afterthought before the "real" concentric rep.
B Accentuated eccentric loading reliably produces greater strength and hypertrophy signalling than matched concentric-only work in trained lifters; delayed-onset soreness is also reliably greater, especially early in a programme.

4.6Putting the Two Curves Together

Length–tension and force–velocity are not two separate facts about a muscle; they operate simultaneously, at every instant, on the same cross-bridges. A muscle's force at any moment is the product of how much actin–myosin overlap its current length allows and how fast it happens to be shortening or lengthening right then. This is why a single muscle can feel powerful in one exercise and weak in another that seems superficially similar: a different joint angle changes sarcomere length and moves you along the length–tension curve; a different bar speed changes cross-bridge attachment time and moves you along the force–velocity curve; most real movements change both at once, continuously, through the range.

It is also why the same muscle produces its most unexpected numbers at the two extremes nobody intuitively predicts: a slow lift through long muscle length near full stretch combines the descending limb's falling cross-bridge overlap with titin's rising passive tension, in ways the two curves alone, read separately, would not obviously predict; and a fast, forcibly lengthening contraction — catching a heavy object, decelerating a sprint stride, landing from a jump — can momentarily demand more force than the same muscle could ever produce by contracting concentrically, which is exactly the loading pattern most associated with muscle damage and delayed soreness after unfamiliar exercise.

The paper
Gordon AM, Huxley AF, Julian FJ. The variation in isometric tension with sarcomere length in vertebrate muscle fibres. J Physiol 1966;184(1):170–92.
Who

Single, isolated muscle fibres from frog skeletal muscle.

What

Fibres were held at carefully controlled, uniform sarcomere lengths using feedback-controlled apparatus, then stimulated to produce isometric tetanic tension, mapping force against length with far more precision than earlier whole-muscle studies allowed.

Found

A clear plateau of maximal tension between roughly 2.0 and 2.2 μm of sarcomere length, a steep ascending limb below it, and a near-linear descending limb above it — matching, almost exactly, the degree of filament overlap predicted by the sliding-filament theory.

Why it matters: this is the experiment that turned the sliding-filament theory from a plausible structural idea into a quantitatively verified mechanism, by showing force tracked filament overlap exactly as predicted at every length tested. Limitation: frog muscle at cold-room temperature, held in strict isometric conditions few real movements ever replicate — human muscle architecture and temperature shift the exact numbers, though not the shape of the relationship.

Five things to carry forward

  1. A sarcomere shortens by sliding, not shrinking its filaments, and the four-step cross-bridge cycle — attach, pull, detach, re-cock — runs independently on every myosin head, with ATP both releasing the attached head and re-cocking it for the next cycle.
  2. Excitation–contraction coupling is a five-step relay from action potential to calcium to exposed binding sites, and without calcium reaching troponin the cross-bridge cycle simply cannot start, however strong the nervous signal.
  3. The length–tension relationship means a muscle's maximum force depends on sarcomere length: weaker at both very short and very long lengths, strongest on the plateau where filament overlap is greatest.
  4. The force–velocity relationship means force falls as shortening speed rises, because fewer cross-bridges stay attached at high sliding speed — and the same logic means forcibly lengthening an active muscle produces more force than holding it isometrically still.
  5. These two curves operate together at every instant, which is why the same muscle can look powerful in one exercise and surprisingly weak in another, depending on the joint angle and speed each one demands.
