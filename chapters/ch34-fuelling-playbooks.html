Fuelling Playbooks

Day plans and a food atlas for each training goal

Every principle in the three chapters before this one — carbohydrate periodisation, the protein leucine threshold, fat as the flexible fuel — is only useful once it survives contact with an actual Tuesday. This chapter is that contact. It converts the targets from chapters 28 to 30 into timed plates of real food for five kinds of training day and three kinds of race day, and it closes with the single nutrition topic in this book most capable of quietly ending a career: chronically eating too little for what you ask your body to do.

34.1How To Build A Day From The Targets In Chapters 28–30

Four decisions, taken in this order, turn a list of nutrient targets into an actual day of eating.

  1. 1. Set energy

    Start from an estimate of total daily energy expenditure (resting rate plus activity plus training cost) and decide, deliberately, whether today is a maintenance day, a surplus day, or a deficit day — never leave this to appetite alone on a day either side of a key session.

  2. 2. Set protein

    Fix total daily protein in g/kg first, from chapter 29's targets, then distribute it across 3–5 meals or snacks each clearing the leucine threshold (roughly 2.5–3 g leucine, about 0.3–0.4 g/kg protein per feeding). Protein is set before carbohydrate and fat because it is the least flexible number — too little, on any day, is the one mistake with no fast fix.

  3. 3. Place carbohydrate around sessions

    Spend the carbohydrate budget from chapter 28 where training demands it most — before, during and after the hardest or longest session of the day — rather than spreading it evenly across 24 hours. An easy day and a hard day can carry identical total energy and wildly different carbohydrate timing.

  4. 4. Fill the rest with fat

    Whatever energy remains after protein and carbohydrate are set becomes dietary fat, from chapter 30's sources. This is deliberately the leftover variable, not because fat is unimportant, but because it is the macronutrient training performance is least sensitive to on any single day — provided it doesn't fall so low, persistently, that essential fatty acid and hormone needs go unmet.

In plain terms

Think of a day's food like packing a rucksack for a day hike with a fixed-size bag (energy). The tent and the first-aid kit go in first, non-negotiable, whatever is left over (protein). Then you pack around the day's actual terrain — more water and snacks for the stretch with the big climb, less for the flat bit at the end (carbohydrate around sessions). Whatever space is left takes the comfortable, flexible stuff — a spare jumper, a book (fat). Pack in the wrong order and you run out of room for the tent.

34.2Playbook: Endurance Base Day

An easy 90-minute aerobic session, the bulk of most endurance athletes' weekly volume, needs almost nothing special — which is itself worth stating plainly, since it is the day most over-fuelled out of habit.

Notice what is absent: no in-session fuelling plan, no elaborate recovery window. An easy day's job is to deliver training stimulus without digging an energy hole, and the biggest practical error on this kind of day is treating it like the next one.

34.3Playbook: Hard Interval Or Race-Prep Day

A session built around sustained high intensity — VO2max intervals, a threshold block, or a dress-rehearsal race-pace run — shifts carbohydrate timing hard toward the session itself, because glycogen availability measurably affects the quality of work you can hold at these intensities.

34.4Playbook: Hypertrophy Day

A day built around resistance training for muscle growth looks different again: total energy is at maintenance or a small surplus, protein distribution across the day matters more than any single pre- or post-workout window, and carbohydrate is placed to fuel the session rather than to replace a huge immediate glycogen debt.

34.5Playbook: Fat Loss While Training

This is the day plan where getting the order of operations wrong costs the most. A deficit that is too large, or carbohydrate cut indiscriminately rather than placed around sessions, reliably costs training quality long before it meaningfully speeds fat loss — and the two mistakes compound, because a worse session this week makes next week's deficit feel worse for less benefit.

The deficit size that best preserves performance while still producing steady loss is roughly 0.5–1.0% of body mass per week B — faster than that and the probability of losing training quality, fat-free mass, and in women menstrual function rises sharply for very little extra speed. Protein is raised, not cut, to roughly 2.0–2.4 g/kg, higher than a weight-stable athlete needs, because adequate protein is the main defence against losing muscle alongside fat in a deficit. Carbohydrate is where the deficit is actually taken from, and it is taken unevenly: higher on hard or long training days, where performance depends on it, and lower on rest or easy days, where the body's need for it is genuinely smaller.

How a fixed weekly deficit is distributed across training and rest days
Day typeEnergyCarbohydrateProteinLogic
Rest / easy dayLarger deficitLower (2–3 g/kg)2.0–2.4 g/kg, unchangedLeast training cost to protect; take most of the deficit here
Moderate session dayModerate deficitModerate (3–4 g/kg)2.0–2.4 g/kg, unchangedBalance deficit against needing to recover for the week's key session
Hard / long session daySmall deficit or maintenanceHigher (5–7 g/kg)2.0–2.4 g/kg, unchangedProtect the session the whole week is built around
Myth vs fact
“To lose fat fastest while marathon training, cut carbs across the board”

Less carbohydrate, every day, should mean a bigger deficit and faster fat loss.

Evenly cutting carbohydrate costs the sessions that matter most

A flat carbohydrate cut takes exactly as much away from the long run, where glycogen availability genuinely limits output, as it does from a rest day, where it barely matters. The same weekly deficit, redistributed toward rest days, produces the same fat-loss rate with a measurably better long run — the total energy gap is what drives fat loss, not which day it is taken from.

Worked example
Naomi, 38, 68 kg, training for her second marathon at 65 km/week, wants to lose fat gradually without wrecking her long runs. Maintenance energy estimated at 2,350 kcal/day.
  1. Set the weekly deficit at the conservative end: 0.5% of body mass per week.68 kg × 0.005 = 0.34 kg/week ≈ a ~270 kcal/day average deficit (1 kg fat ≈ 7,700 kcal)
  2. Set protein high and flat across every day, regardless of training.68 kg × 2.2 g/kg = 150 g protein/day, every day
  3. Build a rest-day target: lower energy, lower carbohydrate, protein unchanged.1,950 kcal: 150 g protein (600 kcal) + 140 g carb (560 kcal) + 87 g fat (790 kcal)
  4. Build a long-run-day target (22 km at the weekend): energy near maintenance, carbohydrate much higher, fat trimmed to make room.2,300 kcal: 150 g protein (600 kcal) + 340 g carb (1,360 kcal) + 38 g fat (340 kcal)
  5. Average the week: five lighter days and two long-run-adjacent days land close to the 270 kcal/day average deficit while the long run itself runs at near-maintenance with carbohydrate stacked around it.Weekly average: ~2,080 kcal/day ≈ 270 kcal/day below the 2,350 kcal maintenance estimate
So: Naomi loses roughly 0.3–0.4 kg a week on paper, protects every long run with a carbohydrate-rich day built around it, and never drops below 150 g of protein — the deficit lives almost entirely on her easy and rest days, where training has the least to lose from it.

34.6Playbook: Race Day

Three events, three different fuelling problems: a half marathon is short enough that pre-race loading matters more than in-race fuelling; a marathon or a four-hour ride both run long enough that in-race carbohydrate delivery, trained in advance, decides the last third of the effort.

Breakfast, 2–3 h before1–2 g/kg carbohydrate (toast, honey, banana)2–4 g/kg carbohydrate (oats, banana, white bread, low fibre)2–3 g/kg carbohydrate

Late-race optionCaffeine 2–3 mg/kg if trialled in trainingCaffeine top-up in the final third; gels with added sodium in heatCaffeine and solid food (bars, rice cakes) tolerated better than running

The thread running through all three columns is the same: nothing in the "during" or "late-race" row should be new on the day. Every gel, every concentration of sports drink, every caffeine dose should already have appeared, in roughly that order and quantity, in a long training session beforehand.

34.7Energy Availability And RED-S

Energy availability is the energy left for the body's normal physiological functions once the energy cost of training has been subtracted from intake — roughly, energy eaten minus energy spent exercising, expressed relative to fat-free mass. It is a different question from total energy intake or body weight alone: two athletes eating identically can have very different energy availability if one trains two hours a day and the other trains six.

Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport (RED-S) is what happens when energy availability stays low for long enough that the body starts deliberately running fewer systems to protect the ones it judges essential. The 2023 International Olympic Committee consensus statement frames this plainly: chronic low energy availability can impair metabolic rate, reproductive hormone function, bone health, cardiovascular health, and psychological wellbeing, in both male and female athletes — this is not, and was never accurately, a condition limited to young female endurance athletes, even though that population was where it was first well described.

Warning signs worth taking seriously, in combination rather than any one alone: recurrent bone stress injuries or a fracture from unremarkable loading; menstrual irregularity or loss of periods not explained by something else; a training plateau or decline despite training harder; unusual fatigue, poor sleep, or getting ill often; a resting heart rate that drifts down or blood pressure that drifts down unexpectedly; and a preoccupation with food, eating or body image that is starting to feel effortful rather than straightforward. Any one of these in isolation has other explanations. Several together, in an athlete training hard, is the pattern worth acting on.

Where this goes wrong

This is the most consequential nutrition topic in this book, not a footnote, and it needs to be treated that way. RED-S is diagnosed and managed by clinicians — typically a sports medicine physician alongside a registered dietitian, sometimes with a mental health professional — not by a book chapter, a calculator, or a training partner's advice. If you recognise several of the warning signs above in yourself or in an athlete you coach, the right next step is a conversation with a GP or sports physician, not a change you design and run yourself. This chapter deliberately gives no body-composition targets anywhere, and nothing in this book should be read as encouragement to restrict intake in pursuit of a number on a scale or a body shape — if any part of your relationship with food or training has started to feel like restriction rather than fuelling, that is itself worth raising with a clinician, independent of whether RED-S turns out to be the right label.

The paper
Mountjoy M, Ackerman KE, Bailey DM, et al. 2023 International Olympic Committee's (IOC) consensus statement on Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport (REDs). Br J Sports Med 2023;57(17):1073–1097.
Who

A multidisciplinary IOC expert panel, updating the 2014 and 2018 statements with evidence gathered since, including in male and Para athletes.

What

A synthesis of the mechanisms, screening, diagnosis and management of RED-S, introducing an updated Clinical Assessment Tool for risk-stratifying affected athletes rather than relying on a single energy-availability number.

Found

Low energy availability, sustained over weeks to months, produces measurable harm across metabolic, reproductive, skeletal, cardiovascular, haematological, immune and psychological systems in both sexes, with prevalence estimated at roughly 15–80% of elite athletes depending on sport and screening method — and the syndrome is routinely unrecognised by athletes, coaches and even clinicians until a fracture or a performance collapse forces the issue.

Why it matters: it moved the field away from a single contested energy-availability cutoff toward recognising a graded, multi-system syndrome that needs clinical judgement to identify early, when it is most reversible. Limitation: as a consensus statement it synthesises expert interpretation of a still-growing evidence base, and prevalence estimates vary widely because different studies screen for it differently.

Activate it

Turning the week's food atlas into a week of practice, not just a plan on paper.

  • Eat Build each day from the four-step method: energy, then protein, then carbohydrate around the session, then fat filling the rest.
  • Dose On a fat-loss block, keep the weekly deficit at 0.5–1.0% of body mass and protein at 2.0–2.4 g/kg every single day, regardless of training load.
  • Timing Move carbohydrate toward the hardest or longest session of the day; don't spread it evenly if the day itself isn't even.
  • Test Rehearse every race-day food, gel, drink concentration and caffeine dose in a long training session first — race day should contain no nutritional surprises.
  • On the bike A long indoor trainer session is an easy, controlled place to rehearse a marathon or 4-hour-ride fuelling plan exactly as planned, including the fluid and carbohydrate numbers from the calculator above.
  • Signal You're doing it right when hard sessions feel fuelled rather than flat, periods and recovery markers stay normal through a deficit block, and nothing you eat or drink on race day is new.
B The deficit-size and protein targets for training while losing fat are a reasonable synthesis of the literature; the exact day-to-day carbohydrate split is good practice, not a single tested protocol.

Five things to carry forward

  1. Build any day in a fixed order — energy, then protein, then carbohydrate placed around sessions, then fat filling what's left — rather than trying to balance all four at once.
  2. Easy days need almost no special fuelling; hard, long and race days need carbohydrate and fluid rehearsed in training exactly as planned for the event, never tried for the first time on the day.
  3. Fat loss while training works best at a 0.5–1.0% body-mass-per-week deficit, with protein raised to 2.0–2.4 g/kg and the deficit taken mostly from rest and easy days, protecting the sessions that matter.
  4. Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport is not a niche or female-only condition — sustained low energy availability damages metabolic, reproductive, skeletal, cardiovascular and psychological function in athletes of either sex, and is the single most consequential topic in this book's nutrition section.
  5. Warning signs of RED-S cluster rather than appear alone, and the right response to recognising them is a referral to a sports medicine clinician and dietitian, not a self-designed fix.
