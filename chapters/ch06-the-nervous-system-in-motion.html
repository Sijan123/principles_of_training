The Nervous System in Motion

Intent to contraction: drive, feedback, skill and central fatigue

Decide to jump, and roughly a tenth of a second later you leave the ground. In that gap, a signal has travelled from your motor cortex down the length of your spinal cord, out along a nerve longer than your leg, across a chemical junction, and into thousands of muscle fibres — while a separate set of sensors has been reporting back the whole time on exactly how far, how fast and how hard each joint is moving, ready to override the plan if anything goes wrong. Strength is usually described as a property of muscle. For roughly the first month of any new training programme, it is far more accurately described as a property of this circuitry — and the circuitry never stops mattering, even once the muscle has grown.

6.1From Intention to Contraction

A voluntary movement begins in the motor cortex, a strip of brain tissue running over the top of the head that maps, roughly and unevenly, onto the body's muscles — more cortical territory is devoted to the hands and face than to the back, reflecting how finely controlled each needs to be. From there, the signal descends almost the full length of the central nervous system along the corticospinal tract, a bundle of nerve fibres running from cortex to spinal cord, most of it crossing to the opposite side of the body on the way down, which is why damage to one side of the brain typically weakens the opposite side of the body.

At the spinal cord, the signal is handed off to an alpha motor neuron — the same motor neuron introduced in chapter 5 as half of a motor unit — whose cell body sits in the spinal cord but whose single long fibre runs, uninterrupted, all the way out to the muscle. Where that fibre meets the muscle fibre, there is a specialised chemical synapse called the neuromuscular junction. The arriving electrical signal triggers the release of a neurotransmitter, acetylcholine, across a microscopic gap; acetylcholine binds receptors on the muscle fibre membrane and triggers the fibre's own electrical impulse, which is what sets off excitation–contraction coupling as described in chapter 4. Every step in this chain — cortex, tract, cord, neuron, junction — has to work for a decision to become a contraction, and every step is itself subject to adaptation, which is the subject of most of this chapter.

In plain terms

Think of the whole chain as a relay race with exactly four runners and two handoffs: cortex to spinal cord by wire, spinal cord to muscle by a different wire, and one chemical handoff at the very end where the baton has to be literally thrown across a gap.

Training can make any leg of this relay faster or more reliable — a cleaner signal from the cortex, a less hesitant handoff at the junction, a motor neuron that fires sooner after being asked to. None of that requires the muscle itself to change size at all, which is exactly why strength can rise noticeably before a muscle looks any different.

6.2Proprioception: The Body's Own Sensors

Movement is not a one-way broadcast from brain to muscle. Two sensory structures embedded in muscle and tendon report continuously back the other way, giving you proprioception — the sense of where your joints are and how hard your muscles are working, without needing to look.

The two proprioceptors that matter most for training
SensorLocationDetectsReflex effect
Muscle spindleEmbedded within the muscle belly, in parallel with ordinary fibresMuscle length, and how fast length is changingA sudden stretch triggers the stretch reflex — rapid, reflexive contraction of the same muscle, resisting the stretch
Golgi tendon organ (GTO)At the junction between muscle fibres and tendonTension — how hard the muscle is actually pullingVery high tension can trigger reflex inhibition of that same muscle, a protective brake against self-generated overload

The stretch reflex is the fastest reflex arc in the body, running spindle to spinal cord to motor neuron and back to the same muscle in well under a tenth of a second, without needing to involve the brain at all. It is why a doctor's tendon-hammer tap below the knee produces an involuntary kick, and — far more usefully — why your calf muscles can resist an unexpected ankle roll on uneven ground faster than you could ever consciously react to it. This reflex loop is also why static, sustained stretching feels like it has to work slowly: push a muscle into a sudden deep stretch and the spindle reflexively resists you; hold a milder stretch steadily and the spindle's signal gradually adapts, which is part of why flexibility gains are measured in sustained holds rather than fast, forceful ones.

This is a genuine trade-off, not a design flaw. The same fast, local, protective loop that stabilises your ankle on a kerb also actively resists exactly the kind of sudden deep stretch that mobility training sometimes wants to achieve, which is why slow, sustained methods generally outperform fast, forceful ones for lasting range-of-motion gains — a topic covered properly in chapter 19 and chapter 40.

6.3The Stretch-Shortening Cycle

Combine a compliant tendon (chapter 3) with the stretch reflex just described and you get one of the most useful mechanisms in athletic movement: the stretch-shortening cycle (SSC) — a rapid stretch (eccentric phase) immediately followed by a shortening contraction (concentric phase) in the same muscle–tendon unit, with no pause in between. Two separate mechanisms stack during an SSC. First, elastic energy stored in the tendon and other series-elastic tissue during the fast stretch is partly returned during the immediately following shortening, the spring-and-motor arrangement from chapter 3 working in its most dynamic form. Second, the stretch reflex triggered by the fast lengthening phase adds extra reflexive muscle activation on top of whatever was voluntarily intended, a boost called reflex potentiation. Neither mechanism works in isolation nearly as well as the two work stacked together, and the stacking only happens if the transition from stretch to shortening is fast — a slow, paused transition lets the stored elastic energy dissipate as heat and lets the reflex contribution fade before the concentric phase begins.

The clearest everyday demonstration is the gap between a countermovement jump (CMJ) — stand, dip down fast, jump — and a squat jump (SJ) — hold a static dip position, then jump with no preceding downward movement. Both start the concentric, upward phase from the same joint angles with the same muscles available. The only difference is whether that concentric phase was preceded by a fast eccentric stretch. Across large samples of athletes, CMJ height exceeds SJ height by roughly 5–15% C, and that gap is conventionally attributed to the stretch-shortening cycle — though it is worth naming the honest disagreement in the literature here: some biomechanists argue part of the gap reflects how much slack is in the muscle-tendon unit and how quickly that slack is taken up, rather than elastic storage and reflex potentiation alone, so treat the SSC as the dominant but not sole explanation for the difference.

Worked example
Tomasz, a club-level volleyball player, is tested on a force plate: squat jump height 36.0 cm, countermovement jump height 39.0 cm.
  1. Find the absolute gap between the two jump types, which isolates the contribution of the fast eccentric–concentric transition.39.0 − 36.0 = 3.0 cm
  2. Express that gap as a percentage of the squat jump, the baseline with no stretch-shortening contribution.3.0 ÷ 36.0 × 100 ≈ 8.3%
  3. Compare against the typical reported range across athletes of similar training background.8.3% sits within the usual 5–10% band
So: roughly 8% of Tomasz's countermovement jump height — about 3 cm — comes specifically from the stretch-shortening cycle: elastic energy return plus reflex potentiation generated by the fast downward dip, stacked on top of the same muscles and the same concentric range used in the static squat jump.

6.4Neural Adaptations: The First Month's Strength Gain

When a beginner starts resistance training, strength typically rises faster in the first two to four weeks than muscle size does — sometimes considerably faster, with measurable strength gains well before any ultrasound-detectable change in muscle cross-section. This is not a mystery and it is not placebo. It reflects real, measurable change at every level of the chain described in section one.

  • More descending drive — the cortex sends a stronger, more complete signal down the corticospinal tract for a given intended effort.
  • Less intracortical inhibition — the brain's own internal braking circuits, which normally hold some voluntary drive in reserve, relax their restraint, measurable directly as a shortened cortical silent period in brain-stimulation studies.
  • Better motor-unit synchronisation and rate coding — the motor units that are recruited fire in better-coordinated bursts and at a higher rate for the same effort, both mechanisms from chapter 5.
  • Less antagonist co-contraction — a beginner often unconsciously activates the opposing muscle group during a lift, braking their own effort; this co-contraction measurably falls within the first few weeks of practising a movement.

These are genuine structural changes in the nervous system — cortical and spinal plasticity, not "just getting used to it" in some dismissive sense — and they are also why a beginner's early strength gains can outrun their muscle growth so dramatically. The muscle itself has barely changed; the system commanding it has become a measurably better driver of the muscle that was already there.

The paper
Lecce E, Amoruso P, Felici F, Bazzucchi I. Resistance training–induced adaptations in the neuromuscular system: physiological mechanisms and implications for human performance. J Physiol 2026;604:81–115.
Who

A topical review synthesising neurophysiological and performance studies of resistance-trained humans.

What

Integrates findings across cortical, spinal and peripheral levels on how resistance-training variables — load, velocity, volume, rest — produce neuromechanical outcomes such as maximal force and rate of force development.

Found

Early strength gains are consistently traced to reduced intracortical inhibition and increased corticospinal and motoneuronal drive, occurring on a timescale of days to weeks, well ahead of detectable hypertrophy; the review proposes an integrative framework linking specific training variables to the neural mechanism each one predominantly drives.

Why it matters: it draws together decades of smaller neurophysiology studies into one current account of exactly which neural systems adapt, and roughly when. Limitation: as a review of mostly short- and medium-term studies with varied designs, it synthesises direction and mechanism more confidently than it can pin down precise, generalisable timelines for any one person.

6.5Why Skill Is Specific

Because so much of early strength is neural coordination rather than raw muscle capacity, it follows that a large share of what training builds is specific to the exact movement pattern practised — the nervous system is learning this sequence of recruitment, timing and inter-muscular coordination, not a general, transferable quality called "athleticism." This is why a lifter can be dramatically stronger on a back squat than on a nearly identical-looking front squat or belt squat despite near-identical muscles being involved: the coordination demands, bar path and balance requirements differ enough that the nervous system has not yet learned the second pattern as well as the first.

It is also the central reason "functional training" transfer claims are usually narrower than advertised. Balancing on an unstable surface while doing a light exercise trains balance on an unstable surface while doing a light exercise — a real, specific skill, but one that transfers only modestly to a sport's actual demands unless the sport itself regularly asks for exactly that combination of instability and light load. Meaningful transfer to a sporting skill generally requires some real resemblance between the training movement and the target skill in at least one of: the joint angles used, the speed of movement, and the force demanded. Match none of those three and "functional" is describing the exercise's appearance, not its training effect.

Myth vs fact
“Training on unstable surfaces (BOSU balls, wobble boards) builds better athletic stability for sport”

If it challenges balance in the gym, it should improve balance on the field.

It mostly builds balance on that unstable surface

Unstable-surface training reliably improves performance on that same unstable surface, and has a legitimate, narrow role in some rehabilitation settings. The carryover to stable-ground sporting tasks — sprinting, jumping, change of direction — is consistently smaller than the resemblance between the two tasks would suggest, because the specific coordination pattern the nervous system learns on a wobbling surface is not the coordination pattern a stable sprint start requires.

6.6Central Fatigue: When the Brake Comes From Above the Muscle

Not all fatigue happens in the muscle. Peripheral fatigue is a real decline in the muscle fibre's own capacity to produce force — metabolite accumulation, calcium-handling problems, fuel depletion. Central fatigue is a reduction in the voluntary neural drive reaching the muscle, measurable by comparing a maximal voluntary contraction with the extra force an external electrical stimulus can still squeeze out of the same muscle at the same moment: if the muscle still has more to give once stimulated directly, the shortfall during the voluntary effort was central, not peripheral.

Central fatigue is heavily influenced by afferent feedback — the continuous stream of signals travelling back from working muscles, reporting metabolic strain, mechanical tension and other local conditions up to the brain and spinal cord. This feedback is a leading candidate for what rating of perceived exertion (RPE) is actually measuring: not a direct readout of how much ATP is left or how much lactate has accumulated, but an integrated sense built partly from this afferent traffic, partly from central command itself, and partly from prior experience of how hard an effort of this kind has felt before. This is also most of the physiological basis for pacing: afferent feedback from working muscle appears to act as a protective brake on central drive before true peripheral failure is reached, which is part of why an athlete who has learned a pace or a race distance can push closer to their genuine physiological limit than one encountering the same demand for the first time — the brake is calibrated by experience, not fixed.

Activate it

Training the stretch-shortening cycle directly — the fast eccentric-to-concentric transition that a slow lift never rehearses.

  • Do Depth jumps: step off a box, land, and rebound upward as fast as possible with minimal ground-contact time.
  • Load Bodyweight; box height moderate (20–40 cm) and only increased once ground-contact time stays short at the current height.
  • Dose 3–5 sets × 3–5 jumps, full recovery between sets — this is a quality session, not a conditioning one.
  • When Once or twice weekly, early in a session while the nervous system is fresh.
  • Progress Shorten ground-contact time before increasing box height; a taller box with a slow, heavy landing trains something closer to strength than to the SSC.
  • On the bike A circular pedal stroke has no equivalent fast-stretch-then-release phase, so there is no meaningful indoor-cycling substitute for this specific adaptation.
  • Signal You're doing it right when ground-contact time stays short and the rebound feels reactive rather than effortful and deliberate.
B Plyometric training reliably improves jump height and reactive strength in trained and untrained populations; optimal box height and dosing vary by training background.

Five things to carry forward

  1. A voluntary movement travels motor cortex → corticospinal tract → alpha motor neuron → neuromuscular junction, and adaptation can happen at every one of those steps independently of the muscle itself.
  2. Muscle spindles sense length and velocity, Golgi tendon organs sense tension, and the resulting stretch reflex both stabilises joints against sudden strain and resists the fast stretches that mobility training is sometimes trying to achieve.
  3. The stretch-shortening cycle stacks elastic energy return with reflex potentiation, and typically adds roughly 5–15% to jump height when a countermovement precedes the jump compared with a static squat jump.
  4. Most strength gained in the first two to four weeks of training is neural — more drive, less cortical inhibition, better synchronisation, less antagonist co-contraction — which is why strength can rise well before a muscle visibly changes.
  5. Central fatigue is a drop in voluntary neural drive rather than a failure within the muscle itself, afferent feedback from working muscle is a leading candidate for what RPE actually tracks, and both skill transfer and pacing are narrower and more experience-dependent than they are often given credit for.
