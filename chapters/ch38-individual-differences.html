Individual Differences

Age, sex, menstrual cycle, genetics and responders

Two runners follow the identical sixteen-week marathon plan. One takes four minutes off their personal best. The other takes thirty seconds. Both trained honestly, both recovered adequately, and the gap between them has almost nothing to do with effort. This chapter is about the sources of that gap — age, sex, the menstrual cycle, and the plain genetic lottery — and about the uncomfortable discipline of being precise where the evidence is strong and equally precise about where it genuinely is not.

38.1Responders And Non-Responders

Chapter 13 already introduced the core finding: run an identical training programme on a large group of people and the spread of individual results is often larger than the average result itself. In the HERITAGE Family Study, several hundred sedentary adults completed the same standardised 20-week endurance programme, and individual VO2max changes ranged from roughly −5% to nearly +48% B. Statistical modelling of the family structure in that same dataset attributed roughly 0.43–0.72 of the variance in baseline VO2max itself to heredity across twin and family studies more broadly, with a separate, smaller HERITAGE-specific estimate of around 0.47 for the heritability of the training response specifically — a related but distinct number, since where you start and how fast you move are not the same inheritance.

The detail worth repeating from chapter 13 because it changes how this number should be used: a 2017 re-test study found that the same person's "non-response" to one training block often did not repeat in a second block, and that classifying people by dose rather than by fixed trait explained a great deal of the apparent non-response — at the lowest prescribed training volume, roughly 69% of participants failed to show a meaningful VO2max gain; at the highest volume, the non-response rate fell to zero. B The practical reading is not "we cannot predict who will respond," but "a group average tells you about the group, and your own early results are better evidence of your trajectory than someone else's mean" — and that nearly everyone responds to something, provided the stimulus is varied rather than repeated at a dose that happened not to suit them the first time.

In plain terms

Think of a training programme as a recipe and genetics as how your particular oven runs.

Two ovens set to the identical 180 °C will not cook the same cake in the same time if one runs genuinely hot and one runs genuinely cool. That is not a flaw in the recipe, and it is not a reason to give up on baking — it is a reason to watch your own oven rather than someone else's cookbook photo, and to adjust the time, not abandon the cake.

38.2Sex Differences

At the elite end of most sports, the performance gap between men and women runs roughly 10–30%, with the size of the gap tracking the specific demands of the event: it is smaller in ultra-endurance and technical-skill events, and larger in events dominated by absolute power and sprint speed, where the underlying muscle-mass and strength differences matter most directly. B That headline gap sits on top of several genuine underlying physiological differences, not one single cause.

Women tend to have a higher proportion of type I (slow, fatigue-resistant) muscle fibres than men of comparable training status, alongside a somewhat greater reliance on fat oxidation at a given relative exercise intensity and a measure of glycogen sparing that follows from it — useful traits for sustained, moderate-intensity work. Differences in muscle perfusion and skeletal-muscle oxidative capacity have also been reported, generally in a direction that favours endurance over absolute power. Taken together, these differences plausibly underlie one of the more consistent findings in the comparative literature: women are more fatigue-resistant than men in many neuromuscular tasks, particularly in sustained or repeated submaximal contractions, even where absolute strength clearly favours men. B

None of this implies a difference in how well training itself works. Relative trainability — the percentage improvement achievable from a given starting point, for a given programme — is, as far as the evidence shows, equal between the sexes: women and men gain strength, VO2max and muscle mass at broadly similar relative rates from similar relative training stimuli. B The absolute performance gap at the elite level and the trainability of any given individual are different questions, and conflating them is the most common error in this part of the literature.

38.3The Menstrual Cycle

This is the section in this chapter that most needs handling with discipline, because the gap between what athletes report feeling and what controlled research has been able to confirm is unusually wide, and both halves of that gap are real and worth stating plainly.

Survey after survey of elite female athletes finds that a clear majority — estimates across different sports and cohorts range from roughly 50% to nearly 90%, with most falling in the 60–80% range — report that their menstrual cycle affects their training or competition in some way. B This is a large, consistent, and clearly genuine pattern of lived experience; it should not be minimised or explained away.

What is much less settled is what controlled performance testing actually shows. A systematic review of studies in elite athletes specifically found that where performance differences between cycle phases were reported, the direction of the difference was inconsistent between studies, and most individual differences were not statistically significant — some athletes performed better in the follicular phase, some in the luteal phase, and pooling across studies produced no clear, generalisable pattern. B A separate, more recent review restricted specifically to studies using rigorous methodology (direct hormonal verification of cycle phase, rather than simple calendar counting) found the same thing: no consistent, reliable effect of cycle phase on objectively measured performance across the pooled literature.

Part of this gap is a genuine research-quality problem, not a sign that nothing is happening. A large share of the older literature verified "cycle phase" by counting days from the last period rather than by measuring the hormones that actually define the phase, and individual cycles vary enough in length and hormonal profile that calendar counting alone places a meaningful fraction of participants in the wrong phase without anyone knowing it. Interestingly, the same body of evidence shows a consistent split between perceived and objectively measured performance: athletes reliably report feeling worse in the early follicular and late luteal phases, while objective tests of strength, power and endurance taken in the same athletes, in the same phases, do not show a matching, consistent decline. Perception and measured output disagreeing this consistently is itself a finding worth taking seriously, not a reason to dismiss either side of it.

The defensible practical position, given this picture, has three parts. First, individual tracking: because effects, where real, are highly individual rather than following one universal template, an athlete's own logged symptoms and performance against her own cycle are far more useful than a generic phase-based rule borrowed from a magazine article or an app's default assumption. Second, symptom management: where an individual reliably experiences specific symptoms — disrupted sleep, mood change, gastrointestinal discomfort, heavy or painful bleeding — managing those symptoms directly is well supported, regardless of whether the underlying performance evidence for phase-based programming is settled. Third, and most important, is what defensible practice explicitly avoids: imposing a rigid phase-based training template on every athlete on the assumption that a specific phase universally calls for reduced or increased load. The evidence does not support a one-size-fits-all rule, and applying one risks under-training an athlete whose own pattern does not match the template, or creating a self-fulfilling performance expectation where none was physiologically necessary.

Hormonal contraception adds a further layer of individual variation rather than resolving it: different formulations (combined oral contraceptives, progestin-only methods, hormonal IUDs) produce different hormonal profiles, and the research comparing trained performance across contraceptive types and natural cycles remains limited and mixed. This is an area where individual response, not a general rule, should again guide practice, and where decisions about contraception itself are a medical and personal matter entirely separate from training programming.

Where this goes wrong

Significant menstrual symptoms — heavy or very painful bleeding, cycle irregularity following a change in training load, or any suspicion of relative energy deficiency in sport (RED-S, covered in chapter 34) — are a matter for clinical assessment, not a training-programming decision. This chapter describes population-level evidence on performance; it is not a substitute for medical advice about an individual's menstrual or reproductive health.

The paper
McNulty KL, Elliott-Sale KJ, Dolan E, et al. The effects of menstrual cycle phase on elite athlete performance: a critical and systematic review. Sports Medicine 2020;50(10):1813–27.
Who

Seven studies meeting strict inclusion criteria for elite-level athletes, 314 participants pooled.

What

Systematically reviewed studies testing athletic performance across menstrual cycle phases in elite female athletes, critically appraising cycle-phase verification methods alongside the performance results themselves.

Found

Six of seven studies reported some performance difference between the follicular and luteal phases, but the direction of the difference was inconsistent across studies and the great majority of individual comparisons were not statistically significant. The authors concluded there is no clear, generalisable optimal cycle phase for performance.

Why it matters: it is the clearest, most methodologically careful statement that population-level, phase-based performance rules are not currently supported by elite-athlete data. Limitation: seven studies and 314 participants is a small evidence base for a question this individually variable, and the review itself flags inconsistent cycle-verification methods across the underlying studies as a likely contributor to the inconsistent findings — absence of a settled group effect is not the same as proof of no effect in any individual.

38.4Youth

Trainability before puberty is dominated by the nervous system: children reliably improve strength, coordination and skill from resistance training, but with little accompanying muscle growth, because the hormonal environment (low circulating testosterone) that drives hypertrophy in adults is largely absent. After puberty, the hormonal picture shifts substantially and hypertrophic responses to resistance training begin to resemble the adult pattern far more closely, particularly from mid-to-late adolescence onward.

The safety evidence for youth resistance training is now extensive and reassuring: across prospective studies using age-appropriate loading and qualified supervision, injury rates during resistance training in children and adolescents are consistently low, and comparably safer than many mainstream youth sports. A No prospective study has reported injury to growth-plate (epiphyseal) cartilage from properly supervised resistance training, and there is no evidence that resistance training impairs growth or maturation. The real risk factor identified across this literature is not the training itself but its absence of supervision: injuries cluster around inadequate technique instruction, inappropriate equipment, and insufficient oversight, not around the basic act of a child lifting an appropriately scaled load.

Long-term athlete development frameworks, built from this evidence, generally recommend broad, varied movement skill development before heavy specialisation in one sport, for reasons that are only partly about injury: early specialisation is associated with higher overuse injury rates in the specific, repeatedly loaded tissues of the specialised sport, and with higher rates of burnout and early dropout, compared with athletes who sample multiple sports through childhood and specialise later. B This is a population pattern with well-known individual exceptions (some sports, such as elite gymnastics, have development windows that make very early specialisation close to unavoidable), but it is the right default assumption for most young athletes in most sports.

38.5Masters Athletes

Performance decline with age is not uniform across qualities, and the shape of the decline matters more than any single number. Power falls faster than endurance: cross-sectional and longitudinal data on masters athletes consistently show steeper age-related decline curves for sprint speed, jump height and maximal power output than for sustained aerobic performance, where well-trained masters athletes can retain a remarkably high fraction of their younger performance for decades before a steeper late-life decline sets in. The mechanistic driver is sarcopenia — age-related loss of muscle mass and, disproportionately, of fast-twitch (type II) fibres and the motor units that drive them — which erodes the specific qualities (rate of force development, peak power) that depend most on type II fibre function, while leaving the oxidative, type-I-dominant machinery relatively better preserved.

Ageing muscle also develops anabolic resistance: the same dose of dietary protein produces a smaller rise in muscle protein synthesis in an older muscle than in a younger one, which is why the per-meal protein target generally recommended for older adults sits higher than the equivalent young-adult figure — commonly cited at around 0.4 g/kg of high-quality protein per meal for untrained older adults, roughly 50–65% higher than the roughly 0.25 g/kg that saturates the response in a younger adult. B There is an important nuance worth stating honestly: well-trained masters athletes appear to show less of this anabolic resistance than sedentary older adults of the same age, with some evidence suggesting a trained older athlete's per-meal requirement sits closer to 0.3 g/kg — meaningfully above a young adult's but somewhat below the figure typically quoted for untrained older people. C The practical range worth working from is roughly 0.3–0.4 g/kg per meal, trained status shifting an individual toward the lower end of that range rather than requiring the full untrained figure.

The single most important message for this population, and the one most often inverted in casual advice, is that intensity and strength work become more important with age, not less. Because power and fast-twitch capacity decline fastest, and because strength is one of the strongest available protections against the falls and fractures that drive most age-related loss of independence, an older athlete has a stronger case for maintaining heavy strength training and some genuinely high-intensity work than a younger one does — not a weaker one, as the common instinct to "take it easier" with age would suggest. The dose simply needs to respect a longer connective-tissue ramp and more deliberate recovery planning, covered in chapter 39, rather than being abandoned altogether.

Activate it

A masters template that keeps real strength and intensity in the programme rather than quietly removing them as the default response to age.

  • Do Two full-body strength sessions a week covering a squat pattern, a hinge, a push and a pull, plus one weekly session with genuine high-intensity intervals.
  • Load Strength work up to 75–85% of a cautiously re-established 1RM once technique is solid; intervals hard enough to be uncomfortable, not merely brisk.
  • Dose 2–3 sets of 5–8 reps per strength exercise; 4–6 short (1–3 min) hard efforts for the intensity session.
  • When Space hard sessions at least 48 hours apart and build load over 10–12 weeks rather than the 4–6 a younger athlete might use, respecting the connective-tissue ramp in chapter 39.
  • On the bike An indoor trainer is an excellent low-impact way to deliver the intensity session — 5–6 × 3 min hard with full recovery — without the joint loading of running intervals, while the strength work still handles the bone and connective-tissue stimulus cycling cannot provide.
  • Signal You're doing it right when strength and power numbers are stable or rising year over year, not merely "maintained" — a masters athlete who is still getting stronger is doing more for long-term function than one who has simply stopped losing ground.

38.6Body Size And Scaling

Absolute and relative measures of the same underlying quality can rank athletes in opposite orders, and which one matters depends entirely on the task. A larger athlete generally produces more absolute force and more absolute power — useful in shot put, in a scrum, in an unweighted sprint — but carries more mass to move, which penalises them in any event where the athlete's own body weight must be lifted, accelerated or carried, such as climbing, distance running, or any weight-category sport. Relative measures (strength or power per kilogram of body mass) favour smaller athletes in exactly these tasks, which is why scaling the right measure to the right event, rather than defaulting to absolute numbers because they are easier to measure, changes which athlete actually looks "better" on paper.

38.7Clinical Populations

The guiding principle across every clinical condition this book touches on — cardiovascular disease, diabetes, pregnancy, osteoporosis, a healing injury — is the same: exercise prescription is modified, not withheld. The default response to a clinical condition should not be to stop exercising, but to adjust intensity, mode, volume or supervision to match what the specific condition and the specific individual can currently tolerate, on the understanding that the dose-response relationship covered in chapter 13 and the longevity evidence in chapter 39 both apply to clinical populations as much as to healthy ones, often more so.

Where this goes wrong

This chapter describes population-level evidence and general principles. It is not a substitute for individualised clinical assessment, and anyone with an existing medical condition, injury, or significant health concern should involve an appropriately qualified clinician in decisions about exercise prescription.

Five things to carry forward

  1. Individual response to identical training varies enormously (HERITAGE: roughly −5% to +48% VO2max on the same dose), but most "non-response" resolves with a different dose rather than reflecting a fixed ceiling.
  2. Sex differences in performance (roughly 10–30% at the elite level) reflect real physiological differences in fibre type, fuel use and fatigue resistance, but relative trainability from a given baseline appears equal between sexes.
  3. Over half of elite female athletes report their cycle affects training, yet controlled performance studies find no consistent, direction-stable effect — individual tracking and symptom management are defensible; a rigid phase-based template for everyone is not.
  4. Resistance training is well supported as safe for children and adolescents under proper supervision, and early single-sport specialisation carries real overuse-injury and burnout costs worth weighing against any early-development advantage.
  5. Power declines faster than endurance with age, anabolic resistance raises the per-meal protein target to roughly 0.3–0.4 g/kg, and intensity and strength work become more important with age, not less.
