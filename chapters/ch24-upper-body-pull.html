Upper Body — Pull

Lats, traps, rhomboids, biceps and grip

Ask someone to mime "pulling" and almost everyone does the same two things: they either haul an imaginary rope down from overhead, or they row an imaginary oar in toward the ribs. Those are genuinely different movements, loading different parts of the back, and a programme that only does one of them is only half-trained. This chapter works through the pulling muscles the way chapter 22 worked through the legs and chapter 23 worked through pressing: attachment, action, fibre mix, the long-length position where growth is best driven, best exercises, and how each muscle typically fails.

24.1Latissimus Dorsi — Vertical Pulling's Prime Mover

The latissimus dorsi ("lat") is the largest muscle in the upper body by surface area, a broad sheet running from the lower spine, the back of the pelvis and the lower ribs all the way up to a single tendon on the upper arm bone, tucked into the armpit. Despite covering most of the back, it does a simple job: it pulls the raised arm down and in toward the body — shoulder extension and adduction — and rotates the arm inward. Every vertical pull, from a pull-up to a lat pulldown, is the lat doing exactly that.

Because the lat's fibres run from a fixed origin on the trunk up to a single point on the arm, the muscle reaches its longest length when the arm is stretched fully overhead — the top of a pull-up, the dead-hang start of a pulldown. A pulldown or pull-up that stops short of a full overhead stretch, which is common when lifters chase heavier loads by shortening the range, trains the lat through its shorter, stronger portion and skips the position where the mechanical-tension signal for growth is largest. A straight-arm pulldown, where the elbow stays nearly locked and the whole movement happens at the shoulder, isolates this long-length stretch deliberately and is worth including even though the load it can handle is modest.

In plain terms

Picture the lat as a pair of broad curtains hanging from the back and gathered into one cord under each armpit. Pulling the cord down is exactly what a pull-up does. But the curtain only feels the full pull along its whole length if it was hanging open — arm stretched overhead — before you pulled. Start the pull with the curtain already half-gathered, as a short-range pulldown does, and the bottom half of the fabric barely moves.

24.2Trapezius — One Muscle, Three Genuinely Different Jobs

Trapezius is often trained as if it were one thing, usually via a shrug, which only ever addresses a third of it. The muscle is a single diamond-shaped sheet from the base of the skull to the mid-back, but its upper, middle and lower thirds pull in different directions on the shoulder blade and are loaded by different exercises almost entirely.

Upper trapezius elevates the shoulder blade — a shrug — and extends the neck. It is heavily loaded by any carrying or shrugging movement and, in most people, it is already tense rather than weak: years of carrying bags on one shoulder and sitting with the neck slightly forward keep it in a state of low-grade, chronic activation. It responds to being trained loaded and long — a heavy farmer carry, where the weight pulls the shoulder blade down and the muscle works eccentrically to resist — far better than to stretching or manual pressure, which address the symptom of tightness without changing the loading pattern that caused it.

Middle trapezius, working with the rhomboids immediately beneath it, retracts the shoulder blade — pulls it toward the spine, the finishing position of a row. Lower trapezius depresses the shoulder blade and, together with serratus anterior (chapter 23), helps rotate it upward during overhead reaching. It is consistently the weak link in people with overhead shoulder pain, and the mismatch is usually a programming one: lower trapezius needs low load held through a long range — a prone Y-raise, a slow face pull finished high and wide — not heavy rowing, which recruits it only as a minor assistant to the much larger lat and middle trapezius.

Activate it

Training the two-thirds of trapezius that shrugs never reaches.

  • Do Prone Y-raise or band pull-apart finished high and wide, for lower and middle trapezius and the rhomboids.
  • Load Light — a few kilograms or a light band. The point is a long isometric hold at full retraction and depression, not maximal weight.
  • Dose 3 sets of 10–15 reps with a 1–2 second pause at the top, 2–3 times weekly.
  • When Any session; it is low-fatigue accessory work and pairs well after a pulling or pressing day.
  • Progress Lengthen the pause before adding load.
  • On the bike An indoor-cycling session delivers essentially none of this stimulus — the torso is static and the arms bear weight rather than retracting the shoulder blade, so this gap needs dedicated land-based work regardless of cycling volume.
  • Signal You're doing it right when you can hold the top position for the full pause without the neck or upper trapezius taking over.

24.3Posterior Deltoid & Levator Scapulae

The posterior deltoid, on the back edge of the shoulder, extends the arm and pulls it horizontally away from the body — the motion of a reverse fly or the finish of a wide row. It works alongside the lat and middle trapezius in most pulling exercises but is rarely the limiting factor in a heavy row, which means programmes built entirely around rows and pull-ups under-train it relative to the anterior deltoid, which gets saturated by every pressing movement (chapter 23). A dedicated reverse fly or face pull, with the arm reaching across the body before the pull begins, closes that gap and trains the posterior deltoid near its long-length position.

Levator scapulae, a thin strap running from the top two or three neck vertebrae to the top corner of the shoulder blade, elevates the shoulder blade alongside upper trapezius and is rarely discussed because it is rarely trained in isolation — and rarely needs to be. It becomes relevant mainly as a source of neck and upper-shoulder tightness in people who sit for long periods with the head held slightly forward, which keeps it under low-level chronic tension for the same reason upper trapezius suffers the same fate. Loaded carries that require the shoulder blade to stay actively set, rather than stretching the neck passively, address both muscles together.

24.4Biceps Brachii & Brachialis — Why Grip and Elbow Position Change Who Does the Work

The biceps crosses two joints — the shoulder and the elbow — and has two separate jobs: it flexes the elbow and it supinates the forearm, rotating the palm to face upward. Both jobs depend on hand and arm position, which is why the same basic motion, curling a weight toward the shoulder, can load the biceps very differently depending on how it is set up. A chin-up, gripped with palms facing the body (supinated), recruits the biceps heavily because the position suits both of its jobs at once. A pull-up, gripped palms-away (pronated), takes the forearm out of supination and shifts more of the elbow-flexion work onto brachialis, a muscle that lies underneath the biceps, crosses the elbow only, and flexes it with exactly the same force regardless of forearm rotation.

Because the biceps crosses the shoulder as well as the elbow, it also follows the same two-joint rule met already in rectus femoris and the hamstrings (chapter 22): it reaches long length only when the elbow is straight and the shoulder is extended at the same time — the arm trailing behind the torso, as in an incline curl performed on a bench angled back, or the bottom of a dip. A standard curl, performed with the arm hanging straight down at the side, stretches the elbow joint but not the shoulder, so it never reaches the biceps' true long length. A preacher curl, by contrast, flexes the shoulder forward onto the pad, which shortens the biceps at the shoulder end even as the elbow straightens — useful for peak contraction, but the opposite length condition from an incline curl. Training both covers the muscle's full range properly.

Worked example
Farida, 27, does chin-ups and standing barbell curls twice weekly, wants bigger arms, and has plateaued for two months.
  1. Chin-ups and standing curls both keep the shoulder roughly neutral — neither stretches the biceps at the shoulder end.0 sets currently at true long length
  2. Add an incline curl, bench set to 45–60°, arm trailing behind the torso at the bottom.3 sets × 8–10 reps
  3. Keep the standing curl for a short-to-mid-length, heavier stimulus; it is not redundant, just incomplete alone.Retain 3 sets × 8–10 reps
  4. Add a pronated (palms-away) pull-up variant or hammer curl to load brachialis directly, which chin-ups alone under-train.2 sets × 8–12 reps, hammer grip
  5. Reassess arm circumference after one full hypertrophy block.≈8–10 weeks
So: Farida's plateau was a range-of-length gap, not a volume problem; adding one long-length exercise and one brachialis-biased grip variant covers what two short-length exercises, however heavy, could not.

24.5Grip — The Usual Limiter in Pulling

The forearm flexors, running from the inner elbow to the wrist and fingers, close the hand and are collectively what most people mean by "grip." They are slow-dominant and extremely fatigue-resistant — a hand has to maintain its hold through thousands of daily actions without rest — which also means they need high-volume, frequent training rather than the low-rep, high-load approach suited to a fast-twitch muscle. In almost every heavy pulling exercise — a deadlift, a heavy row, a dead-hang pull-up — the forearm flexors fatigue before the lat, the biggest and strongest muscle in the chain, comes close to its own limit. Grip is the usual bottleneck in pulling, not the back.

The forearm extensors, on the back of the forearm, open the hand and stabilise the wrist against the flexors' pull; they are trained far less often and are relevant mainly as the muscles irritated in lateral elbow pain from chronic, unbalanced gripping.

The paper
Leong DP, Teo KK, Rangarajan S, et al. (PURE study investigators). Prognostic value of grip strength: findings from the Prospective Urban Rural Epidemiology (PURE) study. Lancet 2015;386(9990):266–273.
Who

139,691 adults across 17 countries, followed for a median of four years.

What

Grip strength measured by hand dynamometer at baseline; all-cause mortality, cardiovascular mortality and major cardiovascular events tracked prospectively.

Found

Each 5 kg reduction in grip strength was associated with a 16% higher risk of all-cause mortality and a 17% higher risk of cardiovascular mortality — a stronger predictor than systolic blood pressure in the same cohort.

Why it matters: grip strength is cheap to measure and behaves as a readable marker of overall muscular and physiological reserve, not a claim that gripping harder itself extends life. Limitation: an observational association across a huge, varied population — it cannot show that training grip specifically changes mortality risk, only that weak grip tracks with broader frailty and health status.

That limitation matters for what you do with the finding. The sensible reading is not "train grip and live longer," which the data cannot support, but that grip strength is a cheap, practical marker worth tracking alongside the training that builds it anyway — carries, dead hangs, heavy rows — as part of training pulling properly rather than as a target in its own right.

24.6Vertical vs Horizontal Pulling — Why You Need Both

A pull-up and a row look like cousins, but they load the back differently enough that one does not substitute for the other. A vertical pull brings a fixed point (the bar) toward a moving body, with the arm travelling from overhead down to the side — the lat's primary action, with upper trapezius and the lower trapezius/serratus pairing managing the shoulder blade's upward rotation at the top. A horizontal pull brings a weight toward a stationary torso, with the arm travelling from in front of the body to beside it — here the middle trapezius and rhomboids do proportionally more of the work, retracting the shoulder blade through a range a vertical pull barely uses.

Myth vs fact
“Pull-ups are the complete back exercise — rows are optional extra”

A common shortcut in time-pressed programmes.

Each covers roughly half the picture

Pull-ups load the lat through a long overhead range and train upper trapezius and lower trapezius/serratus coordination at the top. They do comparatively little for middle trapezius and rhomboid retraction, which only a horizontal pull trains through a full range. A programme with only vertical pulling typically shows it as rounded shoulders that strength training alone does not fix — not because the lifter is weak, but because half the scapular job was never loaded.

Five things to carry forward

  1. The lat reaches its longest, most growth-responsive length with the arm fully overhead, which a shortened-range pulldown or pull-up never reaches however heavy the load.
  2. Trapezius is three muscles doing three different jobs — a shrug trains only the elevating upper third, and the usual weak link, lower trapezius, needs low-load long holds rather than heavy rowing.
  3. Biceps and brachialis share the elbow-flexion job but respond differently to forearm rotation and shoulder position, so chin-ups, curls and pronated-grip pulling each train a genuinely different balance of the two.
  4. Grip is the usual limiter in pulling, not the back, and it needs the same high-volume, fatigue-resistant training approach as the soleus rather than low-rep heavy work.
  5. Vertical and horizontal pulling load the shoulder blade in different directions — overhead pulling upward-rotates it, rowing retracts it — and a programme needs both, not one treated as a complete substitute for the other.
