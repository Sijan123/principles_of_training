Training in Heat, Cold & Altitude

Environmental stress as a training tool

Thirteen elite American runners went to live at altitude in 1997 so that scientists could finally settle an argument: does training high actually make you faster low down, or does it just make you slower at altitude and call it a camp? The answer split the group in two, and the split had almost nothing to do with how hard anyone trained. This chapter is about using the air you breathe, the heat you tolerate and the cold you endure as deliberate training tools — and about the uncomfortable amount of individual variation hiding inside each one.

37.1Heat As A Deliberate Stimulus

Chapter 11 already covered the physiology of heat strain in detail — how evaporation dominates heat loss during exercise, why cardiovascular drift pushes heart rate upward at an unchanged workload in warm conditions, and how the first wave of heat adaptation (plasma volume expansion of roughly 8–15% within days, earlier and more dilute sweating, a lower heart rate at the same external work) unfolds over the course of about a week (chapter 11). This chapter builds on that foundation rather than repeating it: the question here is not what heat acclimation does, but how to deliberately schedule it as a training block with a specific event in mind.

A full heat acclimation protocol runs 10–14 days, with 60–90 minutes of exercise in the heat each day — long enough and hot enough to raise core temperature to roughly 38.5–39 °C and hold it there for a sustained period, which is the actual trigger for adaptation, not simply "feeling hot" for a few minutes. Most of the measurable benefit — the plasma volume expansion, the drop in heart rate at fixed power, the earlier sweat onset — is in place within the first 5–7 days, with sweat-sodium conservation and the full suite of adaptations continuing to refine over the second week. A

Few amateur athletes have access to a genuinely hot training environment on demand, which is where the practical versions earn their place. A post-session sauna (15–20 minutes immediately after a normal training session, while core temperature is already elevated) or a hot bath of similar duration both reliably drive meaningful heat adaptation, because what the body is responding to is sustained core temperature elevation, not the specific source of the heat. The most accessible version of all for anyone who already owns one is an indoor trainer in a warm room: close the window, turn the fan down or off, and ride at a modest, sustainable intensity for the session's duration — the controlled, consistent conditions that make the trainer valuable for interval work (chapter 44) make it equally valuable for deliberately engineering a heat stimulus.

37.2The Heat–Altitude Link

The most striking recent finding in this area, covered in full in chapter 11, is that heat and altitude are not two separate adaptations sitting side by side — they appear to interact. The plasma volume expansion that heat acclimation drives dilutes the concentration of haemoglobin per litre of blood, and that dilution appears to be sensed by the kidney's oxygen-sensing machinery as a drop in effective oxygen delivery, nudging erythropoietin signalling upward through the same renal pathway altitude exposure uses. In the study detailed in chapter 11, athletes who combined heat exposure with altitude training retained significantly more of their altitude-induced haemoglobin mass gains after returning to sea level than a control group training at altitude alone, whose gains largely faded over the following days. C — a recent, promising finding, not yet settled practice.

The practical implication for this chapter is simple to state even though the mechanism is still being worked out: a short heat block layered onto the end of an altitude camp, or even run independently around a period of hard training, may help lock in haematological gains that would otherwise be lost within days of coming back down. It is one more reason heat acclimation deserves a place in a serious endurance programme even for athletes who have no plans to go anywhere near altitude at all.

37.3Altitude — Live High, Train Low

At altitude, lower atmospheric pressure means fewer oxygen molecules in each breath of air, even though the percentage of oxygen in the air itself (roughly 21%) is unchanged. The acute effects are immediate and uncomfortable: resting and exercising heart rate rise, maximal oxygen uptake falls (roughly 1% for every 100 m above about 1,500 m, accelerating above 2,500 m), and the same pace or power output feels much harder than it does at sea level. These acute effects are why training performance at altitude is almost always worse than at sea level — which is precisely the problem the live high, train low (LHTL) strategy was designed to solve.

The logic of LHTL is straightforward once stated: sleep and spend most non-training hours at altitude, where the hypoxic stimulus drives the haematological adaptations below, but descend to train the actual hard sessions at a lower altitude, where oxygen availability allows training intensity and quality to stay close to sea-level standard. The alternative — living and training continuously at altitude — gets the haematological benefit but at the cost of training quality throughout the whole camp, which for many athletes erases much of what the blood adaptations would otherwise buy.

Given sufficient altitude (typically 2,000–2,500 m) and sufficient daily hours of exposure (generally cited as at least 12–16 hours a day, most practically achieved by sleeping there), haemoglobin mass rises by roughly 1–4% over 3–4 weeks B — a figure consistent across several controlled studies, with the rate of increase running at roughly 1% per week of exposure and accumulating toward the upper end of that range by three to four weeks. This happens alongside a separate, faster adaptation moving the opposite direction: plasma volume contracts in the first days at altitude, which concentrates existing haemoglobin and gives an early, partly illusory sense that blood oxygen-carrying capacity has already improved, before the genuine, slower haemoglobin mass increase has had time to accrue underneath it.

In plain terms

Live high, train low works like studying at altitude and sitting the exam at sea level.

The body you sleep in overnight is quietly building more red blood cells in response to thin air, the same way a student absorbs material during undisturbed study time. But you would not want to sit the exam — train your hardest, most important sessions — gasping for oxygen at the same altitude. Training low keeps the "exam" (the actual hard session) at full sea-level quality, while the "studying" (the blood adaptation) keeps happening quietly in the background, overnight, where its cost to training quality is lowest.

The complication that makes altitude camps expensive gambles rather than reliable investments is the responder/non-responder problem: given an identical altitude exposure, some athletes show large haemoglobin mass gains and some show almost none, and the original LHTL trial found this split tracked individual differences in erythropoietin response to the hypoxic stimulus — some athletes' kidneys respond briskly to reduced oxygen delivery, others barely respond at all, for reasons not fully explained by training status or starting fitness. B Crucially, performance gains tracked the haematological response, not the training itself: the athletes whose EPO and haemoglobin mass rose were the ones who ran faster afterward; those whose blood markers barely moved saw little performance benefit despite completing the identical camp.

None of this works without adequate iron status, for the same reason given in chapter 11: building new haemoglobin needs iron as raw material, and an athlete arriving at altitude already iron-deficient — common in endurance athletes and particularly in menstruating women — will blunt or entirely block the haematological response regardless of how favourable their EPO signalling otherwise is. Ferritin screening before committing to an altitude block is standard practice for exactly this reason (chapter 31).

The paper
Levine BD, Stray-Gundersen J. "Living high-training low": effect of moderate-altitude acclimatization with low-altitude training on performance. Journal of Applied Physiology 1997;83(1):102–12.
Who

39 well-trained collegiate distance runners.

What

Four weeks living at roughly 2,500 m while training at either high altitude, low altitude (LHTL), or sea level throughout, with 5,000 m time trial performance and VO2max measured before and after.

Found

Only the live-high, train-low group improved both VO2max and 5,000 m race time; the live-high, train-high group gained similar haematological benefit but did not improve performance, because training quality had suffered too much at altitude. Within the LHTL group, performance improvement tracked individual erythropoietin and red-cell-volume response.

Why it matters: it established, in a controlled design, that altitude's value comes specifically from separating where you sleep from where you train, and that the benefit is gated by an individual haematological response rather than guaranteed by the exposure itself. Limitation: one population (collegiate runners), one altitude, one duration — later work has refined the minimum hours and altitude needed, and individual response remains, decades later, the field's least-resolved problem.

37.4Cold

Cold has a mixed performance signature. Mild cold can modestly improve endurance performance by reducing the heat-storage problem described above — less of the body's effort goes into shedding heat, so more of it is available for the work itself — but once muscle temperature itself drops, force production, conduction velocity in nerves and the elastic properties of tendon all decline, which is why a poorly warmed-up cold-weather effort so often feels stiff and underpowered regardless of fitness.

Cold-induced vasoconstriction — blood vessels in the skin and extremities narrowing to conserve core heat — protects the body's core at the direct expense of the hands and feet, which is why fine motor control and grip strength degrade noticeably in cold conditions well before anything resembling a safety concern. In long events in genuinely cold or wet-cold conditions, the real risk escalates to hypothermia — a falling core temperature that, left unchecked, progressively impairs judgement, coordination and the ability to recognise the problem in oneself — which is the reason ultra-distance and open-water event organisers take wind chill and water temperature as seriously as they take heat indices.

It is worth separating two uses of cold that get run together in casual conversation. Cold exposure for adaptation — training in genuinely cold conditions to build tolerance and pacing skill for a cold-weather event — is a specificity argument, not a recovery one: you train in the cold because you will race in the cold, for the same SAID-principle reasons covered in chapter 13. Cold water immersion for recovery, by contrast, is the post-exercise ice bath discussed in chapter 36, which speeds short-term recovery of force output but can blunt long-term hypertrophy and strength adaptation when used habitually after resistance training. The two share a word and very little else; conflating them is how an athlete ends up icing a muscle that needed the opposite stimulus.

37.5Blood Flow Restriction Training

Blood flow restriction (BFR) training applies a pneumatic cuff to the top of a limb, inflated to a pressure that substantially restricts venous outflow while still permitting arterial inflow, and then performs resistance exercise at low loads — typically 20–40% of 1RM — against that partial occlusion. The trapped blood and the resulting build-up of metabolites (lactate, hydrogen ions) and cell swelling inside the working muscle appear to recruit a disproportionate hypertrophic and signalling response for the load actually lifted, a mechanism distinct from the mechanical-tension route that drives adaptation from heavy conventional lifting (chapter 17).

Multiple systematic reviews and meta-analyses have found that low-load BFR training produces muscle hypertrophy comparable to conventional heavy-load resistance training, and clearly superior to the same low load lifted without occlusion A. Strength outcomes are a more mixed picture: some comparisons show BFR nearly matching heavy training, others show a modest shortfall, and the balance of evidence suggests BFR produces reliably greater strength gains than low-load training alone but slightly smaller gains than genuinely heavy loading for maximal strength specifically. B

The real value of BFR is not as a replacement for heavy lifting in a healthy, uninjured athlete who can simply lift heavy — it is in the much larger population for whom heavy loading is contraindicated or currently impossible: post-surgical rehabilitation, a joint that cannot yet tolerate high mechanical load, older adults for whom heavy barbell work carries more risk relative to benefit, or anyone returning from injury who needs to maintain muscle mass while the tissue itself is not ready for heavy load (chapter 42). In exactly these situations, BFR offers a way to drive a genuine hypertrophy stimulus from loads light enough to be joint-friendly, which heavy training by definition cannot.

Where this goes wrong

BFR is generally well tolerated in supervised research settings, but applying tourniquet-level pressure to a limb is not risk-free: it is contraindicated in anyone with a history of blood clots, significant cardiovascular disease, or peripheral vascular disease, and pressure that is too high, or cuffs that are not purpose-built and pressure-controlled, create a genuine safety problem rather than a training one. This is a technique to learn from a qualified practitioner or validated device, not to improvise with a household tourniquet, and anyone with a relevant medical history should seek clinical clearance first.

37.6Air Quality And Pollution As Training Decisions

Air quality is an environmental training stress that gets far less attention than heat, cold or altitude despite being, for many urban athletes, the most frequently encountered of the four. Fine particulate matter (PM2.5) and ground-level ozone both rise with ventilation rate during exercise — breathing harder and deeper simply draws more pollutant deeper into the lungs — and higher short-term exposure during exercise is associated with measurable reductions in lung function and, in sensitive individuals, with acute respiratory symptoms. B The practical decision is a simple substitution rule rather than a reason to stop training altogether: on days when a local air-quality index is flagged poor or worse, move intense outdoor sessions indoors, or shift an outdoor session to early morning when traffic-related pollution is typically lower, rather than defaulting to "train hard regardless."

37.7Travel And Jet Lag

Crossing time zones disrupts the circadian rhythm that governs core temperature, hormone release and sleep timing — the same system that sleep hygiene in chapter 36 is built around protecting. As a rough guide, full circadian re-entrainment takes about one day per time zone crossed, somewhat faster travelling west than east, because the body's internal clock finds it easier to lengthen a day than shorten one. For a competition that matters, the practical options are arriving early enough for full adaptation (the most reliable, least available option for most amateur athletes), or arriving as close to competition time as logistics allow and competing on home-time physiology before jet lag has had time to set in — the worst window is usually the middle few days after a long-haul flight, when the old rhythm has broken down but the new one has not yet taken hold.

So: Farah's heat acclimation is a 10-day block of daily indoor-trainer sessions in a heated, unventilated room, scheduled three to four weeks out so the adaptation is fresh but not decayed by race day — and her falling heart rate at a fixed power is the number that tells her it is working, well before race day confirms it.

Five things to carry forward

  1. A full heat acclimation block runs 10–14 days of 60–90 minutes daily, and an indoor trainer in a warm, unventilated room is one of the most practical and controllable ways to deliver it.
  2. Heat and altitude interact: heat's plasma volume expansion appears to nudge the same renal oxygen-sensing pathway altitude uses, and heat exposure has been shown to help preserve altitude-induced haemoglobin gains that would otherwise fade after descent.
  3. Live-high, train-low altitude training can raise haemoglobin mass by roughly 1–4% over 3–4 weeks, but the gain is gated by individual erythropoietin responsiveness and by adequate iron status — and performance improvement tracks the haematological response, not just the camp completed.
  4. Blood flow restriction training can produce hypertrophy comparable to heavy loading from loads as low as 20–40% of 1RM, making it most valuable precisely where heavy loading is contraindicated — rehabilitation, injury, and some older or clinical populations.
  5. Cold exposure for adaptation and cold water immersion for recovery share a word but serve opposite purposes — one is specificity training for a cold event, the other is a recovery tool with a real adaptation cost when used habitually after lifting.
