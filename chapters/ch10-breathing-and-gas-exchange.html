Breathing & Gas Exchange

Ventilation, diffusion, and whether lungs ever limit you

A healthy pair of lungs has roughly ten times more surface area for gas exchange than it will ever need during exercise, and a resting breath uses perhaps 5% of your total oxygen uptake to power itself. That spare capacity is the reason most people never feel their lungs as the limiting factor in anything — the legs or the heart give out first. But "most people" is not "everyone": push an elite endurance engine hard enough and the lungs stop being a bystander. This chapter is about where that spare capacity comes from, what it is for, and the narrow circumstances in which it runs out.

10.1The Mechanics Of A Breath

Breathing is not the lungs doing something; the lungs are passive bags of elastic tissue with no muscle of their own. Air moves because the space around the lungs changes volume, and the lungs are forced to follow by the pressure difference that creates. The main engine for that volume change is the diaphragm, a dome-shaped sheet of muscle slung under the lungs. When it contracts, it flattens and drops, like a plunger being pulled down a syringe barrel, and the chest cavity gets taller. The external intercostal muscles, running between the ribs, assist by lifting the rib cage up and out, widening the cavity from the sides — picture a bucket handle swinging up and out rather than lying flat. Together these two do essentially all the work of a quiet breath at rest.

Breathing out at rest costs almost nothing: the chest wall and stretched lung tissue simply spring back to their resting size, the way a stretched elastic band snaps back once you let go. There is no active muscular effort in a resting exhale at all. That changes the moment effort rises. During hard exercise, the accessory muscles join in on both ends of the breath: the sternocleidomastoid and scalenes in the neck help haul the rib cage up further on inhalation, while the internal intercostals and the abdominal muscles actively squeeze air out on exhalation, because passive recoil alone can no longer move air fast enough. Hard breathing visibly recruiting the neck, shoulders and abdomen is not panic — it is simply more muscle switched on to move more air, faster.

Two numbers describe how much air moves: tidal volume, the amount of air in a single breath, and breathing rate, how many breaths per minute. Their product is minute ventilation (V̇E), the total air moved per minute. At the start of exercise, and through low-to-moderate intensities, tidal volume does almost all of the early work — it can roughly triple from a resting 0.4–0.5 L toward 2–2.5 L before breathing rate climbs very much at all. Only as intensity climbs further, and tidal volume starts to run into the practical ceiling set by lung size and the awkward mechanics of a very deep breath, does breathing rate take over as the main driver, climbing from a resting 12–15 breaths per minute to 40–50 or more at exhaustion. The practical upshot: deepen first, then quicken — which is exactly the order the body chooses on its own, because a deep slow breath moves more air per unit of muscular effort than a shallow fast one.

In plain terms

Think of filling a bucket with a hose. You can fill it with a few big, slow pours, or with lots of small, fast splashes.

The big slow pour is more efficient — less water sprayed everywhere, less effort per litre delivered. That is tidal volume. Once the bucket is nearly full and you are rushing, you switch to fast splashing — that is breathing rate taking over once tidal volume nears its ceiling.

10.2Gas Exchange At The Alveolus

Air finally arrives at roughly 300–500 million tiny sacs called alveoli, each wrapped in a mesh of capillaries so fine that a red blood cell and a breath of air are separated by a wall often under half a micrometre thick — thinner than a soap bubble. Gas moves across that wall by simple diffusion: molecules travel from where they are concentrated to where they are not, no pump required. What drives it is a partial-pressure gradient — each gas in a mixture behaves as if it alone exerted part of the total pressure, and it diffuses down its own gradient regardless of what the other gases are doing. Oxygen is at high partial pressure in fresh alveolar air and low partial pressure in the blood arriving from the body, so it moves in; carbon dioxide (CO2) is the mirror image, high in the blood and low in the air, so it moves out. Both cross in a fraction of a second, well within the roughly three-quarters of a second a red blood cell spends transiting the lung capillary at rest — a transit time that shortens considerably during hard exercise, the seed of a problem covered later in this chapter.

Once oxygen is in the blood, most of it latches onto haemoglobin, the iron-containing protein packed into red blood cells. The relationship between how much oxygen is available (the partial pressure of oxygen in blood, PaO2) and how saturated haemoglobin becomes is not a straight line; it is an S-shaped curve called the oxyhaemoglobin dissociation curve. The flat top of the S means haemoglobin stays nearly fully saturated across a wide range of oxygen pressures in the lung — a built-in safety margin. The steep middle section is where the curve does its real work: in contracting muscle, where oxygen pressure has fallen, a small further drop in pressure now releases a large amount of oxygen from haemoglobin.

The curve itself shifts position depending on local conditions, and this is where the physiology gets genuinely elegant rather than merely descriptive. Heat, a rise in CO2, and a rise in acidity (falling pH) all shift the curve to the right — haemoglobin releases oxygen more readily at any given pressure. This is called the Bohr effect once CO2 and pH are the cause. It is worth being explicit about why this is a feature and not damage: a working muscle is, by definition, hot, acidic and CO2-rich compared with resting tissue, and those are precisely the three signals that tell haemoglobin "release more oxygen here, right now, where it is needed." The same shift reverses in the lung, where CO2 is being exhaled and conditions are cooler, helping haemoglobin load up fully before it leaves. The curve does not malfunction under exercise stress; it reads the stress as an address and delivers there.

10.3Oxygen Transport In Blood — Why Haemoglobin Is Almost Everything

Oxygen barely dissolves in blood plasma on its own — roughly 1.5% of total oxygen content travels dissolved. The other ~98% rides on haemoglobin A. This single fact is why anaemia, a shortage of haemoglobin or red blood cells, is so devastating to endurance performance specifically, even when the lungs, heart and muscles are all functioning normally. A lung with healthy alveoli can still saturate the haemoglobin that arrives; the problem is that there simply is not enough haemoglobin arriving to carry a normal oxygen load. Cardiac output can rise to compensate up to a point — the heart beats faster and harder to push the same, now oxygen-poor, blood around more times per minute — but this is an expensive patch, not a fix, and it is why anaemic athletes report the same effort feeling disproportionately hard well before any muscular or cardiac disease is present. Iron status matters here directly, because iron sits at the centre of the haemoglobin molecule; this connects forward to the slower haematological adaptations covered in chapter 11.

10.4Ventilation During Exercise — VT1 And VT2

As exercise intensity rises from easy to maximal, minute ventilation does not climb in a straight line. It tracks closely with two breakpoints that testing laboratories use as practical markers of metabolic state, called the ventilatory thresholds.

  1. VT1 — the first breakpoint

    Ventilation starts rising slightly faster than oxygen uptake. Physiologically this tracks the point where blood lactate first begins to accumulate above resting levels — often called LT1 in chapter 7's language — because the mild rise in acidity and CO2 production starts to stimulate breathing a little beyond what oxygen demand alone would require. This is roughly the top of comfortable, conversational-pace exercise.

  2. VT2 — the second breakpoint

    Ventilation rises sharply and disproportionately relative to both oxygen uptake and CO2 output. This marks the point where lactate and acid are now accumulating faster than the body can buffer and clear them, forcing a large additional ventilatory drive to blow off the extra CO2 this buffering produces. This closely tracks LT2, the second lactate threshold, and the pace here is sustainable for perhaps 30–60 minutes at most.

Why measure breathing instead of pricking a finger for lactate directly? Because a face mask measuring expired air gives a continuous, needle-free readout of both thresholds in a single incremental test, which is why ventilatory threshold testing is a mainstay of laboratory fitness assessment covered fully in chapter 35. The two methods agree well but not perfectly B, and a good lab reports both where possible.

Owen, 41, 78 kg, recreational cyclist, resting tidal volume 0.45 L at 14 breaths/min, tested maximum tidal volume 2.9 L at 48 breaths/min.

So: Owen's minute ventilation rises roughly 22-fold from rest to maximum, and the air-moving muscles that make that possible go from costing him a few percent of his oxygen uptake at rest to perhaps one-tenth or more of it at his limit — a real, measurable tax that grows fastest exactly when he can least afford it.

10.5Do Lungs Ever Limit Performance?

For the overwhelming majority of people, the answer is a clean no. A healthy respiratory system has so much spare diffusing capacity that arterial blood leaves the lung almost fully saturated with oxygen even at maximal effort, and ventilation rarely reaches its mechanical ceiling before the legs or heart call a halt. In untrained and moderately trained people, breathing hard is uncomfortable, not limiting — a true signal of effort, not evidence the lungs are the bottleneck.

Three situations complicate that reassurance, and they matter mainly at the very top of the sport.

  • Exercise-induced arterial hypoxaemia (EIAH). In a substantial minority of elite endurance athletes — estimates run to roughly 40–50% of highly trained cyclists and runners in some samples B — arterial oxygen saturation actually falls during maximal exercise, instead of staying pinned near 97–98%. The leading explanation is mechanical: enormous cardiac outputs push blood through the lung capillaries too fast for gas exchange to fully complete, compounded in some by ventilation-perfusion mismatch and, in a few, a genuine diffusion limitation. Each 1-point fall in saturation below about 95% costs roughly 1–2% of VO2max B — the lungs have run out of spare capacity and become a genuine limit.
  • Respiratory muscle fatigue. The diaphragm and intercostals are muscles, and like any other muscle under prolonged heavy load they can fatigue. When they do, a reflex response — often called the respiratory muscle metaboreflex — triggers sympathetic nervous activity that constricts blood vessels in the limbs, redirecting blood toward the fatiguing respiratory muscles and away from working legs or arms B.
  • Work of breathing competing for cardiac output. This is the mechanism behind the worked example above stated as a systemic cost: at very high ventilation rates, the respiratory muscles can consume 10–15% of total VO2 and a comparable share of cardiac output C. Every litre of blood sent to the diaphragm is a litre not available to the quadriceps.

Pioneering work from Jerome Dempsey's laboratory established both EIAH and the respiratory metaboreflex as real, measurable constraints specifically in elite athletes. The honest summary: for a recreational or sub-elite athlete, training the lungs directly is rarely the highest-value use of time; for a genuine elite already near the ceiling of cardiac and muscular capacity, the respiratory system can become one of the last remaining margins.

The paper
Dempsey JA, Hanson PG, Henderson KS. Exercise-induced arterial hypoxaemia in healthy human subjects at sea level. J Physiol 1984;355:161–75.
Who

Highly trained endurance athletes with very high VO2max values, studied at sea level.

What

Arterial blood sampled directly during progressive maximal exercise to measure oxygen saturation and partial pressure, rather than relying on indirect estimates.

Found

A meaningful subset of these elite athletes showed significant arterial desaturation during maximal exercise, something essentially never seen in untrained subjects, implicating pulmonary gas-exchange limitation as a genuine ceiling in highly trained individuals.

Why it matters: it was the first clear demonstration that the lungs can become the limiting organ, overturning the assumption that respiratory reserve is effectively unlimited for everyone. Limitation: the athletes studied were an unusually fit, self-selected sample; EIAH is not representative of recreational exercisers and should not be generalised as a reason to worry about lung function at ordinary training intensities.

10.6Breathing Practices — What Has Evidence And What Doesn't

Breathing has attracted more folk technique than almost any other system in this book, partly because it is the one physiological process most people can consciously control. Separating what helps from what is theatre matters.

Myth vs fact
“Nasal breathing during exercise improves oxygen uptake and should replace mouth breathing”

Breathing through the nose filters and warms air and is claimed to boost performance.

No performance benefit shown; a real ventilatory limiter

Nasal passages add useful humidification and filtering at rest and at very low intensities, but they also add substantial airflow resistance. At the ventilation rates hard exercise demands, nasal-only breathing cannot move enough air, full stop — it is not a matter of training the habit, it is a plumbing bottleneck. Controlled studies have not shown a performance or VO2max benefit from nasal breathing during exercise C, and mouth breathing exists specifically because the body needs the larger airway it provides once demand rises.

Myth vs fact
“Breath-hold training, by deliberately restricting oxygen, builds the same adaptations as altitude training”

Popularised by some swimming and freediving-adjacent programmes.

Different stimulus, different and much smaller effect

Voluntary breath-holding during exercise raises CO2 and drops pH sharply but does not reliably reproduce the sustained, whole-body hypoxaemia that drives real altitude adaptations such as increased haemoglobin mass (see chapter 11). Evidence for meaningful performance gains from breath-hold or hypoventilation training in swimmers is weak and inconsistent C; some protocols even impair technique and intensity during the sets where they are applied, trading quality work for a stimulus of uncertain value.

Set against those two, inspiratory muscle training (IMT) — breathing against a hand-held resistance device for a few minutes, several times a week — has a genuinely more defensible, if modest, evidence base. Meta-analyses find it reliably strengthens the inspiratory muscles and produces small but real gains in time-trial and constant-load performance, detected more consistently there than on simple incremental VO2max tests B. Benefits appear larger in less-fit individuals and longer events, smaller in already highly trained athletes. Best thought of as a low-cost accessory practice with a believable mechanism — reducing respiratory muscle fatigue and its blood-flow-stealing reflex — rather than a major performance lever.

Activate it

Inspiratory muscle training to reduce respiratory muscle fatigue and its metaboreflex, for endurance athletes who have otherwise exhausted bigger-ticket training levers.

  • Do Resisted inspiratory breathing using a hand-held flow- or pressure-resistance device.
  • Load Resistance set around 50% of maximal inspiratory pressure, reassessed every 2–4 weeks.
  • Dose 2 sets of 30 breaths, twice daily, 5–6 days a week.
  • When At least 4–6 weeks before expecting a measurable effect; most trial periods run 4–11 weeks.
  • On the bike No direct indoor-cycling substitute exists for the resisted-breathing stimulus itself, but a hard threshold session (e.g. 4 × 8 min at 90–95% FTP) trains the respiratory muscles incidentally through sustained high ventilation — useful, but not a substitute for the targeted resistance device.
  • Signal Maximal inspiratory pressure rising on retest, and a subjective sense that hard efforts no longer feel breathing-limited before the legs give out.
B Multiple meta-analyses show small but consistent constant-load and time-trial benefits; effect on VO2max itself is not reliably significant.

Five things to carry forward

  1. Tidal volume (breath depth) does most of the early work of raising ventilation during exercise; breathing rate only takes over once tidal volume nears its ceiling.
  2. The oxyhaemoglobin dissociation curve's rightward shift under heat, CO2 and acidity — the Bohr effect — is a useful delivery mechanism, releasing more oxygen exactly where working muscle is hottest and most acidic, not a sign of anything going wrong.
  3. Haemoglobin carries roughly 98% of blood oxygen, which is why anaemia specifically devastates endurance capacity even when heart, lungs and muscle are otherwise completely healthy.
  4. VT1 and VT2, the two breaks in the ventilation-versus-intensity curve, track the same two metabolic transitions as the lactate thresholds and give labs a needle-free way to test them.
  5. For almost everyone the lungs have large spare capacity and are never the limiting organ; only in a minority of elite endurance athletes does exercise-induced arterial hypoxaemia, respiratory muscle fatigue, or the rising oxygen cost of breathing itself become a genuine ceiling.
