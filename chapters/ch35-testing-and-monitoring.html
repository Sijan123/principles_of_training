Testing & Monitoring

Measuring the engine, the chassis and the readiness

A mechanic who plugs a car into a diagnostic reader and then does nothing with the printout has wasted the reader's time and the owner's money. Testing in training is no different. A number that does not change a decision — what session to do next, how hard to push this week, whether to see a doctor — was not worth the fatigue it cost to collect. This chapter is about the handful of tests that reliably earn their keep: what they measure, how to run them without fooling yourself, and the much longer list of popular numbers that mostly measure how much you paid for a wearable.

35.1Why Test At All

Every test in this chapter costs something. A maximal aerobic test leaves you fatigued for a day. A 1RM attempt carries real injury risk at the margin. A heart-rate-variability reading costs five minutes and a moment of attention every single morning, which, summed over a year, is not nothing. The question to ask before any of them is not "is this interesting?" but "what will I do differently depending on the result?" If the honest answer is "nothing," the test is entertainment, not training.

This filter rules out more than it seems to. Testing VO2max twice in eight weeks of base training will not change what you do in week nine — you were going to keep doing aerobic volume either way. Testing critical power before and after a six-week threshold block, by contrast, tells you whether the block worked and what number to build the next block from. The first is curiosity; the second is a decision input. Both chapters' worth of tests below are organised the same way this chapter is: decide why you are testing before you decide what to test.

Key idea

A test is only worth running if a different result would change what you do next. Testing for its own sake is not rigour — it is a session's worth of fatigue spent on information with no destination.

35.2Testing The Aerobic Engine

VO2max — the maximum rate at which the body can take up and use oxygen, introduced properly in chapter 9 — is the number people reach for first, and the hardest of all the tests in this chapter to get right. The gold-standard laboratory test runs a treadmill or bike protocol to volitional exhaustion while a mask samples every breath, and it is genuinely the most accurate measurement available — but it requires equipment almost nobody owns, and even in a good lab, protocol choices (ramp rate, starting intensity, treadmill versus bike) can shift the result by several percent. A faster ramp tends to under-read VO2max slightly because the legs or lungs hit their local limit before the cardiovascular system is fully taxed; a slower ramp risks the test over-running and ending on fatigue rather than a true maximum. Neither error is large, but it means two "VO2max" numbers from two different labs are not automatically comparable.

Lactate threshold testing — drawing a small blood sample from a fingertip or earlobe every few minutes during a graded test to find where blood lactate concentration starts rising sharply (introduced in chapter 7 and modelled in the widget below) — is, if anything, more protocol-sensitive than VO2max. The stage length, the starting intensity, whether lactate is sampled from a warmed earlobe or a cold fingertip, and which of several mathematical definitions of "threshold" is applied (a fixed 4 mmol/L, the point of steepest rise, or the point where lactate first rises 1 mmol/L above baseline) can each move the answer by a meaningful margin. This is not a flaw unique to lactate testing — it is the general rule for every test in this chapter: the absolute number depends on the protocol; the change over time, run on an identical protocol, does not.

For almost everyone outside a funded programme, field tests are the realistic option, and they are not a poor substitute so much as a different instrument. The Cooper test (distance covered in 12 minutes, run flat-out) and the 1.5-mile time trial both estimate VO2max from validated regression equations with a typical error of around 10–15% against a true laboratory value B — too wide to compare against someone else's lab figure, entirely adequate for tracking your own trend. A 5 km time trial, run honestly, gives less a VO2max estimate than a direct performance number, which for most purposes is more useful anyway: you rarely care what your VO2max is in the abstract, you care whether you are getting faster.

The ramp test — a continuous, steadily increasing load to exhaustion, typically 15–30 W per minute on a bike or an equivalent speed/gradient increase on a treadmill — sits between the lab and the field. It needs no blood sampling and no gas analysis, only a power meter or treadmill and a willingness to go to failure, and it produces a reasonably reliable peak power or peak speed that correlates well with VO2max without measuring it directly. It is the test behind most of the "FTP estimate" features built into cycling apps and the 20-minute test discussed below.

In plain terms

Think of these tests as rulers, not scales at the butcher's.

A butcher's scale has to be exactly right in absolute terms, because you are paying for grams. A ruler can be slightly the wrong length and still be extremely useful, because what you actually care about is whether this plank is longer than that one — and a ruler that is 2% short measures that difference just as well as a perfect one. Every aerobic field test in this chapter is a ruler. It does not need to match a laboratory's number. It needs to be the same ruler every time.

35.3Critical Power And FTP

Critical power (CP) is the power output you could, in theory, sustain indefinitely before fatigue becomes mathematically inevitable — the asymptote of the relationship between power and how long you can hold it. Above CP, a second, finite store of work capacity is being spent down; below it, that store is not being touched and fatigue develops far more slowly. That finite store is called W′ ("W-prime"), measured in kilojoules, and it behaves like a battery: spend it above CP, and it recharges only once you drop back below CP. In trained amateur cyclists and runners, W′ typically falls in the region of 15–25 kJ B, though the exact figure varies with the test protocol used to derive it, as the next paragraph explains.

The simplest way to get both numbers is the 3-minute all-out test: after a thorough warm-up, go maximally hard for exactly three minutes against a fixed resistance (so effort, not pacing, determines power). CP is approximated by the mean power of the final 30 seconds — by that point, W′ is almost fully depleted and power has settled onto the sustainable asymptote. W′ is approximated by the total work done above that end-power line for the full three minutes — literally the area between your power curve and the flat CP line, converted to kilojoules. An alternative, slightly more laborious method pairs a maximal 3-minute and a maximal 12-minute effort on separate days and solves the two data points for the power-duration relationship directly; it tends to agree closely with the single 3-minute test in recreational and club-level athletes, though research in elite cyclists has found the single all-out test can overestimate CP and correspondingly underestimate W′ compared with multi-trial methods B — a caution worth knowing if you are already riding at a very high level, and largely irrelevant if you are not.

FTP (functional threshold power), already introduced in chapter 44, is a simpler, cruder cousin: the highest power you could hold in a quasi-steady state for about an hour, commonly estimated as 95% of a 20-minute maximal effort. FTP is a single number with no accompanying measure of anaerobic capacity, it assumes a fixed and somewhat arbitrary relationship between 20-minute and 60-minute power that does not hold equally well for every athlete, and 95% was chosen for convenience rather than derived from first principles. CP is the more physiologically grounded of the two — it comes from an actual mathematical model of the power-duration relationship, fitted to real data, and it comes with W′ attached, which FTP simply does not provide. The practical case for FTP is adoption, not accuracy: it is baked into almost every cycling app's zone calculator, requires only one test rather than a protocol some software does not yet support, and for setting training zones the two numbers usually land within a few percent of each other anyway.

35.4Testing Strength And Power

The one-repetition maximum (1RM) — the most weight you can lift for a single good-form repetition — is the most direct strength test there is, and also the one with the highest cost: a true 1RM attempt is, by definition, a maximal, fatiguing, technically demanding single effort, carrying meaningfully more injury risk at the margin than a submaximal set. For most lifters, most of the time, an estimated 1RM from a set taken close to failure is the better trade. Equations such as Epley's or Brzycki's convert a known weight and a rep count into a projected maximum, and they are reasonably accurate in the 2–6 rep range — but accuracy degrades sharply above about 8–10 reps, where fatigue and technique drift start to swamp the simple linear relationship the equations assume, and typical error even in the sweet spot runs 5–10% B. An estimate from a 3-rep set taken to genuine failure is usable; an estimate from a 15-rep set is a guess with a number attached.

The isometric mid-thigh pull — pulling maximally against a fixed, immovable bar set at mid-thigh height, usually on a force plate — sidesteps both problems. There is no eccentric phase, no technical failure point, and no weight to miscalculate; the athlete simply pulls as hard as possible for a few seconds and the force plate records peak force and rate of force development directly. It is a specialist-equipment test, but where available it is one of the lowest-risk, most reliable ways to track maximal and explosive strength over a season without the fatigue cost of a 1RM attempt.

The countermovement jump (CMJ) — a vertical jump with a quick dip before take-off, performed on a force plate or even a simple jump mat — deserves special mention as the best low-cost fatigue monitor available. It takes under a minute, requires no warm-up beyond what any session already includes, and jump height, combined with more detailed force-plate metrics such as the rate of force development and the length of the eccentric braking phase, is sensitive to acute neuromuscular fatigue in a way that is both reliable (day-to-day variation under about 10% in trained athletes) and meaningful: a CMJ that is 5–10% below an athlete's rolling baseline is a genuinely useful signal that the nervous system has not recovered from recent load, independent of how the athlete says they feel. B

Velocity-based training (VBT) uses a linear position transducer or accelerometer to measure how fast the bar moves on every rep, and builds an individual's own load–velocity profile: because bar speed at any given percentage of 1RM is remarkably consistent for a given lift and a given lifter, measuring velocity at a few known loads lets you estimate 1RM without ever touching it, and — more usefully day to day — lets you autoregulate load session to session based on how fast today's warm-up sets are actually moving. Velocity-based 1RM predictions from a well-built profile correlate strongly with directly tested 1RM (typical error around 9–10% of 1RM across lifts), with accuracy generally better for upper-body, single-joint-dominant lifts such as the bench press than for complex, multi-joint lifts such as the squat, where technique variation adds noise to the velocity signal. B

Where this goes wrong

A 1RM attempt in an untrained lifter, under fatigue, or without a qualified spotter is a meaningfully higher-risk test than anything else in this chapter. If you are not confident in your technique at near-maximal loads, use an estimated 1RM from a submaximal set, or have a qualified coach supervise the attempt. This is not medical advice, but it is the one place in this chapter where the cost side of the test-cost ledger can genuinely include an injury, not just fatigue.

In plain terms

A countermovement jump is like checking a car's tyre pressure before a long drive.

It takes seconds, it needs no special occasion, and a reading that is noticeably lower than usual tells you something is off before you find out the hard way on the motorway. It cannot tell you why — a slow puncture, the cold weather, a knock from a kerb — only that today is not a day to assume everything is as it was yesterday.

35.5Readiness Monitoring — HRV, Resting Heart Rate, Sleep, And The Honest Verdict

Heart rate variability (HRV) — the beat-to-beat variation in the time between heartbeats, usually measured for a few minutes on waking — is marketed as a window into the autonomic nervous system's recovery state, and it is built into nearly every running watch and recovery app sold today. The honest finding, from a comprehensive 2016 systematic review and meta-analysis of the evidence, is more muted than the marketing: resting HRV was found to be largely unaffected by functional overreaching across the pooled studies, which is a genuinely different claim from "HRV detects overreaching." B A related heart-rate-based measure — how quickly heart rate falls after stopping a bout of exercise (heart-rate recovery) or how it responds during a standardised sub-maximal effort — showed somewhat more promise as a fatigue marker than resting HRV did, though the evidence base there is thinner still.

None of this means HRV is useless; it means it is specific and slow. A single morning's HRV reading, set against yesterday's, is mostly measuring noise — breathing pattern, sleep position, time since waking, even how recently you ate. Weekly or rolling-average trends are far more informative than any single daily number, because they average out the noise and leave the slower signal, if one exists, visible underneath it. Treat a single bad reading as exactly as meaningful as a single bad night's sleep — worth noting, not worth reacting to.

Resting heart rate, taken first thing on waking before any stimulant or movement, is the simpler, older cousin of HRV and suffers from a milder version of the same problem: day-to-day noise is real, but a sustained rise of 5–10 beats per minute above your personal baseline, held for several consecutive days, is a far more useful signal than any single morning's number. Sleep — duration, and increasingly, consumer-tracked sleep stages — is covered in full in chapter 36; for monitoring purposes, duration and perceived quality are the parts worth trusting, and stage-by-stage breakdowns from a wrist-worn device should be read with real scepticism, since their agreement with clinical polysomnography is modest at best.

The least technological tool on this list is, by a consistent margin in the literature, one of the most useful: a short subjective wellness questionnaire — rating sleep quality, muscle soreness, stress, mood and overall fatigue on a simple scale, taking under a minute each morning. Across multiple comparisons, simple subjective wellness measures have matched or outperformed HRV, resting heart rate and other objective markers at detecting meaningful changes in training status and flagging athletes heading toward non-functional overreaching. B This is not an argument against gadgets; it is a reminder that the body already has excellent sensors built in, and asking it a direct question is often cheaper and at least as accurate as buying a device to ask it indirectly.

Myth vs fact
“My HRV app told me I'm not recovered, so today has to be an easy day”

The number is a direct readout of how trained or fatigued I am.

One reading is mostly noise

A 2016 systematic review found resting HRV largely unchanged even during genuine functional overreaching, and single daily readings are heavily influenced by breathing, sleep timing and measurement conditions. A rolling weekly trend, combined with a simple wellness question or two, is a better-supported signal than any single morning's number from any device.

The paper
Bellenger CR, Fuller JT, Thomson RL, Davison K, Robertson EY, Buckley JD. Monitoring athletic training status through autonomic heart rate regulation: a systematic review and meta-analysis. Sports Medicine 2016;46(10):1461–86.
Who

A systematic review and meta-analysis pooling studies of athletes undergoing deliberate overload or overreaching protocols.

What

Compared resting heart rate, heart-rate variability and heart-rate recovery/response measures against training status (functional overreaching, non-functional overreaching, and normal training) across the pooled literature.

Found

Resting HRV was largely unaffected by functional overreaching across pooled studies — a null result for the single most heavily marketed readiness metric. Heart-rate-recovery and sub-maximal heart-rate-response measures showed more consistent, if still modest, sensitivity to overreaching.

Why it matters: it is the strongest single piece of evidence that daily resting HRV, as sold to consumers, does not reliably do the job it is marketed for. Limitation: pooled studies varied widely in protocol, overreaching definition and HRV measurement method, and the authors explicitly flag that methodological inconsistency across the field could be masking a real effect rather than disproving one — the honest reading is "not yet shown to work," not "proven not to work."

35.6What To Track, And What To Ignore

Put the previous two sections together and a short, defensible dashboard falls out almost on its own. It is short on purpose: every extra metric is another few minutes a day and another number competing for attention, and the evidence above does not support most of what gets tracked by default.

  • Sleep-stage breakdowns from a wrist device. Poor agreement with real polysomnography; duration and subjective quality are the trustworthy parts.

35.7Test Reliability — Smallest Worthwhile Change Versus Typical Error

Every test has noise, and the single most useful statistic nobody asks for is the typical error — roughly, how much a test's result bounces around on repeated measurement of someone whose true ability has not changed at all. A Cooper test with a typical error of 10% means that a 5% improvement on retest could be real fitness, or could simply be a better day, a cooler morning, or more sleep the night before. The useful comparison is against the smallest worthwhile change (SWC) — the smallest improvement that would actually matter for the athlete's goals, often estimated as a fraction of the normal between-athlete variation in the metric. If a test's typical error is larger than the smallest change you care about, that test cannot reliably tell you whether the change happened, no matter how precisely the number is reported to two decimal places.

This has a counter-intuitive practical consequence: repeating the same imperfect test beats switching to a theoretically better one. A field test run exactly the same way — same course, same warm-up, same time of day, same device — builds up a personal baseline and a personal typical error, against which a real change becomes detectable even if the test itself is crude in absolute terms. Swap to a "better" test halfway through a programme — a lab VO2max instead of last quarter's Cooper test, a different brand of force plate for the CMJ — and you lose that baseline entirely; the new number cannot be compared with the old one, and you are, in effect, starting the clock again. Precision is a property of the comparison, not of the instrument alone, which is the organising idea of this entire chapter.

Activate it

A minimal, repeatable testing calendar that actually earns its cost in decisions made, built around the dashboard above.

  • Do One aerobic test (field test, ramp test, or a 3-minute all-out for CP/W′) and, if resistance training, one strength proxy (estimated 1RM from a 3-rep set, or a CMJ baseline).
  • Dose Daily: a 60-second wellness check and resting heart rate. Every 6–8 weeks: the aerobic and strength tests, run identically each time.
  • When Schedule the big tests for the start or end of a training block, never mid-block when fatigue will contaminate the result.
  • On the bike A 3-minute all-out test on a calibrated indoor trainer is one of the most controllable versions of any test in this chapter — no wind, no traffic, no gradient, so a change in the number is overwhelmingly likely to be a change in you.
  • Progress Keep the exact same protocol every time; if you must change equipment, run the old and new protocol back-to-back once to establish the offset between them before relying on the new one alone.
  • Signal You're doing it right when a test result changes what you do in the next training block — not just what you post about it.

Five things to carry forward

  1. Only run a test if a different result would change a decision; testing for curiosity alone spends fatigue on information with no destination.
  2. Critical power and W′, from a 3-minute all-out test, give a more physiologically grounded picture than FTP alone — a sustainable boundary plus a measured reserve above it, typically 15–25 kJ in trained amateurs.
  3. The countermovement jump is the best low-cost acute fatigue monitor available; a 5–10% drop below personal baseline is a real, actionable signal.
  4. A 2016 systematic review found resting HRV largely unaffected by overreaching, and simple subjective wellness questionnaires have consistently matched or beaten gadget-based readiness scores — read HRV as a weekly trend, if at all, never a single morning's number.
  5. A test's absolute accuracy matters less than its reliability: repeating the same imperfect test over time beats switching to a theoretically superior one, because the comparison, not the instrument, is what carries the signal.
