Power, Speed & Plyometrics

The stretch-shortening cycle and the force-velocity profile

A sprinter's foot is on the ground for about a tenth of a second — roughly the time it takes to blink — and in that instant it must reverse a force several times the sprinter's own body weight. That is not mainly a strength problem. The sprinter could double their squat and still be slow, because squatting and sprinting sit at opposite ends of the same curve. Power — force multiplied by velocity — is the quantity that actually wins races and jumps, and it peaks not at maximum force, nor at maximum speed, but somewhere in the uncomfortable middle.

18.1Power Is Force Times Velocity

Power is simply how fast work gets done: force multiplied by velocity (P = F × v). A heavy bar moved very slowly can produce the same power as a light bar moved very fast — and neither resembles the lift that produces the most power of all.

Inside a single muscle fibre, force and velocity trade off in a way first described by A.V. Hill in the 1930s: the faster a fibre shortens, the less force it can produce, in a relationship close to a straight line. Multiply that falling force by the rising velocity and the product — power — traces a curve starting at zero (maximum force, zero velocity: nothing moves), rising to a peak, then falling back to zero (maximum velocity, zero force: a limb flailing in the air). A single fibre's peak sits almost exactly halfway along that curve. Whole, multi-joint human movements — a jump, a squat, a throw — are less tidy, because tendon elasticity and joint geometry distort the simple fibre-level picture, but peak power still generally falls between 30% and 60% of an athlete's maximum force for that movement: closer to 0–30% of 1RM for a vertical jump, 30–45% for a loaded jump squat, higher again for pulling-dominant Olympic-lift derivatives. In plain terms

A car's gearbox makes the point better than any muscle diagram. First gear gives you huge pulling force and almost no speed. Top gear gives you speed but hardly any pulling force at the wheels. The engine's maximum power — the number that actually gets the car down the road fastest — is delivered in neither gear. It happens in the middle ratios, where force and speed are both merely decent and their product is biggest.

A heavy squat is first gear: enormous force, almost no speed. An empty-bar speed squat is top gear: lots of speed, not much force. The jump squat with a moderate load in between is third or fourth gear — the one that actually produces the most power, even though neither end of the curve feels as impressive on its own.

18.2The Stretch-Shortening Cycle

Drop a tennis ball and it rebounds most of the way back up, because a stretched rubber-like structure releases the energy it stored. Drop a bag of wet sand and it doesn't rebound at all; the energy disappears as heat. Your tendon, during a jump, is imitating the tennis ball — and how good that imitation is happens to be one of the most trainable things about human power output.

The stretch-shortening cycle (SSC) is the pattern in which a muscle-tendon unit is rapidly stretched (the eccentric phase — landing, or the dip before a jump) immediately before it shortens to produce force (the concentric phase, the jump itself). Two things make that following contraction stronger than one starting from rest. First, the tendon, a stiff spring, stores elastic energy on the stretch and returns some of it on the way out, like a snapped-back elastic band. Second, the sudden stretch triggers a spinal stretch reflex, via sensors called muscle spindles, adding extra involuntary neural drive before the voluntary contraction would have arrived on its own. The combination, reflex potentiation, is why a countermovement jump (dip first) reliably jumps higher than a static jump from a dead stop, using identical muscles.

How much of that stored energy you keep depends almost entirely on amortisation time — the pause between the end of the stretch and the start of the shortening, at the bottom of the dip. A short amortisation keeps the tendon loaded and the reflex intact; a long one lets both leak away as heat, the mechanical equivalent of dropping the tennis ball onto wet sand instead of a hard floor. This is why coaches obsess over minimising time spent at the bottom of a jump, a bound or a sprint stride.

Fast and slow stretch-shortening cycles
TypeAmortisationExamplesWhat dominates
Fast SSC<~0.25 sSprinting stance, pogo hops, low-box depth jumps, rebound jumpsTendon elastic return and reflex potentiation; contractile force contributes comparatively little
Slow SSC>~0.25 sCountermovement jump with a deep dip, heavier depth jumps, a loaded jump squatContractile (muscular) force dominates; elastic contribution is smaller and reflex potentiation is weaker

The 0.25-second boundary is a widely used approximation, not a sharp biological line, and varies by joint and athlete. C The practical point survives the imprecision: fast-SSC work trains the tendon's spring and the reflex; slow-SSC work trains the muscle's ability to produce force after being stretched. Sprinting and most change-of-direction actions are fast-SSC tasks — one reason heavy, slow strength training alone doesn't automatically make someone faster. It builds the wrong half of the cycle.

18.3Force–Velocity Profiling

Two athletes can jump exactly the same height for opposite reasons: one is strong but slow to apply it, the other quick but with little force to apply. Training them identically wastes a training year. Force–velocity profiling, developed principally by Pierre Samozino and Jean-Benoît Morin, tells them apart: an athlete performs jumps or jump squats across a range of loads, from light to heavy, and the resulting force and velocity values are plotted to estimate two theoretical numbers — F0, force at zero velocity, and v0, velocity at zero force. The ratio between them is the athlete's force–velocity profile, comparable against the mathematically optimal profile for their jump height and power output.

The comparison yields one useful number: the F-V imbalance, classifying an athlete as force-deficient (skewed toward velocity; get stronger before more speed-work pays off) or velocity-deficient (skewed toward force; another heavy block won't help, but faster, lighter work will). The prescription is deliberately counter-intuitive: train the opposite of what the athlete is already good at. A player who squats 2× body weight but jumps modestly is not short of force; more heavy squats trains a quality that is no longer the bottleneck.

Worked example
Owen, 24, rugby forward, 90 kg, back squat 1RM 180 kg (2.0× body weight), countermovement jump (no arm swing) 30 cm. His coach wants to know where to spend the next training block.
  1. Estimate peak power from the jump using the Sayers field equation (bodyweight and jump height only, no force platform needed): PP(W) = 60.7 × jump height(cm) + 45.3 × mass(kg) − 2055.60.7×30 + 45.3×90 − 2055 = 1,821 + 4,077 − 2,055 = 3,843 W
  2. Express that relative to body mass, which is what allows comparison against position norms rather than absolute size.3,843 ÷ 90 ≈ 42.7 W/kg
  3. Compare against typical published ranges for professional rugby forwards, roughly 45–55 W/kg.42.7 W/kg sits below the position norm
  4. Compare his strength the same way: 2.0× body weight on the back squat sits comfortably above the general strength-trained benchmark of roughly 1.5–1.8× body weight.
  5. Read the two findings together: force capability is above average; power output, and by extension velocity capability, is below average for his role. The imbalance points one way.Strong relative to norms, modestly powerful relative to norms → velocity-deficient
  6. Prescribe the opposite of what he already has: reduce heavy squatting to one maintenance session a week and add two sessions built around unloaded jumps, bounds and light jump squats at 0–30% of his 1RM, trained for maximal intended speed.
So: Owen's 180 kg squat is not the problem; at an estimated 42.7 W/kg his jump is below the range expected for his position, and the fix is less heavy strength work and more unloaded, maximal-intent jumping — not another block of the thing he is already good at.

Two honest caveats: the Sayers equation is a published regression estimate, not a direct force-platform measurement, so treat it as a season-long trend rather than a precise one-off number B; and a full profile needs jumps or sprints across several loads, not one jump and one 1RM — Owen's example simplifies the logic, not the complete method. 18.4Plyometrics — A Progression, Not A Category

Plyometric exercises deliberately use the stretch-shortening cycle above, ranging from gentle to genuinely dangerous if the ladder is skipped. The mistake most programmes make is treating "plyometrics" as one difficulty level rather than a spectrum climbed in order, over months, the way load is climbed on a barbell.

  1. 1 · Pogo hops

    Small, stiff-ankled bounces in place, minimal knee bend, ground contact under 0.2 s. Almost pure tendon and reflex; the entry point for everyone, including a returning rehab patient.

  2. 2 · Ankle and line hops

    Pogo hops added to forward, lateral and single-leg variations. Still low force, now adding direction and asymmetry.

  3. 3 · Low hurdle hops and skips

    Short, rhythmic, double-leg; introduces a brief flight phase and a slightly longer amortisation without adding real height or landing force.

  4. 4 · Bounding

    Alternating-leg, exaggerated running strides covering distance. Higher single-leg landing force than hopping, and the first drill that closely resembles sprinting mechanics.

  5. 5 · Box jumps and tuck jumps

    Maximal vertical or broad jumps, usually onto a soft landing surface. Trains concentric power without the landing stress of coming back down from height.

  6. 6 · Depth jumps

    Step off a box, land, and rebound immediately into a jump. The highest-force, highest-skill entry on the ladder — box height starts low (20–30 cm) and rises only once landing mechanics and amortisation time are both solid.

C The direction matters more than the exact numbers: fewer, higher-quality contacts as intensity rises, with box height treated as the load variable — exactly as kilograms are the load variable on a bar.

The payoff isn't confined to jump height. A tendon trained to store and return energy also makes running cheaper: a stiffer, more elastic tendon returns more of the energy put into it each stride instead of losing it as heat in a slack structure — one of the few places a power-training method measurably improves an endurance outcome.

The paper
Ramirez-Campillo R, et al. Effects of plyometric training on lower body muscle architecture, tendon structure, stiffness and physical performance: a systematic review and meta-analysis. Sports Med Open 2022;8:40.
Who

Pooled data across dozens of plyometric training trials in healthy populations.

What

Meta-analysis of plyometric training's effect on muscle architecture, tendon cross-sectional area, tendon stiffness and physical performance outcomes.

Found

A moderate increase in tendon stiffness (SMD ≈0.55), plus increases in muscle thickness and fascicle length. Linked studies report a roughly 16% increase in tendon stiffness tracking with about a 4% drop in the oxygen cost of running at a given pace.

Why it matters: direct evidence that plyometric training changes the mechanical property — stiffness — running economy depends on, not just the muscle around it. Limitation: the running-economy figure comes from a smaller linked subset, not the full pooled sample, and the effect builds up over many weeks, not days. B

18.5Sprint Mechanics — Two Different Jobs

Acceleration and maximal-velocity sprinting look similar on video but are mechanically almost opposite tasks. In acceleration, the body is pitched forward, and the faster a sprinter can direct ground force backward rather than straight down, the faster they accelerate — confirmed repeatedly by Jean-Benoît Morin's force-plate work. Two athletes producing identical total force can accelerate at very different rates simply because one directs more of it horizontally.

Once near maximal velocity the job inverts. Ground contact time has shrunk to roughly 0.08–0.10 seconds in elite performers, and the problem is no longer propulsion but resisting collapse — producing enough vertical force, fast enough, to support body weight and get back into the air before the leg buckles. The ceiling on top speed is set mainly by vertical force in that tiny window, a different physical problem from driving hard out of a crouched start.

So "sprint training" is not one thing. Resisted sled pushes and hill sprints train horizontal force production and sit at the force end of the curve; fly-in sprints at or near top speed train the short-contact, high-rate problem of maximal velocity and sit closer to the velocity end. Programming both, in the proportion a sport needs, matters more than mastering either alone. Chapter 22 covers the muscles doing this work.

18.6Training Power — Intent Over Load

A bar moved at maximal intended speed and the same bar ground up slowly to the same final position are different exercises, because the nervous system recruits differently for each. Ballistic movements — jump squats, throws, Olympic-lift derivatives — accelerate throughout and are released or left to decelerate in the air; grinding movements — a heavy back squat, a slow bench press — decelerate mechanically near lockout, because the lifter must control the bar rather than let it fly off the hands. That deceleration actively cuts force output in the last portion of a grinding lift — exactly the part a ballistic lift never has.

Olympic lifts (the clean and jerk, the snatch) are the textbook ballistic exercise, but they are not the only route to this adaptation, and for most athletes not the most efficient one: a competition-standard snatch takes months to groove technically, and poorly executed risks the shoulders and wrists for a return achievable more cheaply elsewhere. Olympic-lift derivatives — hang pulls, high pulls, cleans from the hang without the catch — keep most of the triple-extension stimulus with a fraction of the technical demand, and jumps and med-ball throws deliver the same ballistic, uninterrupted acceleration without a barbell at all. Most athletes get the great majority of the transferable benefit from jumps, throws and pulling derivatives, and never need to learn a competition lift.

Myth vs fact
“You have to learn the Olympic lifts to train power properly”

Otherwise you are missing the gold-standard power exercise.

The lifts are one route among several, not a prerequisite

What matters is a ballistic movement performed with maximal intent at an appropriate load, somewhere across the force–velocity curve. Jump squats, broad jumps, bounds and med-ball throws deliver that stimulus with far less technical coaching and lower injury risk while the skill is learned. Competitive weightlifters need the actual lifts because they are the sport; almost nobody else does.

Contrast training (or complex training) pairs a heavy strength exercise with a biomechanically similar explosive one in the same session — heavy back squats, then jump squats a few minutes later. The proposed mechanism, post-activation potentiation (PAP), is that a near-maximal contraction temporarily boosts motor-unit recruitment and cross-bridge cycling speed, so the explosive set that follows has more drive available than it would have had cold. The effect is real but modest and individual, and needs a rest window of roughly 4–12 minutes between the two; too short a gap just adds fatigue on top of the heavy set instead of potentiation. C

18.7Programming Power — Quality, Not Quantity

Power training inverts almost every volume instinct built up from hypertrophy and endurance work. The stimulus that matters is bar or body speed under intent, and speed is the first thing to degrade with fatigue, well before the lifter feels tired in any conventional sense. A set slower than the session's best set has quietly become a strength-endurance set wearing a power exercise's clothes.

The rule that follows is simple to state and easy to violate: low volume, long rests, and the session ends — for that exercise, that day — once output drops by around 5% from the best set, measured as bar velocity, jump height, or the coach's eye. Three to six reps, three to six sets, two to five minutes of rest between them, are typical; the rest exists to let full recovery happen before the next rep, because a power set performed partially-recovered trains fatigue-resistance, not power.

Activate it

A general power session structure built from the variables in this chapter.

  • Do One ballistic lower-body movement (jump squat or trap-bar jump), one horizontal plyometric (bound or broad jump), and either a sprint-mechanics drill or a throw.
  • Load Jump squats at roughly 0–30% of 1RM; bounds and sprints are bodyweight; adjust toward the force or velocity end per an athlete's profile from earlier in this chapter.
  • Dose 3–6 sets of 3–6 reps per exercise, full recovery between sets (2–5 min).
  • When 2–3 sessions a week, ideally when the nervous system is freshest — early in a session, early in the week, not after a heavy lower-body day.
  • Progress Track best-set velocity or jump height session to session; progress load only once speed at the current load stops improving.
  • On the bike Standing, maximal-cadence sprints — 6–8 × 6 s all-out from a rolling start, 2–3 min easy spin between — train rate of force development and peak power with no landing force at all, useful when joints need a rest from jumping.
  • Signal You're doing it right when the last working set looks and feels almost as fast as the first; stop the exercise the moment it doesn't.
C The 5% drop-off threshold is a widely used velocity-based-training heuristic rather than a single validated number; treat it as a sensible stopping rule, not a law.

Five things to carry forward

  1. Power is force multiplied by velocity, and it peaks at a moderate load — roughly 30–60% of maximal force depending on the task — not at either extreme of the curve.
  2. The stretch-shortening cycle adds stored tendon energy and a reflex boost to a contraction, but only if amortisation time — the pause between lowering and lifting — stays short; a long pause lets both leak away.
  3. Force–velocity profiling identifies whether an athlete is force-deficient or velocity-deficient, and the correct prescription is the opposite of whichever quality they already have plenty of.
  4. Plyometrics are a progression from pogo hops to bounding to depth jumps, not one difficulty level, and the payoff includes a stiffer, more elastic tendon that measurably cuts the oxygen cost of running.
  5. Power sessions want low volume, long rests between sets, and a hard stop once output — bar speed or jump height — drops by around 5% from the best set of the day.
