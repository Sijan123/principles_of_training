The Heart & Circulation

Cardiac output, stroke volume, and the Fick equation

An untrained heart and an elite rower's heart can beat at exactly the same maximum rate and still move more than twice as much blood per beat. That single fact — not a faster pulse, not a bigger lung — is the single largest reason one of them can sustain an effort the other cannot. The heart is not primarily a metronome that speeds up; it is a pump whose chamber size and filling behaviour change dramatically with training, and almost everything in this chapter is really an explanation of why that chamber, not the rate at which it beats, is the trainable variable that matters most.

9.1The Cardiac Cycle And What Determines Stroke Volume

Each heartbeat is a two-phase cycle: diastole, when the heart's chambers relax and fill with blood, and systole, when they contract and eject it. The amount of blood ejected by the left ventricle in one beat is stroke volume (SV), and three things determine it.

  1. Preload

    How much blood is in the ventricle just before it contracts — set mainly by how much blood returns to the heart through the veins. Think of filling a balloon before you squeeze it: more filling, more to push out, up to a point.

  2. Contractility

    How forcefully the heart muscle itself squeezes, independent of how full it is — a property of the muscle fibres, boosted acutely by adrenaline.

  3. Afterload

    The resistance the ventricle must push against to eject blood — mainly set by the pressure in the arteries. A higher afterload (e.g. high blood pressure) makes each beat work harder for the same output.

The relationship between preload and stroke volume is called the Frank–Starling mechanism: within limits, a fuller ventricle contracts more forcefully and ejects more blood, the way a stretched elastic band snaps back harder. This is not a trained adaptation in itself — it operates on every heartbeat, trained or not — but it is the mechanism that lets a larger, more compliant trained ventricle translate "fills with more blood" directly into "ejects more blood per beat."

In plain terms

Picture two identical water pumps, but one has a bigger intake chamber that also opens more easily.

Both pumps can cycle at the same maximum speed. But the pump with the bigger, easier-opening chamber draws in more water on every single stroke, so at the same cycling speed it moves far more water per minute. That is the entire difference between an untrained and a highly trained heart at matched heart rate — not a faster pump, a bigger and more compliant intake.

9.2The Fick Equation: Delivery Meets Extraction

The amount of oxygen the body can use per minute — VO2 — can be split into exactly two components, a relationship known as the Fick equation:

VO2 = Q × a-vO2 diffthe whole equation
Oxygen uptake equals cardiac output multiplied by the arterial-venous oxygen difference

Cardiac output (Q) is how much blood the heart pumps per minute — heart rate multiplied by stroke volume. This is the delivery side: how much oxygen-carrying blood arrives at the muscle. The arterial-venous oxygen difference (a-vO2 diff) is how much oxygen the tissue actually strips out of that blood as it passes through — the difference between the oxygen content of blood going in (arterial) and coming out (venous). This is the extraction side: how good the muscle itself is at grabbing the oxygen it's handed.

Both sides matter, but they are not equally trainable. Delivery, driven mainly by stroke volume, can roughly double or more with years of endurance training. Extraction improves too — mitochondrial and capillary adaptations from chapter 8 raise it — but far less dramatically: from around 14–15 mL of oxygen per 100 mL of blood in untrained muscle to roughly 16–17 mL/100 mL at the limit in highly trained muscle B. 9.3Why Stroke Volume Is The Trainable Variable

Untrained maximal stroke volume is typically around 70–80 mL per beat. Elite endurance athletes — particularly in sports with very high training volumes, such as cross-country skiing and rowing — have measured maximal stroke volumes of 160–210 mL, roughly double to nearly triple the untrained figure B. Maximal heart rate, by contrast, does not rise with training and often falls slightly; it is set largely by age and genetics (discussed further below). The entire story of a trained endurance heart's superior cardiac output is therefore a story about the ventricle's size and behaviour, not its speed.

Two structural changes drive the increase. First, the left ventricle's internal chamber enlarges — a true increase in end-diastolic volume, the amount of blood the chamber holds when full. Second, and less widely appreciated, the ventricle wall becomes more compliant: easier to stretch and quicker to recoil. Part of this recoil is active rather than purely elastic, a phenomenon sometimes called diastolic suction — the relaxing ventricle actively helps draw blood in rather than simply waiting passively to be filled, rather like the chamber briefly creating its own negative pressure to pull blood toward it. Research led by Benjamin Levine's group has shown that lifelong endurance athletes retain a ventricular compliance indistinguishable from healthy young adults decades later, while sedentary ageing stiffens the chamber substantially B — a finding explored further below.

Worked example
Comparing the arithmetic of improving delivery versus improving extraction, holding heart rate fixed at a maximal 190 bpm.
  1. Start with an untrained profile: stroke volume 75 mL, a-vO2 diff 15 mL/100 mL.Q = 190 × 0.075 L = 14.25 L/min. VO2max = 14.25 × 0.15 = 2.14 L/min
  2. Now double stroke volume alone — a realistic outcome of years of endurance training — and hold extraction unchanged.Q = 190 × 0.150 L = 28.5 L/min. VO2max = 28.5 × 0.15 = 4.28 L/min
  3. Instead, hold stroke volume at the untrained 75 mL and improve extraction by a generous 15% (from 15 to 17.25 mL/100 mL) — near the upper end of what extraction alone typically achieves.Q = 14.25 L/min (unchanged). VO2max = 14.25 × 0.1725 = 2.46 L/min
  4. Compare the two improvements against the same untrained baseline of 2.14 L/min.Doubling SV: +2.14 L/min (+100%). Improving extraction 15%: +0.32 L/min (+15%)
So: a doubling of stroke volume — well within the documented range for trained endurance athletes — moves VO2max roughly six to seven times further than a generous 15% improvement in extraction, for the simple reason that stroke volume has much further realistic room to grow. This is the arithmetic case for why endurance programming (chapter 14) spends so much of its effort on the cardiovascular side rather than purely on the muscle.
Activate it

The target here is the single most trainable term in the Fick equation: maximal stroke volume — a larger, more compliant left ventricle that fills better and ejects more blood per beat.

  • Do Long intervals at an intensity that holds you near your oxygen-uptake ceiling: 4 × 4 min at 90–95% of maximum heart rate, with 3 min of easy active recovery. Use a mode with large muscle groups — running, cycling, rowing or uphill walking.
  • Why that dose Stroke volume is stressed only when the ventricle is filling and ejecting maximally, and oxygen uptake needs 60–120 s to reach its ceiling. Intervals shorter than about two minutes barely visit it.
  • Dose 1–2 sessions a week for 3–4 weeks, then reduce. This is a block stimulus, not a year-round setting.
  • Build underneath it 3–5 easy aerobic sessions a week. Plasma volume expands within days of starting consistent training and is itself a direct contributor to stroke volume through preload — see chapter 11.
  • On the bike 4 × 8 min at roughly 106% of FTP with 3 min easy, or 5 × 4 min at roughly 113% of FTP with 4 min easy. The trainer is the easiest place to hold these targets precisely — with a fan running, since an uncooled room inflates heart rate and corrupts the intensity. See chapter 44.
  • Signal You're doing it right when your heart rate at a fixed submaximal workload falls by 5–10 beats over six to eight weeks. That drop is the stroke-volume adaptation: the same cardiac output delivered by fewer, bigger beats.
A Stroke volume's dominance in VO2max trainability is established across bed-rest, training and cross-sectional athlete studies; the specific interval prescription comes from work-matched trials covered in chapter 15.

9.4Blood Pressure And Flow Redistribution During Exercise

At rest, skeletal muscle receives a modest share of cardiac output. During maximal exercise, blood flow is aggressively redistributed: vessels supplying working muscle dilate dramatically while vessels supplying the gut and kidneys constrict, so that muscle can receive on the order of 80% or more of a now much larger total cardiac output. Systolic blood pressure rises substantially during dynamic exercise (reflecting the rising cardiac output), while diastolic pressure typically stays roughly flat or falls slightly, because the dilated muscle vasculature lowers overall resistance even as flow surges.

Returning that blood to the heart against gravity, especially from the legs, is helped considerably by the muscle pump: each contraction squeezes the deep veins running through the muscle, pushing blood toward the heart past one-way valves that stop it falling back. This is a genuinely mechanical effect, not a cardiovascular reflex, and it is a meaningful part of why standing still after a hard effort (rather than continuing to move) can leave people feeling light-headed — venous return briefly loses its mechanical assistance at the same time demand is still elevated.

9.5The Athlete's Heart — And Telling It From Disease

Sustained training enlarges the heart, but not in one uniform way. Endurance training — large volumes of sustained aerobic work — produces predominantly eccentric remodelling: the ventricular chamber enlarges in volume, with wall thickness increasing only modestly, matched to the increase in chamber size. Strength and power athletes more often show a relatively greater degree of concentric remodelling: wall thickness increasing somewhat more relative to chamber size, consistent with training against high pressure loads rather than high volume loads. Many athletes, particularly in mixed sports, show a blend of both C — the clean eccentric/concentric split is a useful teaching model more than a strict rule for any individual.

This "athlete's heart" is a normal, benign adaptation, but it can superficially resemble pathological hypertrophy on a screening test, which matters for anyone undergoing cardiac screening as part of sports participation. Distinguishing the two is a clinical judgement, not something to self-diagnose, but the broad clues doctors use include: athlete's heart enlargement is usually proportionate to body size and training volume, preserves or improves diastolic function (filling), regresses somewhat with detraining, and occurs alongside normal or supranormal exercise capacity. Pathological hypertrophy more often shows disproportionate wall thickening, impaired filling, and is not explained by training load. Anyone with a family history of sudden cardiac death, unexplained fainting during exercise, or chest pain on exertion should be assessed properly rather than reassured by this paragraph.

The paper
Bhella PS, Hastings JL, Fujimoto N, et al. (incl. Levine BD). Impact of lifelong exercise "dose" on left ventricular compliance and distensibility. J Am Coll Cardiol 2014;64(12):1257–66.
Who

Seniors grouped by lifelong exercise history: sedentary, casual exercisers, and competitive Masters athletes, compared against healthy young adults.

What

Measured left-ventricular compliance and distensibility (how easily the chamber stretches to fill) using invasive pressure-volume assessment and cardiac MRI.

Found

Sedentary seniors had markedly stiffer, less compliant ventricles than young controls. Lifelong competitive Masters athletes had compliance statistically indistinguishable from the young healthy group; casual exercisers fell in between.

Why it matters: it shows that the age-related stiffening of the heart, long assumed to be an inevitable feature of ageing, is substantially a feature of inactivity, and that a high lifelong training dose can prevent most of it. Limitation: observational comparison across groups who differ in many ways besides training history, not a randomised trial; it cannot fully separate the effect of decades of training from other lifestyle and genetic differences between people who remain competitive athletes into their sixties and those who do not.

9.6Heart Rate: What It Tells You And What It Doesn't

Heart rate is the most measured number in training and one of the most widely misread. A few corrections are worth making explicitly.

  • HRmax is not a fitness measure. It is set largely by age and genetics and does not rise with training; comparing your maximum heart rate to someone else's tells you almost nothing about relative fitness.
  • Age-predicted formulas are rough. "220 minus age" carries error of roughly ±10–12 beats per minute for any individual — useful as a population average, unreliable for prescribing an individual's training zones without a measured value.
  • Heart rate lags the actual demand. It takes tens of seconds to catch up to a sudden change in effort, which is exactly why heart rate is a poor guide for short intervals (see below) and a reasonable guide for sustained steady efforts.
  • Cardiovascular drift is real and not a sign of fading fitness. During prolonged exercise, especially in heat, heart rate creeps upward at a constant workload as stroke volume falls slightly (from fluid shifts and rising core temperature) and the heart compensates by beating faster to hold cardiac output steady — covered fully in chapter 11.
Where This Goes Wrong

Using heart rate to regulate short intervals — anything under roughly 2–3 minutes — is close to useless, because the lag between a change in effort and the corresponding change in heart rate means the number you are watching reflects what you were doing 30–60 seconds ago, not what you are doing now. By the time heart rate has caught up to a 60-second effort, the effort is nearly over. Pace, power output, or perceived effort are far more honest guides for anything short; heart rate becomes genuinely informative again for efforts of several minutes or more, where the lag is a small fraction of the total duration. See chapter 35 for the full case on monitoring tools and their limits.

Five things to carry forward

  1. VO2 is the product of cardiac output (heart rate × stroke volume) and the arterial-venous oxygen difference — delivery multiplied by extraction — and the two sides respond very differently to training.
  2. Stroke volume is the dominant trainable variable: untrained maximum is roughly 70–80 mL, elite endurance athletes reach 160–210 mL, while oxygen extraction improves only modestly, from about 14–15 to 16–17 mL per 100 mL of blood.
  3. The enlargement behind that extra stroke volume is mostly a bigger, more compliant left ventricle, including an active relaxation effect called diastolic suction, not simply a thicker heart wall.
  4. Endurance and strength training produce different patterns of cardiac remodelling — broadly eccentric versus concentric — both of which are normal adaptations that can superficially resemble, but are distinguishable from, pathological heart disease.
  5. Maximal heart rate does not improve with training and should never be used as a fitness marker, and heart rate's natural lag makes it unreliable for regulating short intervals even though it remains useful for sustained efforts.
