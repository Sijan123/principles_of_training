Blood, Fluid & Heat

Plasma volume, haemoglobin mass, and the thermal cost of work

Within five days of training in the heat, before a single red blood cell has changed, an athlete's blood plasma expands by close to a tenth of its starting volume — and heart rate at the same easy pace can drop by ten beats or more. That is faster than almost any other adaptation in this book. This chapter is about blood as a tissue you can expand and enrich on two very different clocks, and about heat as the specific stress that, surprisingly, speeds up both of them at once.

11.1Blood Is A Tissue

Blood counts as connective tissue under the four-fabric scheme from chapter 1: cells scattered in a matrix, except here the matrix is liquid rather than fibrous. That matrix is plasma — mostly water, carrying proteins, salts, glucose and clotting factors — and it makes up roughly 55% of total blood volume. The remaining 45% is almost entirely red blood cells (erythrocytes), the biconcave discs packed with haemoglobin that carry oxygen, alongside a small fraction of white blood cells and platelets. The proportion of blood that is red cells is called haematocrit, typically 40–45% in men and 36–42% in women.

Total blood volume scales with body size at roughly 70 mL per kilogram of body mass B, so an 80 kg adult carries around 5.6 litres. That volume is not fixed; both of its two components — plasma and red cell mass — respond to training, but on wildly different schedules, which is the organising idea of this chapter.

11.2Plasma Volume Expansion — The Fastest Endurance Adaptation There Is

Within the first few days of a new training stress — particularly heat exposure or hard aerobic work — the body increases plasma volume by roughly 8–15% B. The trigger is partly an acute shift of fluid and protein from surrounding tissue into the bloodstream during exercise itself, reinforced over repeated sessions by hormonal changes (increased plasma proteins and a shift in fluid-regulating hormones) that hold the extra volume in place rather than letting the kidneys excrete it back out.

Why does more plasma matter so much? Recall the Fick equation from chapter 9: VO2 = cardiac output × the arterial-venous oxygen difference, and cardiac output itself is heart rate × stroke volume. Extra plasma means more blood returning to the heart on every beat — a bigger preload under the Frank–Starling mechanism — so the ventricle fills fuller and ejects more blood per beat without the heart muscle itself having changed at all. This is why cardiac output and stroke volume are often the very first measurable improvements in a new training programme, weeks before any muscular or mitochondrial change could plausibly explain them. It is also, not coincidentally, a large part of the "fast bar" effect on stroke volume shown in chapter 1's adaptation-timescale chart.

In plain terms

Imagine topping up a garden hose with more water before you turn the tap on.

A fuller hose delivers more water per squeeze of the nozzle, even though the hose itself and the tap are unchanged. Plasma expansion is exactly this: the heart (the nozzle) hasn't got stronger, but it now has more fluid arriving to push out on every single beat. That alone raises how much oxygen-carrying blood reaches the muscles per minute — cheaply, and within days.

There is a cost to this speed, though: plasma expansion dilutes haemoglobin concentration per litre of blood, even though total haemoglobin mass is unchanged. This is why a haemoglobin blood test taken a week into a hot training camp can look slightly lower than before training started — sometimes mislabelled "sports anaemia" — when in fact nothing pathological has happened; the athlete simply has more plasma carrying the same amount of haemoglobin. It is a dilution, not a deficiency, and it resolves as the second, slower adaptation below catches up.

11.3Haemoglobin Mass — The Slow, Powerful Adaptation

Where plasma volume is fast and temporary-feeling, total haemoglobin mass — the actual quantity of oxygen-carrying protein in circulation, as distinct from its concentration per litre — is slow and durable. Meaningful increases take weeks rather than days, typically in the range of 5–12% over several weeks to months of appropriate stimulus B, and once established it persists far longer than plasma expansion does after the stimulus stops.

Two distinct stimuli drive it. The classical route is altitude: reduced oxygen availability at height triggers the kidney to sense lower oxygen delivery and release more erythropoietin (EPO), the hormone that signals bone marrow to produce more red blood cells. The second, less obvious route is heat, covered in detail below, which appears to produce a meaningful fraction of the same haemoglobin-mass benefit without the logistical cost, altitude illness risk, or access problems of an altitude camp.

Either route runs into the same hard constraint: building new red blood cells requires iron, which sits at the centre of every haemoglobin molecule. An athlete who is iron-deficient, which is common in endurance athletes and particularly in menstruating women, simply cannot build new haemoglobin at the normal rate no matter how strong the EPO or heat signal is — iron status gates the whole process. This is one reason endurance programmes that include altitude or heat blocks routinely screen ferritin (the storage form of iron) beforehand; a training camp built around a stimulus the body cannot act on is wasted expense. Iron and other micronutrient considerations are covered fully in chapter 31.

Worked example
Priya, 29, 58 kg triathlete, blood volume at 70 mL/kg, undertaking a 3-week heat-training block.
  1. Estimate total blood volume before the block.58 × 70 = 4,060 mL
  2. Plasma is roughly 55% of that.4,060 × 0.55 ≈ 2,233 mL plasma
  3. A 10% plasma expansion in the first week of heat exposure.2,233 × 0.10 ≈ +223 mL plasma, reached within days
  4. A separate, slower 8% rise in haemoglobin mass over the full 3-week block, starting from an estimated baseline of roughly 580 g.580 × 0.08 ≈ +46 g haemoglobin, accruing over weeks, not days
  5. Net effect: total blood volume rises by more than the plasma figure alone suggests, because red cell mass is climbing too, just on a slower curve layered underneath the fast one.
So: in week one Priya's improved stroke volume is almost entirely the +223 mL of new plasma; by week three a slower, smaller, but much more durable gain in actual oxygen-carrying capacity has started to show through underneath it.

11.4Thermoregulation — Four Routes To Losing Heat

Exercise generates heat as an unavoidable by-product of muscle contraction — muscles are only around 20–25% efficient at converting chemical energy into movement, and the rest becomes heat (the remaining detail is covered in chapter 7). The body sheds that heat through four physical routes, and which one dominates changes everything about how heat and humidity each affect performance differently.

  • Radiation — heat leaving the body as infrared energy, the same way a radiator warms a room without touching it. Dominant at rest in a cool, still environment.
  • Conduction — direct heat transfer to something you are touching, such as cool water or ice. Usually a minor route during land-based exercise.
  • Convection — heat carried away by moving air or water passing over the skin, which is why a fan or a headwind cools you even at the same air temperature.
  • Evaporation — heat lost as sweat converts from liquid to vapour on the skin, consuming a large amount of energy to do so. This is the dominant route once exercise intensity rises, often accounting for 80% or more of total heat loss during hard effort in warm conditions B.

Because evaporation depends on sweat actually turning to vapour, not merely being produced, humidity matters more than temperature for exercise heat stress once you are already sweating heavily. In dry heat, sweat evaporates efficiently and does most of its cooling job; in humid heat, the air is already close to saturated with water vapour, so sweat drips off the skin without evaporating, providing little or no cooling despite soaking the kit just as thoroughly. This is why humid-but-moderate conditions can feel, and physiologically are, more dangerous than hot-but-dry ones at the same air temperature.

Where This Goes Wrong

Judging heat risk by temperature alone misses the variable that actually predicts heat illness. A wet-bulb or wet-bulb-globe-temperature reading, which factors in humidity, radiant heat and wind alongside air temperature, is a far better guide to real exercise heat stress than a thermometer reading by itself — two days at the identical air temperature can carry very different real risk depending on humidity.

11.5Cardiovascular Drift — The Real Cost Of A Rising Core Temperature

During prolonged exercise, especially in heat, heart rate creeps steadily upward even though pace or power output is held perfectly constant. This is cardiovascular drift, and it is not fading fitness or motivation — it is a direct, mechanical consequence of two things happening together. First, a share of blood volume is diverted to the skin to support sweating and radiant heat loss, and plasma is also being lost as sweat, so less blood returns to fill the heart — preload falls, so stroke volume falls slightly. Second, core temperature itself is climbing, and a hotter heart muscle beats faster partly as a direct thermal effect. The body's solution to a falling stroke volume, if it wants to protect cardiac output and therefore oxygen delivery, is to raise heart rate to compensate.

The practical consequence is one every endurance athlete has felt without naming it: the same pace that costs 140 beats per minute on a cool day can cost 155–160 on a hot one, for identical external work. Heart-rate-based training zones, discussed in chapter 35, need to be read with this in mind on hot days — the number is responding faithfully to a real physiological cost, not malfunctioning.

11.6Heat Acclimation — And Its Unexpected Haematological Bonus

Heat acclimation is the set of adaptations that occurs after repeated heat exposure during exercise, and it is one of the fastest, most reliable adaptations in exercise physiology. Within roughly five days of daily heat exposure — training in a hot environment for 60–90 minutes, or using passive methods such as a hot bath afterward — the plasma volume expansion described earlier occurs specifically as a heat-driven response, alongside two further changes: sweating begins earlier, at a lower core temperature, and produces more dilute sweat, conserving sodium that would otherwise be lost (see chapter 32). The combined effect is a lower heart rate and lower core temperature at the exact same external workload, usually measurable within one to two weeks A.

The genuinely striking finding, and the reason this chapter pairs blood and heat together rather than treating them separately, concerns that last row. Recent work has shown that the plasma volume expansion driven by heat exposure activates the kidney's oxygen-sensing machinery — the same renal sensing pathway that detects reduced oxygen delivery at altitude and releases erythropoietin — because a diluted, expanded plasma volume effectively looks to the kidney like reduced oxygen-carrying capacity, prompting a compensatory erythropoietic signal C. In one notable finding, athletes who combined heat exposure with a period at altitude retained significantly more of their altitude-induced haemoglobin mass gains after returning to sea level than a control group training at altitude without the added heat stimulus, which largely lost those gains over the following days C. The mechanism is still being worked out — it does not appear to depend on sustained elevation of circulating EPO itself — but the practical implication is notable: heat may not just be a separate adaptation alongside altitude, it may help preserve what altitude built.

The paper
Jenkins EJ, Koep JL, Douglas AJM, et al. (incl. Stembridge M). Daily hot-water immersion preserves altitude-induced haemoglobin mass expansion following descent independent of erythropoietin. Exp Physiol 2026.
Who

Endurance-trained participants undergoing an altitude exposure, then returning to sea level with or without daily hot-water immersion.

What

Measured haemoglobin mass and circulating erythropoietin across the post-altitude descent period, comparing a daily hot-water-immersion group against controls.

Found

The hot-water-immersion group retained their altitude-induced haemoglobin mass gains across the days after descent, while the control group's gains substantially declined over the same period — without a corresponding sustained difference in circulating erythropoietin between groups.

Why it matters: it suggests heat exposure can help lock in haematological gains from altitude that would otherwise fade quickly on return to sea level, through a mechanism distinct from ongoing EPO elevation. Limitation: a small, recent study; the precise mechanism of haemoglobin-mass preservation independent of EPO is not yet established, and it should be read as a promising early finding rather than settled practice.

Worked example
Tomas, 74 kg runner, same 60-minute tempo run on two days: a cool 12 °C morning and a humid 28 °C afternoon.
  1. Cool run: 74.6 kg before, 73.9 kg after, drank 300 mL during the run.Mass lost = 0.7 kg; total sweat loss = 0.7 kg + 0.3 L ≈ 1.0 L over 60 min = 1.0 L/h
  2. Percentage body-mass loss on the cool day.0.7 ÷ 74.6 ≈ 0.9%
  3. Hot/humid run: 74.6 kg before, 73.0 kg after, drank 500 mL during the run.Mass lost = 1.6 kg; total sweat loss = 1.6 + 0.5 = 2.1 L over 60 min = 2.1 L/h
  4. Percentage body-mass loss on the hot day.1.6 ÷ 74.6 ≈ 2.1%
  5. Average heart rate for the identical pace: 142 bpm cool, 156 bpm hot — a 14 bpm cardiovascular drift penalty for the same external work.
So: Tomas's sweat rate more than doubled, from 1.0 to 2.1 L/h, and his body-mass loss crossed the ~2% threshold at which performance and thermoregulatory strain both start climbing meaningfully — all for the same session, at the same pace, on the same legs.
Activate it

A short heat acclimation block to drive plasma volume expansion, earlier and more dilute sweating, and lower heart rate at a given pace before a hot event.

  • Do Steady aerobic exercise in heat, or passive heat exposure (hot bath/sauna) immediately after a cooler training session.
  • Load Core temperature elevated and sustained, not merely "feeling hot" — roughly 38.5–39 °C for 20–30 min within the session.
  • Dose 60–90 minutes of heat exposure daily.
  • When 7–14 consecutive days, finishing within 1–2 weeks of the target hot event; benefits decay within 2–3 weeks without further exposure.
  • On the bike An indoor trainer in a warm room with no fan is one of the most practical and controllable heat-acclimation sessions available — steady 60–75% FTP for 60–75 min, heated room, minimal airflow, replicating the core-temperature stimulus without any outdoor heat risk or route logistics.
  • Signal You're doing it right when heart rate at a fixed easy pace falls session over session and sweat starts noticeably earlier.
A Plasma volume, sweat-onset and heart-rate effects are well replicated; the haemoglobin-retention effect above is newer and graded C.

Five things to carry forward

  1. Blood volume adapts on two different clocks: plasma volume expands 8–15% within days, while haemoglobin mass rises 5–12% only over weeks to months.
  2. Extra plasma raises stroke volume through the Frank–Starling mechanism — more fluid returning to the heart means more ejected per beat, without the heart muscle itself changing — which is why early-programme fitness gains arrive so fast.
  3. Evaporation dominates heat loss during exercise, which is why humidity, not temperature alone, is the better predictor of real heat strain.
  4. Cardiovascular drift — rising heart rate at a constant workload in heat — is a real, mechanical consequence of falling plasma volume and rising core temperature, not a loss of fitness.
  5. Heat acclimation delivers fast, reliable adaptations within about a week, and emerging evidence suggests it can also help preserve altitude-induced haemoglobin gains that would otherwise fade after returning to sea level.
