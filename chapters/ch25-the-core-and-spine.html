The Core & Spine

Bracing, intra-abdominal pressure and anti-movement

Stand a two-litre bottle on its end, empty, with the cap off, and press down on it. It folds instantly. Seal the cap and press again, and it barely yields — the same thin plastic, made rigid by pressurised air inside with nowhere to escape. The muscles this chapter covers do roughly that for your trunk: not by being especially large or especially strong in isolation, but by sealing a pressurised canister around the spine so that force can cross it without the spine itself having to absorb the load as bending. That single idea — the core as a pressure vessel and a force-transmission system, not a set of muscles that "tone the stomach" — is the organising fact behind everything below.

25.1What the Core Actually Does

The word "core" has no fixed anatomical meaning, but for training purposes it covers the muscles that surround the lumbar spine and pelvis: rectus abdominis down the front, the external and internal obliques at the sides, transverse abdominis wrapped around underneath them, erector spinae running up the back of the spine, quadratus lumborum deep in the low back, psoas connecting the spine to the hip, the diaphragm roofing the whole structure, and the pelvic floor forming its base. Most of these muscles spend most of their working lives doing the opposite of what a crunch trains: not producing spinal movement, but preventing it, while force generated by the legs or arms passes through the trunk on its way somewhere else.

A deadlift is the clearest example. The lift is produced by the glutes and hamstrings extending the hip; the bar's weight tries to bend the spine forward at the same time. The job of the abdominal wall and erector spinae in that lift is not to move the spine — a lifter who lets the spine move under that load is in trouble — it is to resist the bending force so that hip-extension power reaches the bar instead of being lost to the spine folding partway. The same principle runs through a tennis serve, a punch, and a sprint stride: in each case force is generated at one end of the body and has to cross the trunk to reach the other end, or to reach the ground, and the core's job is to make that crossing efficient by holding the trunk rigid while it happens.

In plain terms

An empty cardboard box collapses if you stand on it. Fill the same box with tightly packed, slightly pressurised foam and it holds your weight easily — nothing about the cardboard changed, only what is pressing outward against it from the inside. Your trunk works the same way. The diaphragm is the lid, the pelvic floor is the base, and the abdominal wall and spinal muscles are the sides. Brace properly before a heavy lift and you are pressurising the box; a "weak core" is usually a lid, base or wall that has not learned to hold that pressure, not a muscle that is too small.

25.2Intra-Abdominal Pressure & Bracing

Intra-abdominal pressure (IAP) is the pressure inside the sealed abdominal cavity, raised deliberately before a heavy lift by contracting the diaphragm downward, the pelvic floor upward, and the abdominal wall inward, all at once, against each other. The effect is mechanical, not mystical: a pressurised cylinder resists bending far better than an unpressurised one of the same wall thickness, for the same reason a slightly inflated bicycle tyre holds its shape under a rider's weight while a flat one folds. Raising IAP effectively stiffens the trunk from the inside, taking some of the bending load that would otherwise fall entirely on the spine's ligaments, discs and the erector spinae working alone.

The practical skill, usually called bracing, is to take a breath into the belly rather than the chest, then tighten the abdominal wall in every direction — forward, sideways and into the lower back — as though expecting a punch, and hold that brace through the lift rather than releasing it at the hardest point. This differs from a maximal Valsalva manoeuvre — holding the breath fully against a closed throat, which raises pressure further still and is common, deliberately, in maximal powerlifting attempts — mainly in degree: a working brace for ordinary training holds pressure firmly without sealing the airway completely, while a true Valsalva is reserved for near-maximal single efforts because it briefly raises blood pressure substantially and is avoided by anyone with a cardiovascular condition that makes that unwise.

A weightlifting belt does not replace this skill; it gives the abdominal wall something firmer to press against, which measurably raises the IAP a lifter can generate for a given bracing effort B. It is best understood as a performance tool for near-maximal lifts, not a substitute for learning to brace, and lifters who rely on a belt for every working set without ever training unbelted tend to brace noticeably worse the one time the belt is not available.

25.3The Four Anti-Movement Categories

Because the core's real job is resisting motion rather than producing it, the clearest way to programme it is by the direction of force it has to resist, not by which muscle is "worked." Four categories cover nearly everything a trunk is asked to resist in daily life and in sport.

The four anti-movement categories, what they resist, and an exercise for each
CategoryWhat it resistsPrime musclesExample exercise
Anti-extensionThe spine being pulled into an arched, over-extended position by a load in front of the body or gravity on a horizontal trunkRectus abdominis, obliquesPlank, ab wheel rollout, front-loaded (Zercher or goblet) carry
Anti-lateral-flexionThe spine being pulled sideways by an uneven loadObliques, quadratus lumborumSuitcase carry, side plank, single-arm farmer carry
Anti-rotationThe spine being twisted by a force applied off-centreInternal and external obliques, transverse abdominisPallof press, landmine anti-rotation hold
Anti-flexionThe spine being pulled into forward rounding by a load in front of or below the bodyErector spinae, psoas, the abdominal wall acting isometricallyDeadlift, good morning, loaded carry with a tall brace

Most real core injuries and most real core weaknesses are a mismatch between which of these four a sport demands and which a lifter actually trains. A rower who trains only anti-extension (planks) while rowing under enormous anti-flexion demand every stroke is training the wrong category; a field-sport athlete cutting sharply needs anti-lateral-flexion and anti-rotation capacity that a standard ab-focused programme, built around crunches and planks, rarely touches at all.

Activate it

Covering all four categories without turning core training into its own separate session.

  • Do One exercise per category: front-loaded carry (anti-extension), suitcase carry (anti-lateral-flexion), Pallof press (anti-rotation), heavy hinge with a tall brace (anti-flexion, usually already in the programme as a squat or deadlift).
  • Load Heavy enough on the carries that the last 10–15 m genuinely challenges the brace; light-to-moderate and strict on the Pallof press.
  • Dose 2–3 sets of each, 2×/week; the anti-flexion category is usually already covered by existing squat and deadlift work.
  • When At the end of a session, once the main lifts are done, since trunk fatigue degrades bracing on anything heavier that follows.
  • Progress Increase carry distance or Pallof press lever length before adding load.
  • On the bike Hard, out-of-the-saddle climbing efforts recruit the obliques and erector spinae isometrically to resist the bike rocking side to side under each pedal stroke — a genuine, if modest, anti-lateral-flexion stimulus that a seated session does not provide.
  • Signal You're doing it right when the carry or hold fails from fatigue, not from the trunk visibly bending first.

25.4The Diaphragm's Conflict — Breathing and Bracing Compete

The diaphragm has two jobs that work against each other under heavy load. Its first job, covered in full in chapter 10, is ventilation: it flattens and drops with every breath to pull air into the lungs. Its second job, described above, is to act as the pressurised lid of the abdominal canister, pressing down firmly and staying put while the abdominal wall presses inward against it. Those two jobs need opposite things from the same muscle at the same moment — one wants it moving rhythmically, the other wants it held rigid.

During a single heavy rep — one deadlift pull, one maximal press — this is barely a conflict: the lifter takes one brace before the rep and holds it, breathing can wait a few seconds. The conflict becomes real during sustained heavy efforts, high-rep sets, or any sport that requires both a tight trunk and continuous breathing at once — wrestling, rugby contact, a strongman carry over 30 seconds. The usual solution is not to pick one job over the other but to breathe in short, controlled segments within an overall held brace — sometimes called "breathing behind the brace" — keeping the abdominal wall's outward pressure roughly constant while the diaphragm cycles a smaller breath on top of it, rather than fully releasing the brace to take a full breath and resetting it.

Key idea

A brace that collapses every time you breathe is not a brace, it is a single held breath mistaken for one. Learning to keep the abdominal wall's outward tension roughly constant while still cycling air through the diaphragm — not holding the breath for the whole set — is the actual skill behind "breathing while bracing," and it takes deliberate practice because it is not the body's default pattern.

25.5Spinal Loading & Back Pain — What the Evidence Actually Shows

Two beliefs are repeated often enough in gyms and clinics that they function as background assumptions: that spinal flexion under load is inherently dangerous, and that a weak core is the reason people get back pain. Both deserve more scrutiny than they usually get.

Myth vs fact
“A weak core causes back pain, so building core strength prevents and fixes it”

The standard justification for prescribing core exercise to anyone with a bad back.

The causal story is weaker than commonly claimed

A detailed review of the core-stability literature found that measures like isometric back-extension endurance and grip strength — often used as stand-ins for "core strength" — do not reliably predict who develops long-term low back pain or who recovers from it B. Core-focused exercise does help some people with existing back pain feel and function better in the short term, which is a genuinely useful clinical tool, but that treatment effect does not prove that weakness caused the pain in the first place, any more than paracetamol relieving a headache proves the headache was caused by a paracetamol deficiency.

The paper
Lederman E. The myth of core stability. J Bodyw Mov Ther 2010;14(1):84–98.
Who

A narrative review of the core-stability research base, not a single trial.

What

Examined the evidence behind the claim that deep trunk muscles, particularly transverse abdominis, provide a special stabilising function that other muscles cannot, and that training them specifically is necessary for a healthy back.

Found

The specific-muscle version of the theory is poorly supported: transverse abdominis is one contributor among several, timing differences seen in people with back pain are modest and inconsistently replicated, and general exercise performs comparably to core-specific protocols in most trials.

Why it matters: it reframes "core stability" from a special, separate system requiring dedicated clinical exercises into one part of ordinary, general strength and movement capacity. Limitation: a narrative review, not a meta-analysis with pooled effect sizes, and it argues against a strong specific claim without asserting that core exercise is useless — only that its mechanism is less exotic than marketed.

On spinal flexion specifically, a separate and more recent thread of research pushes back against treating any rounding of the lower back under load as automatically dangerous. Reviews of lifting biomechanics have found no consistent prospective evidence that moderate lumbar flexion, by itself, raises injury risk in people who have built tolerance to it gradually C — the deadlift's long-length erector-spinae stimulus, described in the anatomy table above, necessarily involves some spinal flexion under load, and treating that position as inherently injurious would rule out training the erector spinae at the length where it grows best. What the evidence points to instead as the more reliable predictor of injury is load management: sudden jumps in training volume or intensity relative to what the tissue has adapted to, rather than the specific joint angle at which the load is carried. That is the same principle chapter 1 introduced for connective tissue generally and chapter 39 develops in full: tissues fail when demand outpaces adaptation, not simply when a joint reaches a particular angle.

None of this means spinal position is irrelevant or that technique coaching is pointless — a spine that collapses unpredictably under near-maximal load, rather than flexing in a controlled, trained pattern, is a different and genuinely higher-risk situation. The distinction is between controlled flexion within a range the tissue has been progressively exposed to, and uncontrolled flexion imposed suddenly by a load that has outrun the lifter's current tolerance.

25.6The Pelvic Floor

The pelvic floor is the core canister's base, and it works as a genuine partner to the diaphragm and abdominal wall: in a well-coordinated brace, the pelvic floor lifts slightly as the diaphragm presses down and the abdominal wall tightens inward, all three containing the same rise in pressure together. When that coordination fails, the commonest visible sign is stress urinary incontinence — leaking under a sudden rise in abdominal pressure, such as a heavy lift, a jump landing, or a cough — which is reported by a substantial minority of women in heavy strength sports and is not a normal, unavoidable cost of lifting heavy, whatever gym folklore suggests.

Postpartum recovery is the clearest case where this matters practically. Pregnancy and vaginal delivery stretch and can weaken the pelvic floor and the connective tissue of the abdominal wall measurably, and returning to heavy bracing-dependent lifting before that tissue has been progressively reloaded — mirroring the slow connective-tissue timescale from chapter 1 rather than the faster return of general fitness — is a common, avoidable cause of both incontinence and, less commonly, pelvic organ prolapse. A graded return that rebuilds pelvic floor and deep abdominal control before reintroducing heavy axial load is standard guidance, not an excess of caution.

Worked example
Priya, 31, back squatting and deadlifting again eight weeks postpartum, notices leaking on the heaviest sets of the week.
  1. Leaking under load at eight weeks postpartum, with tissue still on its slow connective-tissue timeline, is a signal the brace is being asked to contain more pressure than the pelvic floor currently manages well.Not a strength problem to push through
  2. Reduce axial load temporarily; keep training volume via unilateral and machine-based work that needs less maximal bracing.Back squat/deadlift paused; split squat and leg press continue
  3. Add explicit pelvic-floor coordination work: a slow exhale with a gentle lift of the pelvic floor, practised outside the gym before it is asked to work under a loaded brace.Daily, 2–3 short sessions
  4. Reintroduce axial loading in small increments once symptom-free at the previous load for a full week.+5–10% load per step
  5. Expect the full return to take longer than general fitness recovery, often 3–6 months, because connective tissue and the pelvic floor adapt on that slower clock.Progress measured in months, not weeks
So: leaking under heavy load is useful information, not something to brace through harder; a temporary reduction in axial load paired with direct pelvic-floor work resolves it far more reliably than continuing to push the same weights.

Heavy lifting is not, by itself, harmful to a healthy pelvic floor — strength athletes as a group do not show uniformly worse pelvic-floor function than sedentary women, and some data suggests trained lifters cope with pressure spikes better once the pelvic floor has been conditioned alongside the rest of the brace. The risk is concentrated in the mismatch: heavy axial load reintroduced faster than the pelvic floor's own, slower recovery timeline, which is exactly the same demand-versus-adaptation story that runs through every connective-tissue injury in this book.

Five things to carry forward

  1. The core's main job is transmitting force and resisting unwanted spinal movement, not producing trunk flexion — a crunch trains one minor function of one of eight muscles covered in this chapter.
  2. Bracing works by pressurising the trunk like a sealed cylinder, with the diaphragm as the lid and the pelvic floor as the base; a weightlifting belt raises the pressure a given brace can achieve but does not replace learning to brace.
  3. Core training is best organised by the direction of force it resists — anti-extension, anti-lateral-flexion, anti-rotation, anti-flexion — because most real-world core failures are a mismatch between what a sport demands and which of these four was actually trained.
  4. The evidence that a weak core specifically causes back pain is substantially weaker than commonly claimed, and moderate spinal flexion under load is not inherently injurious in a lifter who has built tolerance to it gradually; load management, not joint angle, is the stronger predictor of injury.
  5. The pelvic floor is a genuine partner in bracing, not a separate concern, and it adapts on the same slow connective-tissue timescale as tendon and bone — which is why postpartum and heavy-lifting pelvic-floor problems are usually a pacing issue, not a sign that lifting itself is unsafe.
