A second-hand car salesman's oldest trick works just as well in a supplement shop: dress a small, genuine advantage up in enough packaging and jargon that it feels like the main event. Creatine, caffeine and beta-alanine really do work, with doses and effect sizes as well established as almost anything else in this book. Honestly measured, though, they buy an athlete who already trains hard, sleeps enough and eats properly somewhere between one and two percent. Everything in this chapter sits inside that number — including the handful of things genuinely worth your money.

The Hierarchy That Makes Supplements Almost Irrelevant

Rank what actually changes performance, by size of effect, and supplements fall off the bottom of the list almost immediately. Training stimulus — the right sessions, at the right dose, repeated for months — produces improvements measured in tens of percent. Sleep, covered in chapter 36, governs whether that stimulus is actually absorbed into adaptation rather than accumulated as fatigue. Total energy intake decides whether the body has the raw material and the hormonal environment to adapt at all — chronic underfuelling, discussed in full in chapter 34, can erase a training block's gains outright. Protein intake, at the right daily total and distribution (chapter 29), decides how much of that energy actually becomes tissue. Only once all four of those are in reasonable order does anything in this chapter start to matter.

This is not a rhetorical flourish to make a chapter on supplements sound humble. It is an honest statement of effect size. A well-designed strength block adds kilograms to a lift. Closing a protein gap can be the difference between a training block that builds muscle and one that does not. The best-supported ergogenic supplement in existence — creatine — adds perhaps 5–15% to performance in a narrow category of effort (repeated high-intensity work) and essentially nothing to a 10 km run. Supplements occupy the top of the pyramid, not the base, and no dose of anything in this chapter substitutes for a session not done, a night not slept, or a plate left half full.

Think of performance like a household budget. Training, sleep, energy and protein are the mortgage, the heating bill and groceries — get those wrong and nothing else you do with money matters. Supplements are the loyalty-card points on top of groceries you were buying anyway: real, worth collecting if they're free or cheap, and completely unable to pay the mortgage for you. The industry's entire pitch depends on you forgetting which bill is which.

Creatine monohydrate is the most robustly supported sports supplement there is A. It works by topping up the muscle's phosphocreatine pool, the fastest-regenerating fuel for the first few seconds of hard effort (the full mechanism is in chapter 7); more stored phosphocreatine means more repeated high-intensity efforts before fatigue forces a slowdown. The dose is 3–5 g/day, taken any time, or a loading protocol of 20 g/day split across the day for 5–7 days if you want the muscle stores saturated faster. Only monohydrate has this evidence base — no other form (buffered, ethyl ester, nitrate, "liquid") has been shown to outperform it, and most are simply more expensive ways to buy the same molecule. It is safe at doses up to 30 g/day for five years in the healthy populations studied, and the 1–2 kg of early weight gain it produces is water pulled into the muscle cell itself (intracellular), not the bloating or subcutaneous water retention people sometimes fear. It does nothing for a single maximal lift attempted once, and it does not turn an endurance athlete into a sprinter — its benefit is confined to repeated efforts in the seconds-to-couple-of-minutes range, set after set, rep after rep.

Caffeine works through a completely different route: it blocks adenosine receptors in the brain, and adenosine is one of the signals that makes effort feel effortful. The practical effect is a lowered perception of effort at a given intensity, which lets you hold a pace or a load that would otherwise feel too hard to sustain. The dose is 3–6 mg/kg, taken 45–60 minutes before the session (a flat 200 mg is a reasonable default for most adults), producing a genuine, replicated 2–4% endurance benefit and a smaller but real effect in strength and sprint work. Two caveats matter in practice. Habituation blunts the effect with daily use, which is why some athletes deliberately taper caffeine intake in the days before a key race to restore sensitivity. And CYP1A2 genotype — the gene that controls how fast you metabolise caffeine — varies enough between individuals that the same dose leaves one athlete sharp and another jittery and anxious; there is no substitute for testing your own response in training before relying on it in competition.

Beta-alanine raises the muscle's store of carnosine, a molecule that buffers the hydrogen ions produced by hard anaerobic work — the same acidosis that is part of why a 400 m or a hard 2 km row burns and forces you to slow. The dose is 3.2–6.4 g/day, split into smaller doses across the day (a single large dose causes a harmless but unpleasant skin tingling, paraesthesia), continued for 4–12 weeks to let carnosine accumulate. The effect, roughly 2–3%, is real but tightly confined to efforts lasting one to ten minutes — it is genuinely useless for a 3-second effort, where there is no time for acidosis to matter, and equally useless for a marathon, where the limiting factor is fuel and heat, not hydrogen ion buffering. This is the clearest example in the chapter of a supplement whose value depends entirely on matching it to the right event duration.

Dietary nitrate, from beetroot juice or concentrated shots, is the most context-dependent of the four. Nitrate is converted by oral bacteria into nitrite, then into nitric oxide, which widens blood vessels and improves the efficiency of type II muscle fibres — the combined effect is that the same pace costs slightly less oxygen. The dose is 6–8 mmol of nitrate, 2–3 hours before a session, or daily loading for 3–15 days for a steadier effect, producing roughly 1–3% in time-trial performance, clearest in recreational and sub-elite athletes B. The benefit consistently shrinks in highly trained athletes, plausibly because their muscles are already closer to maximally efficient. The one rule that trips people up: do not use antibacterial mouthwash in the hours around dosing, because the oral bacteria that perform the first conversion step — nitrate to nitrite — are exactly what mouthwash is designed to kill, and without them the whole pathway stalls before it starts.

Sodium Bicarbonate — The Buffer With A Catch

Where beta-alanine buffers acid inside the muscle cell, sodium bicarbonate buffers it outside, raising the blood's capacity to soak up hydrogen ions as they're exported from working muscle. The two mechanisms are complementary rather than redundant, which is why athletes who use both together see additive, not overlapping, benefit in the right event.

The dose is 0.2–0.3 g per kilogram of body mass, taken 60–180 minutes before the effort, or a split protocol of 0.4–0.5 g/kg per day divided into smaller doses to reduce side effects. The benefit, roughly 2%, is concentrated in severe-intensity efforts — the same one-to-ten-minute window beta-alanine targets, plus repeated-sprint sports and some rowing and swimming events. The limiting factor is almost always gastrointestinal distress: bloating, cramping, urgent diarrhoea, and occasionally vomiting, which is unpleasant enough that a meaningful fraction of athletes who could benefit from bicarbonate never get to try it twice. Splitting the dose, taking it with a carbohydrate-rich meal rather than on an empty stomach, and — critically — rehearsing the exact dose and timing in training rather than discovering your gut's tolerance on competition day, are the three things that most reliably turn bicarbonate from a liability into a usable 2%.

The Honest “Maybes”

A second tier of supplements has a plausible mechanism and some positive trials, but the evidence is thinner, more mixed, or answers a narrower question than the marketing implies. None of these is harmful at the doses studied; none is a reason to skip anything higher up the hierarchy.

Collagen plus vitamin C, taken as roughly 15 g of collagen with about 50 mg of vitamin C, 60 minutes before loading, raises measurable markers of collagen synthesis in tendon tissue around the loaded session C. What it has not clearly shown is that this translates into a functional benefit — faster tendon stiffening, fewer injuries — beyond what the mechanical loading itself is already doing; the loading, discussed in chapter 19, is doing most of the real work, and the supplement is a plausible, low-risk amplifier rather than a proven one.

Citrulline malate, around 6–8 g, 60 minutes before training, is proposed to improve blood flow and reduce the perceived effort of resistance training, and some well-designed trials find a small increase in reps completed at a given load; others find nothing C. It is low-risk and genuinely might help a given individual add a rep or two per set; it is not something to expect a measurable training-outcome difference from over a full programme.

HMB (beta-hydroxy-beta-methylbutyrate) produced striking early results in untrained or detrained populations, which is exactly the population least representative of someone already training properly. In trained athletes, the effect on muscle or strength is negligible C; it may have a role for someone returning from injury or a long layoff, where the body is briefly in a more catabolic state, but it is not a muscle-building supplement for an athlete already training consistently.

Ketone esters supply an alternative fuel directly, bypassing the need to produce ketones metabolically. The evidence for a performance benefit is inconsistent, some trials have found impaired high-intensity performance rather than improved, the taste and gastrointestinal tolerance are genuinely unpleasant for most people, and the products remain expensive C. A possible role in post-exercise recovery signalling is an open research question, not yet a recommendation.

Tart cherry juice (or concentrate), typically around two 250–300 mL servings a day for several days before and after a hard training block or race, is marketed on its anthocyanin and polyphenol content. Some trials show modest reductions in muscle soreness and faster recovery of strength after very damaging exercise; others show no effect on actual performance in the following days C. It is food, essentially harmless, and a reasonable thing to try around an unusually hard block if you enjoy it — it is not something to expect a reliable, sizeable effect from.

What Doesn't Work

BCAAs (branched-chain amino acids, sold as a powder of leucine, isoleucine and valine alone) do not build muscle beyond what adequate whole protein already provides D. Muscle protein synthesis needs a complete set of essential amino acids, not three of them in isolation; a BCAA shake next to an otherwise inadequate diet still leaves the diet inadequate. This is one of the clearest cases in the whole chapter of a supplement that was superseded, not by a counter-argument, but by simply eating enough complete protein — see chapter 29.

Testosterone boosters — tribulus terrestris, D-aspartic acid, fenugreek blends, and similar over-the-counter products — do not meaningfully raise testosterone in healthy men at the doses sold D, and even the modest hormonal shifts occasionally reported in small trials are far below the magnitude that would be needed to change muscle mass or strength. The entire category trades on borrowing credibility from the real, large effects of prescription anabolic hormones without delivering anything close to the dose or mechanism that would produce them.

Most over-the-counter "fat burners" are a blend of stimulants (often caffeine, already covered above, plus smaller amounts of green tea extract, synephrine or similar), sold at doses too low to add meaningfully to caffeine alone, with the actual fat-loss outcome resting on the same energy-balance principles covered in chapter 30 and chapter 34 D. Where they contain genuinely active, higher-dose stimulant combinations, the risk — elevated heart rate and blood pressure, anxiety, poor sleep — rises faster than any fat-loss benefit.

High-dose, isolated antioxidant supplementation (large single-nutrient doses of vitamin C or vitamin E, well above food levels, taken around training) does not improve performance and can actively blunt the training adaptation it is marketed to protect D for the performance claim. The mechanism — antioxidants interfering with the same reactive-oxygen signalling that tells a muscle cell to build more mitochondria — is covered in full in chapter 31's antioxidant paradox; the short version here is simply that this is one of the few places in sports nutrition where "more of a good thing" measurably backfires.

“It's on the shelf next to creatine and caffeine, so it's probably backed by similar evidence”

Supplement aisles and websites display proven and disproven products side by side with equal confidence.

Shelf placement tracks profit margin, not evidence grade

BCAAs, testosterone boosters and most fat burners sell well precisely because the story is simple and flattering, not because the trials are strong. The honest grade for each — D, no credible performance benefit — sits right next to A-grade creatine on the same shelf, in the same tone of packaging, with no signal to the buyer about the gulf in evidence between them.

Contamination And Anti-Doping

Independent testing of commercial supplements has repeatedly found products contaminated with substances not listed on the label — most seriously, anabolic agents and stimulants banned under anti-doping codes, turning up at meaningful rates in pre-workout formulas, "fat burners" and some protein powders B for the contamination risk being real and recurring across independent surveys, even though exact rates vary by product category and market. For a recreational athlete this is mostly a safety and quality issue. For anyone subject to anti-doping testing — which, depending on your sport, can mean a club-level or age-group athlete, not only elite competitors — it is a career issue: a positive test caused by an undeclared contaminant in a protein powder is treated, under strict liability rules, the same as deliberate use.

For any athlete subject to drug testing, third-party certification — schemes such as Informed Sport or NSF Certified for Sport, which batch-test finished products against the current prohibited list — is non-negotiable, not an optional extra. It does not guarantee the product works; it only reduces, without fully eliminating, the risk that it contains something that will end a career. "Pharmaceutical grade," "GMP certified" and similar label language refer to manufacturing hygiene standards, not anti-doping screening, and should not be mistaken for it. If you are tested in your sport, check certification before you check the price.

How To Evaluate A New Supplement Yourself

New products arrive faster than any book chapter can individually grade them. The following six questions, asked in order, sort almost everything on a shop shelf without needing a literature search each time.

  1. Is the mechanism plausible and specific?

    Does the claimed effect target a real physiological bottleneck (acid buffering, phosphocreatine, a genuine deficiency), or is it a vague appeal to "energy," "toxins," or "optimisation"?

  2. Does the dose match the research?

    Many products underdose an ingredient that works at a higher amount than the label contains — check the number against this chapter's doses, not against the front of the packet.

  3. Who ran the trials?

    Independent, replicated trials across several research groups carry more weight than a single manufacturer-funded study, especially one with a small sample and no control group.

  4. What is the realistic effect size?

    If the claim implies a double-digit percentage change from a pill or powder alone, be sceptical by default — almost nothing in this chapter clears 5%, and the things that do are narrowly specific to one type of effort.

  5. Is it third-party tested, if that matters to you?

    Non-negotiable for anyone subject to doping control; a sensible habit for anyone else who wants to know what they are actually swallowing.

  6. What does it cost relative to what it buys?

    Price per effective dose, not per tub — and compared honestly against spending the same money and attention on food, sleep or a session you've been skipping.

A sensible, evidence-weighted supplement stack for an athlete who has already got training, sleep, energy and protein right.

  • Dose Creatine monohydrate 3–5 g daily, taken any time, indefinitely.
  • Dose Caffeine 3–6 mg/kg, 45–60 min before sessions or races that matter — not every single day, to protect sensitivity.
  • Timing If your event is 1–10 minutes of hard, repeated effort, add beta-alanine 3.2–6.4 g/day in split doses, started 4–12 weeks out, and consider sodium bicarbonate 0.2–0.3 g/kg 60–180 min pre-event on top — the two stack additively.
  • Test Trial every new addition in training first, never for the first time on race day — bicarbonate's gut tolerance and caffeine's individual response both need rehearsal.
  • On the bike An indoor trainer session is the ideal place to test sodium bicarbonate dosing and timing: effort and duration are controllable, and if gastrointestinal distress hits, you are five metres from a toilet rather than mid-race.
  • Signal You're getting the stack right when you can name, for each product in your cupboard, exactly which grade-A or grade-B mechanism it targets and in which of your events — if you can't, it's a candidate to drop.

Five things to carry forward

  1. Training, sleep, total energy and protein intake dwarf every supplement in this chapter in effect size — nothing here works until those four are already in order.
  2. Creatine monohydrate (3–5 g/day), caffeine (3–6 mg/kg pre-session) and beta-alanine (3.2–6.4 g/day for 4–12 weeks) are the most robustly supported performance supplements, each tied to a specific, narrow category of effort rather than to fitness in general.
  3. Sodium bicarbonate adds roughly 2% in severe-intensity efforts and stacks with beta-alanine, but gastrointestinal distress is the real limiting factor and must be rehearsed in training, never tried cold on race day.
  4. BCAAs, testosterone boosters, most fat burners and high-dose isolated antioxidants have no credible performance benefit, and the antioxidants specifically can blunt the training adaptation they are marketed to protect.
  5. For anyone subject to anti-doping testing, third-party certification of every product is non-negotiable, because contaminated supplements are treated under strict liability the same as deliberate doping.
