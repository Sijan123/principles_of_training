Put a 20-kilogram barbell and a 2-kilogram dumbbell in front of the same muscle and ask it to grow, and the muscle does not much care which one you chose — it cares how close to failure the set finished. That single, repeatedly replicated result overturns a century of gym folklore about “the right rep range for size”. Hypertrophy is not a reward for a particular number on the bar. It is the delayed, cumulative answer to a mechanical question asked at the end of nearly every hard set: how much tension did the fibre have to produce, and how near to its limit was it when it produced it?

What Actually Grows

Hypertrophy is an increase in the cross-sectional area of existing muscle fibres, built by adding more of the contractile machinery — actin and myosin filaments — inside them. Adult humans do not meaningfully add new fibres; they thicken the ones they have, the way a warehouse grows not by building new buildings but by packing more shelving into the ones that already exist.

The shelving is never finished. Muscle protein is in constant turnover: muscle protein synthesis (MPS, building new protein) runs continuously alongside muscle protein breakdown (MPB, demolishing old or damaged protein). At rest in a fed, trained adult the two roughly balance. A hard resistance session knocks that balance over — MPS can rise 50–100% above its resting rate within a few hours in someone unaccustomed to the exercise, and stays measurably elevated for roughly 24–48 hours afterwards, gradually returning to baseline. Breakdown rises too, by a smaller amount. Growth is simply what is left when you integrate synthesis minus breakdown over that whole window, session after session, for months.

Two things follow immediately, and the rest of this chapter is mostly commentary on them. First, a single session does not build muscle — it opens a 24–48 hour window in which muscle can be built, provided the raw material (amino acids, principally from dietary protein, see chapter 29) and the energy to build with are both available. Second, because that window closes well before a week is out, hitting a muscle only once a week leaves real synthetic capacity on the table — a point this chapter returns to under volume and frequency.

Think of a muscle fibre as a warehouse with a ledger clerk standing at the door. Every day, some stock leaves (breakdown) and some arrives (synthesis), and most days the ledger comes out roughly even.

A hard set of squats is a memo to the clerk: this warehouse needs to hold more stock than it currently does. The clerk does not act immediately — there is no stock on the loading dock during the set itself. For the next day or two, deliveries simply outpace removals, a little at a time, and only if there is stock available to deliver (protein, calories) and someone free to shelve it (which is also why a warehouse that never gets a quiet night — no sleep, no rest day — never quite catches up).

Mechanical Tension Is The Primary Driver

Three things happen inside a muscle during a hard set, and gym culture has historically confused which one matters. There is mechanical tension — the physical force the fibre is made to produce and resist. There is metabolic stress — the burn, the pump, the pooling of metabolites when blood flow is restricted by sustained contraction. And there is muscle damage — microscopic disruption to the fibre's internal structure, felt the next day as soreness. Three decades of training advice treated all three as more or less interchangeable routes to growth. The evidence now points at one of them as the engine and the other two as, at best, passengers.

Tension is detected by a process called mechanotransduction: physical stretch and force on proteins woven through the fibre's internal scaffold and outer membrane literally changes their shape, and that shape change is the signal. It converges on a single molecular switch, mTORC1 (mechanistic target of rapamycin complex 1) — the master controller that, once switched on, tells the cell's protein-building machinery to run faster. You will meet mTORC1 again in chapter 12 and in chapter 20, where endurance exercise turns out to suppress it. Here, the point is simpler: load the fibre hard enough, for long enough, and this switch goes on, roughly in proportion to how much tension was produced and for how long.

Metabolic stress and muscle damage, by contrast, are mostly correlated with growth rather than causal for it. Both tend to accompany big, tension-heavy sets, which is why they got the credit. But when researchers have manipulated them somewhat independently — occluding blood flow to increase metabolic stress during low-load work, or deliberately emphasising the lengthening (eccentric) phase to increase damage — the extra growth on top of a tension-matched comparison is small and inconsistent. Soreness the next day tells you a movement was unfamiliar, not that it worked; a well-adapted lifter making steady gains is often barely sore at all (see chapter 36).

Tension also recruits a repair crew that matters for how much a fibre can ultimately grow. Mature muscle fibres cannot divide, but satellite cells — small stem cells sitting on the outside of each fibre — can. Heavy mechanical loading activates them; some multiply and fuse their nuclei directly into the fibre, a process called myonuclear addition. Each nucleus in a fibre runs its own patch of surrounding cytoplasm (its “domain”), and more nuclei raise the ceiling on how much protein-building machinery that patch can support. This is also the leading explanation for why muscle regained after a layoff comes back faster than it was built the first time: the extra nuclei acquired during earlier training appear to persist for a long time, a biological head start sometimes called muscle memory.

Mechanical tension is necessary for hypertrophy. The burn and the soreness are not — they are common passengers on the same vehicle, not the engine. Train for tension and the other two will show up occasionally on their own; chase the burn or the soreness directly and you can end up working hard without producing much of the one thing that actually matters.

Load Is Flexible, Effort Is Not

If tension is the driver, an obvious question follows: does a heavier bar always produce more tension, and therefore more growth? The surprising answer, now replicated across dozens of trials and several meta-analyses, is no — not once a set is taken close enough to failure.

In one frequently cited ten-week trial, untrained men training to failure at either 30% or 80% of their one-repetition maximum (1RM) produced statistically indistinguishable increases in quadriceps muscle volume (roughly 6.8% versus 7.2%) and in individual fibre cross-sectional area. Across the wider literature, meta-analyses comparing loads from as low as ~30% of 1RM up to ~85%+ find the same pattern when sets are matched for proximity to failure: whole-muscle hypertrophy comes out roughly equal. A

The mechanism is the size principle, covered in detail in chapter 5: the nervous system recruits motor units in a fixed order, smallest and most fatigue-resistant first, largest and most growth-capable last. A heavy set reaches the large, high-threshold motor units almost immediately. A light set does not recruit them at the start — but as the easily-recruited fibres fatigue over the course of a long set, the nervous system is forced to call in the larger ones to keep producing the same force. By the final few repetitions of a light-load set taken to real failure, essentially the same fibres are working at essentially the same relative tension as in a heavy set. The weight on the bar decided how many repetitions it took to get there; it did not decide whether you got there.

This has a liberating practical consequence: rep range is mostly a matter of preference, joint comfort and time, not a determinant of how much muscle you can build. Heavy, low-rep sets reach the same endpoint faster but load joints and the nervous system harder per set. Light, high-rep sets are gentler on joints and cheaper on the central nervous system but take longer and feel considerably worse while you are doing them, since metabolic stress climbs the longer a set runs. Strength itself is a partial exception — translating a gain in muscle into a bigger 1RM benefits from practising with loads similar to the ones you will be tested on, which is why chapter 16 treats load specificity as its own variable. For pure size, it is close to a free choice.

“Light weights and high reps are for toning; only heavy weight builds real muscle”

So anything under about 70% of 1RM is wasted effort for size.

There is no separate biological process called “toning”

A muscle fibre does not know the number on the plates — only the tension it is being asked to produce and how close to its limit that was. Light-load sets build the same tissue through the same mechanism, provided they are carried close enough to failure to recruit the same large motor units. The real distinction is not light versus heavy; it is close to failure versus not.

How Much Volume Actually Works

Volume, for this purpose, means the number of hard sets a muscle does in a week. Counting it honestly requires a distinction most lifters skip: a direct set trains a muscle as a prime mover (a lat pulldown directly trains the lats); an indirect set trains it as a secondary helper (a barbell row also loads the biceps, just less). A sensible way to tally weekly volume, and the method used in the research below, is to count direct sets as one full set and indirect sets as roughly half a set — a fractional set.

The best current answer to “how much volume works” comes from a 2025 meta-regression of 67 studies and just over 2,000 lifters: weekly set volume and hypertrophy rise together with a 100% posterior probability of a positive relationship — about as close to certain as this literature gets — but the relationship bends hard. Early sets buy a lot; each additional set beyond roughly 10–20 per muscle per week buys steadily less. The same analysis found that weekly frequency — how many sessions that volume is split across — independently improved strength but did not independently improve hypertrophy once total weekly volume was accounted for. In other words, ten sets split 2+2+3+3 across four sessions and the same ten sets done 5+5 across two produce, on average, the same size outcome. A

A companion analysis from the same group asked the question the other way round — not how much volume per week, but how much volume in one sitting keeps paying off — and located a point of diminishing within-session returns at roughly 11 fractional sets for one muscle in a single session. Past that point, additional sets in the same sitting add little that an extra session later in the week would not add more cheaply. This is a practical argument for spreading a large weekly total across more sessions, not a claim that frequency itself drives growth.

Put together, the realistic range for a trained lifter sits around 10–20+ direct-equivalent sets per muscle per week, with the lower end suiting beginners, people training many muscles at once, or anyone short on recovery capacity, and the upper end suiting specialisation phases for a lagging muscle — provided it can be fitted in without dragging per-session volume for that muscle much past the ~11-set saturation point.

Proximity To Failure

Reps in reserve (RIR) is a simple scale for how close a set finished to failure: RIR 0 means you could not have done another rep with good form; RIR 3 means you stopped with three left in the tank. A large 2024 meta-regression spanning 55 hypertrophy studies and 67 strength studies modelled proximity to failure as a continuous variable rather than a simple failure/non-failure split, and found a clean, one-directional relationship for size: hypertrophy improves as sets are taken closer to failure. Training in the roughly 0–4 RIR band produces similar growth when total volume is equated; training consistently at RIR 5 or beyond measurably underperforms. A

Strength showed essentially no such relationship across the same dataset — gains in 1RM were comparable across a wide range of RIR, which matches the picture in chapter 16 that maximal strength responds more to practising near-maximal loads and intent than to grinding every set into the ground. The two goals genuinely want different things here, and a programme that serves both — most do — typically keeps early sets of an exercise at RIR 1–3 and reserves true failure, if it is used at all, for the last set.

The mechanism is the same size principle from earlier in this chapter: the largest, most hypertrophy-responsive motor units are recruited last, as fatigue forces the nervous system to call on them. Stop a set five reps shy of failure and, for many lifters, those units were never meaningfully switched on for that set at all — the volume was real, but the stimulus to the fibres that matter most for size was small.

This is not a case for taking every set of every exercise to failure. Doing so across a whole session compounds fatigue faster than it compounds stimulus, degrades the quality of later sets and exercises, and extends recovery time — which can quietly cut the amount of total weekly volume you can sustain. The evidence favours ending sets close to failure, not necessarily at it, and favours concentrating the hardest efforts on the last set of an exercise rather than spreading maximal effort across all of them.

The Other Variables That Matter

Tension, volume and effort explain most of the variance in hypertrophy outcomes between programmes. A handful of second-order variables still move the needle meaningfully.

A general hypertrophy template for one muscle group, built from the variables above.

  • Do Two or three exercises per muscle per week, at least one trained through a long, stretched range of motion.
  • Load Anything from ~30% to ~85% of 1RM — pick what your joints tolerate and your schedule allows.
  • Dose 10–20 direct-equivalent sets per muscle per week, split across 2–4 sessions, no single session exceeding roughly 11 sets for that muscle.
  • Effort RIR 1–3 on most sets; RIR 0–1 on the final set of an exercise.
  • Rest 2–3 minutes for compound lifts; 60–90 s is acceptable for small isolation exercises.
  • On the bike Standing, low-cadence (50–60 rpm), high-resistance intervals — e.g. 6–8 × 1 min near-maximal standing effort, 2 min easy seated recovery — load the quadriceps and glutes with the same near-failure mechanical tension as a leg press, with none of the axial spinal load of a squat.
  • Signal You're doing it right when the load or reps you can perform at the same RIR creeps up most weeks, and when a tape measure or progress photo, not the mirror on any single day, confirms the trend over 6–8 weeks.

How Fast Can You Actually Grow

Training age sets the ceiling on monthly progress far more than any single technique in this chapter, and the first year is not a reliable preview of years two onward. A previously untrained muscle responds to a novel mechanical stimulus with an outsized early burst — part genuine new tissue, part the neural and fluid shifts described in chapter 1 — and that burst cannot be repeated once the muscle has already adapted to being trained.

Five things to carry forward

  1. Hypertrophy is the delayed result of a 24–48 hour window of elevated muscle protein synthesis after each session — growth happens in the days after training, not during it.
  2. Mechanical tension, sensed through mechanotransduction into the mTORC1 pathway, is the primary driver; metabolic stress and muscle damage are common passengers, not separate routes to the same result.
  3. Loads from roughly 30% to 85%+ of 1RM build muscle equally well when sets are taken close to failure, because the size principle eventually recruits the same large motor units regardless of starting load.
  4. Weekly volume and hypertrophy rise together with clear diminishing returns above roughly 10–20 sets per muscle per week; how that volume is split across sessions matters far less than the weekly total, provided no single session grossly exceeds ~11 sets for one muscle.
  5. Hypertrophy improves the closer a set finishes to failure (0–4 RIR), while strength gains are largely indifferent to it — the two goals genuinely ask for different things on this one variable.
