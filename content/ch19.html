Stand a trained gymnast next to someone who has stretched every morning for a year, and ask each to fold forward and touch their toes. Both can do it. Only one of them changed the length of anything. The other simply taught their nervous system to stop objecting so loudly to a sensation it has felt ten thousand times before. Most of what passes for "flexibility training" is this second thing — a negotiation with your own nervous system, not a renovation of your tissue — and knowing which one you are actually doing changes almost everything about how, and when, it is worth doing.

Flexibility, Mobility And Stability Are Three Different Things

Flexibility is the passive range of motion available at a joint when something else — gravity, a partner, your own hands — moves it there. Mobility is the range you can actively reach and control yourself, under your own muscular effort, always somewhat less than passive flexibility, and the number that actually matters for sport. Stability is the ability to control a joint and resist unwanted movement through that range, rather than simply possessing it. A dancer with enormous passive hip flexibility but no active control at the top of that range has flexibility without mobility; a lifter who braces a spine rigidly through its normal range but has no extra range to spare has stability without surplus flexibility. Training needs are rarely "more flexibility" in the abstract; they are almost always one of these three, specifically.

What chronic stretching actually changes has a clearer answer today than it did a generation ago, and it is not the one most people assume. A widely cited review by Weppler and Magnusson (2010) examined single sessions and short programmes (3–8 weeks) of stretching and concluded the resulting range-of-motion gains were explained by a change in sensation, not in the muscle's resting length: the same joint angle produces less perceived stretch and less protective reflex resistance, even when the tissue's mechanical properties are largely unchanged. This is stretch tolerance. B Whether years-long practice (yoga, gymnastics, dance from childhood) eventually produces genuine structural change is less settled and was outside that review's scope — the honest answer for anyone starting today is that the first weeks to months of benefit are overwhelmingly a nervous-system adaptation, not a lengthened muscle.

Imagine turning up the volume at which a smoke alarm goes off. The wiring in the house has not changed and the amount of smoke needed to set off a fire has not changed — you have just made the alarm less sensitive, so it tolerates more smoke before it screams.

A tight hamstring that "won't stretch" works the same way. The muscle is not a fixed length that stretching slowly extends like warm toffee. It is wired with sensors that raise the alarm — felt as stretch, then pain — at a certain joint angle. Weeks of regular stretching teach those sensors to raise the alarm later, at a bigger angle, for the same actual muscle length. You can touch your toes afterwards. The hamstring itself is barely different.

Static Stretching Before Performance

The research on stretching immediately before a sprint, jump or lift has gone through a genuine correction. Early alarm about static stretching causing large strength and power losses has been replaced by a dose-dependent picture, far less dramatic than the headlines suggested but not nothing either. A 2024 systematic review and multilevel meta-analysis, pooling 83 studies and over 400 effect sizes, found a small overall reduction in maximal strength after acute static stretching (effect size around −0.21), depending heavily on hold duration: under roughly 30 seconds per muscle produced no detectable deficit, while 60 seconds or more produced a substantially larger one (around −0.84). B Jump height and sprint speed — the whole-body outputs most athletes care about — were affected far less consistently than isolated lab strength measures.

Put simply: a few short stretches under 30 seconds each, in a normal warm-up, cost next to nothing. Long holds (60 seconds or more) immediately before a maximal lift, jump or sprint are worth avoiding — the deficit at that duration is real and lasts up to roughly 30–60 minutes before resolving. C

None of this means static stretching has no place. It belongs well after performance matters for the session, or as a dedicated flexibility session, where the goal is raising stretch tolerance over weeks with no imminent maximal effort to blunt. It also suits a sport that specifically rewards passive range — gymnastics, swimming, martial arts — provided the extra range is then trained actively, rather than left as flexibility with no mobility behind it.

The injury-prevention evidence is weak and inconsistent

Large military and athletic cohort studies have generally failed to find that routine pre-exercise static stretching reduces injury rates, and some analyses suggest the acute force deficit from long holds could theoretically work against the explosive stiffness a joint needs under sudden load. A dynamic, movement-specific warm-up has better supporting evidence for injury reduction than static holds do. C

Tendon Structure And Adaptation

A tendon is rope, not rubber, and the distinction matters. It is built almost entirely from type I collagen — a protein wound into triple helices, then into fibrils, then into fibres, the way individual threads are spun into string and string is braided into rope. That rope-like construction gives tendon a distinctive stress–strain curve: a short initial toe region, where slack and the natural crimp in the collagen fibres straighten out under small loads at relatively little mechanical cost; a long, steep linear region, where load and stretch rise together in a predictable straight line and the tendon is doing its main mechanical job, storing and returning elastic energy as described in chapter 18; and, under excessive load, a yield point beyond which fibres begin to fail permanently, heading toward rupture.

Resistance training changes tendon in two measurable ways: it increases cross-sectional area (more rope, physically thicker) and it increases stiffness (less stretch for a given force, a steeper linear region). Both changes raise the load a tendon can tolerate before strain reaches a damaging level, and both take considerably longer to appear than the muscle changes covered in chapter 17 — measurable tendon adaptation generally needs 8–14 weeks of consistent loading, not the days to weeks that neural and early hypertrophic gains take.

The biological reason for that slowness is visible at the level of a single loading session. Collagen is not built on demand the way a fast-turnover protein is; collagen synthesis in human tendon rises after a bout of loading — roughly doubling from its resting rate — and does not peak immediately. It peaks at around 24 hours after the session and remains measurably elevated at 72 hours, considerably later and longer than the muscle protein synthesis curve from chapter 17. For roughly the first 24–36 hours after a hard loading bout, the balance between collagen breakdown and synthesis can actually run slightly negative before turning positive; the net gain in collagen is a slow, delayed signal integrated over several days, not hours. B This single fact explains why tendon-specific training plans use session spacing of 48–72 hours rather than the 24–48 hours typical of a muscle-hypertrophy split — load the tendon again before its synthetic window has finished, and you are interrupting the response rather than adding to it.

The Protocols That Actually Change A Tendon

If tendon responds slowly and specifically to mechanical loading, the training question becomes: which loading pattern produces the most strain, held for the longest useful time, without causing the tendon pain that signals overload? Two protocols dominate the evidence, and both share the same underlying logic — time under high load, not total volume, is the active ingredient.

Heavy slow resistance (HSR) loads the tendon through a full range of motion at a high percentage of maximum, deliberately slowed down: typically 3–4 sets of 6–15 repetitions, performed with a 3-second concentric and a 3-second eccentric phase, three times a week on non-consecutive days, with the working load progressed over 8–12 weeks from roughly a 15-repetition maximum down to a 6-repetition maximum as the tendon adapts. Long-duration isometrics — holding a position under high tension for 30–45 seconds, for several repetitions — achieve something similar by maximising time spent at high strain within a single contraction rather than across many repetitions. Both outperform fast, bouncy, low-load loading for tendon adaptation specifically, because a tendon under light or brief load barely enters the steep part of the stress–strain curve where the useful adaptation signal lives.

Bone — The Mechanostat

Bone is living tissue that remodels according to the load it experiences, governed by a feedback system physiologist Harold Frost named the mechanostat: too little mechanical strain and bone is quietly removed (disuse, the same process that shrinks bone density during long bed rest or spaceflight); strain within a moderate working range and bone mass is simply maintained; strain above a threshold and bone actively adds material to cope with it. The practical lever for reaching that upper threshold is not steady load but impact — a brief, high, rapidly-applied force, roughly above 4g (four times the acceleration of gravity) of ground-reaction force, of the kind produced by jumping, skipping or running and essentially absent from walking, cycling or swimming.

Two further properties of the mechanostat matter for programming it. It is strongly rate-dependent: a force applied suddenly is far more osteogenic than the same peak force applied slowly, which is why impact (fast) beats resistance training alone (comparatively slow, even when heavy) for bone-specific adaptation, though heavy resistance training still contributes meaningfully through the large forces tendons transmit into bone at their attachment points. And it saturates quickly within a single bout: the first few dozen loading cycles provide most of the osteogenic signal, and piling on hundreds more repetitions in the same session adds little extra benefit for a disproportionate increase in joint stress. The practical dose that emerges from this, used in several bone-loading intervention studies, is modest: roughly 50–100 impacts a day, most days of the week, is sufficient to drive a measurable bone response over months, and little extra is gained by going far beyond that in a single session. Cartilage And Ligament — The Slowest Tissues Of All

Articular cartilage, the smooth cap covering the ends of bones inside a joint, has essentially no blood supply of its own. It is fed by diffusion from the synovial fluid that bathes the joint, and that diffusion is itself driven by joint movement — compressive loading squeezes fluid, and the nutrients dissolved in it, into and out of the cartilage the way squeezing a sponge moves water through it. Complete immobilisation is actively harmful to cartilage for this reason: without the pumping action of normal joint movement, nutrient exchange slows and the tissue begins to thin. The flip side is equally true — cartilage has a loading tolerance, and sudden, very high compressive loads, especially combined with rotation, can damage it faster than it can ever repair. Its turnover is measured in years to decades, among the slowest of any tissue in the body, so damage accumulated carelessly over a training career does not meaningfully reverse on any timescale a training programme can address; the practical target is appropriate loading and avoiding both extremes, not rapid adaptation.

Ligament sits one step above cartilage for blood supply and healing speed but still well below tendon and muscle. A ligament sprain generally heals with a higher proportion of disorganised scar tissue than a comparable muscle strain, and the resulting tissue is often mechanically somewhat inferior to the original even once "healed" and pain-free — part of the reason a joint with a history of significant ligament injury (an ankle sprain, an ACL tear) typically needs ongoing proprioceptive and strength work indefinitely, not a finite rehabilitation course, as covered further in chapter 39 and chapter 42.

Collagen Plus Vitamin C — A Plausible Mechanism, Not Yet A Proven Benefit

Vitamin C is a required chemical partner for an enzyme (prolyl hydroxylase) that stabilises the triple-helix structure of newly made collagen; without adequate vitamin C, collagen synthesis is measurably impaired, which is why severe deficiency (scurvy) causes connective tissue to fail. That much is uncontroversial biochemistry. The supplement-industry claim built on top of it — that taking extra collagen protein alongside vitamin C, timed before loading, measurably speeds up tendon repair or adaptation beyond what loading and ordinary dietary protein already provide — rests on considerably thinner evidence.

A frequently cited 2017 trial gave participants 15 g of vitamin-C-enriched gelatin an hour before a bout of intermittent exercise and found roughly double the blood markers of collagen synthesis compared with a placebo drink, with the effect specific to the window around exercise rather than present at rest. That is a genuine, mechanistically coherent signal: loading creates the demand, and the timed supplement appears to support the biochemical machinery responding to it. C What the trial did not show, because no trial yet has done so convincingly, is that this translates into a faster-adapting tendon, a meaningfully reduced injury rate, or quicker return from tendinopathy over weeks to months of real training. The mechanism is plausible; the functional benefit remains unproven.

A reasonable, honest position: collagen-plus-vitamin-C supplementation around loading sessions is low-cost, essentially risk-free for most people, and biochemically sensible enough to be worth trying for someone already doing everything else in this chapter correctly. It is not a substitute for the loading itself, and nobody should expect it to move the 8–14 week timeline for tendon adaptation described earlier by more than a marginal amount, if at all.

Five things to carry forward

  1. Chronic stretching mostly raises stretch tolerance — a nervous-system change in sensation — rather than genuinely lengthening muscle, at least over the weeks-to-months timescale most programmes run on.
  2. Static stretches under about 30 seconds cost little before performance; holds of 60 seconds or more produce a measurable strength and rate-of-force-development deficit and are better placed after training, not before it.
  3. Tendon adapts by getting thicker and stiffer, driven by collagen synthesis that peaks around 24 hours after loading and stays elevated to about 72 hours — which is why tendon-specific sessions are spaced 48–72 hours apart, not 24–48 like a muscle split.
  4. Heavy slow resistance and long-duration isometrics outperform light, fast loading for tendon adaptation because time spent under high strain, not total volume, is the active ingredient.
  5. Bone responds to impact above roughly 4g of ground-reaction force, with most of the benefit from around 50–100 daily impacts; cartilage and ligament have the slowest turnover of any tissue and reward patient, appropriate loading over any attempt to rush their adaptation.
