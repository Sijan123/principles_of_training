Two runners cross the line of a marathon seconds apart. One has a laboratory-measured VO2max of 70 mL/kg/min; the other's is 58. The second runner won. Nothing about that result is a paradox once you accept what this chapter is really about: your aerobic ceiling is only one of three numbers that decide an endurance performance, and it is routinely the least decisive of the three. The other two — how much of that ceiling you can hold without blowing up, and how cheaply you can move at any given speed — are trainable in ways a stopwatch can track from week one. This chapter gives you the thresholds that define them, the weekly mix the best endurance athletes in the world actually use, and a complete worked method for turning one honest race time into a week of paces.

What Endurance Actually Is

VO2max — the greatest rate at which your body can take in, transport and use oxygen, measured in millilitres of oxygen per kilogram of body mass per minute — sets the ceiling on aerobic performance. It is built from the heart's stroke volume and the muscle's ability to extract oxygen from blood, both covered in full in chapter 9, and this chapter will not re-derive it. What matters here is narrower and more useful: VO2max tells you the size of the engine, and almost nothing about who wins a race between two people who both have one.

The two numbers that actually separate them are fractional utilisation and economy. Fractional utilisation is the percentage of VO2max you can sustain for a given duration without the effort becoming unsustainable — a well-trained marathoner might hold 80–85% of VO2max for over two hours, where an equally gifted but less-trained runner with the identical ceiling might only hold 70% before fatigue forces them to slow. B Economy is the oxygen cost of moving at a given pace — how many millilitres of oxygen per kilogram it takes you to run a kilometre at 4:00/km, or to cycle at 250 watts. A runner with poor economy spends more of their ceiling just to hold the pace, leaving less in reserve; a runner with excellent economy arrives at the same speed having spent less of theirs.

Think of three cars with identical fuel tanks lined up for a long-distance rally. VO2max is the size of the tank. Fractional utilisation is how much of a full tank you can burn at speed before the engine overheats and you have to back off — one car can run at 85% throttle for hours, another has to drop to 70% after twenty minutes. Economy is miles per gallon at a given speed — one car sips fuel cruising at 60 mph, another gulps it.

A car with the biggest tank and the worst mileage loses to a car with a smaller tank, a higher sustainable throttle setting, and better mileage. That is the entire story of why VO2max predicts almost nothing about who wins a race between two trained athletes, and why the rest of this chapter spends so little more time on it.

Economy itself is shaped by more than physiology: tendon stiffness, tissue elasticity, biomechanics, even footwear and (for cyclists) equipment and position all move it independently of fitness, which is why economy does not improve in a straight line with training volume the way fractional utilisation does. Years of consistent aerobic training improve it gradually, through small gains in tendon stiffness, coordination, and the familiar economy a nervous system learns for a well-practised movement pattern — see the discussion of neural learning in chapter 6.

LT1 And LT2 — The Two Thresholds That Actually Matter

Blood lactate — a byproduct of glucose metabolism that rises whenever its production outpaces the body's ability to clear it, covered mechanistically in chapter 7 — behaves predictably as intensity rises, and that predictability gives endurance training its two most useful landmarks.

LT1 (the first lactate threshold, also called the aerobic threshold) is the first intensity at which blood lactate rises detectably above its resting baseline, typically to around 2 mmol/L. Below LT1, fat oxidation dominates, lactate stays flat, and the effort can in principle continue for many hours. LT2 (the second lactate threshold, also called the anaerobic threshold or maximal lactate steady state, MLSS) is the highest intensity at which lactate production and clearance remain in balance — the fastest pace you could hold while lactate stays level rather than climbing — and it sits at roughly 4 mmol/L for most people, though this exact number varies between individuals more than the tidy figure suggests. Above LT2, lactate rises continuously and the clock on the effort starts running from the first stride.

These two points cut the whole intensity spectrum into three intensity domains, and the domain you are training in matters more than the number on a watch. Moderate (below LT1) is the domain of all-day efforts; metabolism reaches a true steady state almost immediately. Heavy (between LT1 and LT2) still reaches a steady state, but a slowly rising one — you can hold it for a long time, but not indefinitely, and fatigue accumulates gradually throughout. Severe (above LT2) never reaches a steady state at all: oxygen uptake, lactate and perceived effort all climb continuously until the effort ends, whether that is in ninety seconds or fifteen minutes.

Here is the practical problem with the way most watches and apps hand out training zones: they anchor to a percentage of maximal heart rate, and that percentage is not where LT1 or LT2 actually sit for a given individual. In published samples of trained endurance athletes, LT1 has been measured anywhere from roughly 65% to 85% of HRmax, and LT2 anywhere from about 80% to 92%, depending on training history, fibre-type profile and the sport itself. B A fixed rule such as “Zone 2 is 60–70% of HRmax” will sit a highly aerobic athlete's LT1 comfortably above their prescribed zone — wasting trainable intensity — while sitting a less aerobically developed person's LT1 below it, so that their “easy” run is quietly accumulating lactate the whole time. The fix is not a better formula; it is anchoring to a measured or field-tested threshold rather than an age-predicted percentage, which is what the testing protocols in chapter 35 exist to do.

How Much Time In Each Domain — The Evidence

If thresholds define the domains, training intensity distribution (TID) — how your weekly volume is split across them — is the question that actually decides a programme's outcome. Three patterns recur in the literature. A polarised distribution puts the great majority of volume below LT1, almost none in the heavy domain, and a small, hard dose above LT2. A pyramidal distribution also puts the majority below LT1, but tapers down through heavy and severe rather than skipping the middle — more threshold work, less top-end work, than polarised. A threshold-dominant distribution spends a large share of weekly volume at or near LT2 itself.

The descriptive evidence on what elite athletes actually do is unusually consistent across sports. In the landmark analysis by Seiler and Kjerland, described in full below, roughly 80% of training volume sat below LT1 across cycling, rowing, running and cross-country skiing alike, with a small amount of hard, high-intensity work and comparatively little time at LT2 itself. A broader review collating 162 published training-intensity distributions from elite endurance athletes across sports found 89 were pyramidal, 65 were polarised, and only 8 were threshold-dominant — and in 91% of all the distributions reviewed, more than 60% of total training volume was low-intensity. B The pattern, in other words, is not close: almost nobody training at an elite level is spending a large fraction of their week at or near LT2.

The intervention evidence — what happens when you randomise athletes to one pattern or another — points the same way, if slightly less overwhelmingly. Trials and meta-analyses comparing polarised or pyramidal programmes against threshold-dominant programmes of matched total volume tend to find larger VO2max gains with the low-intensity-biased models, roughly 5–11% against typically under 5% for threshold-dominant training, and faster time-trial or race finishing times, roughly 3–6% against 1–2%. B — the direction is consistent across several controlled comparisons, though sample sizes per trial are small and training histories vary, so treat the exact percentages as a useful range rather than a guarantee for any one person.

It is worth resisting the temptation to turn "polarised versus pyramidal" into the central question of endurance training, because the honest reading of the evidence is that both work, and the argument between them is louder in online debate than it is in the data. Both models agree on the one point that actually drives the outcome: the great majority of volume sits below LT1. Where they disagree — how much of the remainder goes to heavy versus severe — moves the result by single-digit percentages, not multiples. The costly mistake in real training is not choosing pyramidal when polarised would have been marginally better, or vice versa. It is spending too much time in the grey zone — comfortably hard efforts that are neither genuinely easy nor genuinely productive at the top end, the "no-man's land" that both models are explicitly built to minimise and that unstructured training drifts into by default. Section 7 returns to exactly how that trap is sprung.

Seiler S, Kjerland GØ. Quantifying training intensity distribution in elite endurance athletes: is there evidence for an ‘optimal’ distribution? Scand J Med Sci Sports 2006;16(1):49–56.
Who

Well-trained junior cross-country skiers, with their training intensity distribution analysed across three independent methods.

What

318 individual training sessions were classified into three intensity zones using heart rate, session RPE (rating of perceived effort) and, in a subset, blood lactate, to check whether the methods agreed.

Found

All three methods converged on the same picture: roughly 75–80% of sessions sat in the lowest zone, with a further substantial block of hard work above the second threshold and comparatively little time spent at the threshold itself — a polarised pattern, not an evenly graded one.

Why it matters: it was among the first papers to quantify, rather than simply assert, that elite endurance training clusters at the extremes of intensity rather than spreading evenly across them, and it established the "three-zone" method still used to classify training intensity distribution today. Limitation: a single-sport, youth-skiing sample studied descriptively, not an experiment — it shows what successful athletes already did, not proof that an alternative distribution would have made them less successful.

Tempo and threshold are easy to blur together and worth separating cleanly. Tempo work sits below LT2 and is sustainable for far longer than it is typically run; its job is raising the floor — LT1 — so that more of your easy running sits further from the point where fatigue starts to accumulate. Threshold work is deliberately run at or fractionally below LT2 itself, precisely because that is the highest intensity at which lactate production and clearance still balance; its job is raising the ceiling of the heavy domain so that marathon and half-marathon pace become, relatively, easier.

The Norwegian double-threshold method, developed through more than 5,000 individual lactate measurements by the Norwegian coach Marius Bakken and brought to wide attention through the training of the Ingebrigtsen brothers, inverts the usual logic of threshold training. Instead of one long, hard threshold session, it runs two shorter threshold sessions on the same day — often one in the morning and one in the evening — each controlled in real time with a portable lactate meter to stay in a tightly controlled band of roughly 2.0–4.0 mmol/L, deliberately kept below the point where a single session would become exhausting. The logic is arithmetic rather than mystical: two sessions at a slightly lower, lactate-capped intensity accumulate more total time near LT2 across the week than one longer session pushed harder, while leaving enough recovery capacity to run threshold work again the very next day. C — the method's results in the athletes who use it are striking, but it has not been tested against conventional single-threshold programming in a controlled trial; most of the evidence is case-based and specific to very high weekly volumes, and it is not obviously appropriate for anyone running under roughly 80–100 km a week.

Jack Daniels' VDOT — One Number, Five Paces

VDOT is a single number, developed by the exercise physiologist and coach Jack Daniels, that converts one honest race performance into an estimate of running fitness and, from there, into training paces for every session type above. It is not literally VO2max — it is a "VO2max-equivalent" derived from race performance and running economy together — which is precisely its strength: it bakes your personal economy, not just your aerobic ceiling, into every pace it hands you.

Daniels assigns five named paces, each anchored to an approximate percentage of VDOT (itself a close proxy for percentage of VO2max):

Durability — The Threshold That Matters Is The One After Three Hours

Everything above describes your thresholds measured fresh, after a warm-up, in the first twenty minutes of effort. That number is useful, but it is not the one that decides most real endurance events, which are not twenty minutes long. Durability — a term formalised by Maunder, Seiler and colleagues in 2021 — is the resistance of your physiological markers, including LT1, LT2 and economy, to deteriorating over the course of a long bout of exercise. B Two athletes with an identical fresh LT2 can produce very different fourth hours of a marathon or a long ride: one holds the same threshold intensity relatively steady, the other watches it quietly fall as glycogen depletes, core temperature rises and fatigue accumulates in type I fibres.

The simplest field proxy for durability is aerobic decoupling: compare the ratio of heart rate to pace (or to power, on a bike) in the first half of a long steady effort against the second half. A rise of more than roughly 5% — the same output now costing noticeably more heart rate, or the same heart rate now buying noticeably less output — signals that the aerobic system is struggling to keep up with the duration, independent of how fit the athlete looked in the first twenty minutes. C It is a useful, low-cost signal rather than a validated clinical measurement; cardiovascular drift from heat and dehydration can inflate it for reasons that have nothing to do with aerobic fitness, so it is best tracked over repeated sessions in similar conditions rather than read from any single run.

Durability is trained the same way the rest of the aerobic system is trained — principally by accumulating time on feet or on the bike, since the adaptations that resist late-session deterioration (glycogen storage capacity, fat oxidation rate, capillarisation, fatigue resistance in the muscle itself) are largely the same adaptations the long easy run already builds in section 1. What changes the training target, rather than the training method, is duration: a session that never runs past 75 minutes cannot meaningfully test or improve what happens in hour three, however hard it is run. The practical implication is specific to event length — marathoners and ultra-distance athletes have a direct reason to extend their longest single session toward the territory where their own durability actually starts to fray, within the bounds of sensible weekly load management covered in chapter 36.

Building A Week

Putting sections 3 through 6 together into an actual training week comes down to a short list of sequencing rules rather than a rigid template.

  • Fix the hard days first, then fill around them. One threshold or VO2max-type session, occasionally two for well-developed athletes, anchors the week; everything else is easy by comparison, not by accident.
  • Separate the two hard sessions by at least 48 hours where possible, so neither is run on residual fatigue from the other.
  • Put the long run or ride where the following day can be the easiest or lightest of the week — durability work borrows recovery from the day after it, not just the day it happens.
  • Do not let an "easy" day creep upward in pace because the legs feel good. That single habit, repeated for months, is how a polarised plan on paper becomes a threshold-dominant plan in practice.
  • Strides and hill sprints are nearly free: two to three short sessions a week add negligible fatigue and measurably support economy, so there is little reason to omit them.

The single most common way real training drifts away from both the polarised and the pyramidal pattern is the one named in section 3: the grey zone, sometimes called the "moderate-intensity rut." It is the pace that feels appropriately virtuous — not lazy, not all-out — and so gets chosen by default on days with no plan, typically landing somewhere in the heavy domain, just below where a genuine threshold session would sit. A week built entirely from grey-zone runs produces a large total of heavy-domain fatigue for a comparatively small threshold-raising stimulus, crowding out both the true-easy volume that should anchor the week and the true-hard work that should sit on top of it. B The fix is not more discipline in the abstract; it is a plan specific enough that "easy" and "hard" are both pre-decided before the session starts.

A general weekly template built from sections 3–7, suitable as a starting structure for a runner training 4–6 days a week.

  • Do One long E run, one threshold or tempo session, one VO2max/interval session, 2–3 further easy runs, strides woven into two of the easy days.
  • Load E pace from the VDOT table (or, lacking a race time, a pace that allows full sentences); T at or just below LT2; I at current 3–5 min best-effort pace.
  • Dose 70–80% of weekly distance or time at E; the remainder split roughly 2:1 between threshold-type and interval-type work, skewed toward threshold for longer events.
  • When Hard sessions 48+ h apart; long run placed so the next day can be the lightest of the week.
  • On the bike A matched turbo week: one 60–90 min endurance ride at a conversational power/HR, one 3×12–15 min at 88–93% FTP (functional threshold power, roughly your LT2-equivalent for cycling) with 4 min easy, one 5–6×3 min at 106–110% FTP with equal recovery — the full library is in chapter 44.
  • Signal You're doing it right when your easy pace feels genuinely easy by feel and by heart rate, and your hard sessions feel appropriately hard — not when every run feels roughly the same.

Five things to carry forward

  1. VO2max sets a ceiling, but races are decided mostly by fractional utilisation (how much of the ceiling you can hold) and economy (what holding it costs) — both far more trainable week to week than the ceiling itself.
  2. LT1 (≈2 mmol/L) and LT2 (≈4 mmol/L) define three intensity domains — moderate, heavy, severe — and fixed percentage-of-HRmax zones routinely misplace both thresholds by a wide margin for any given individual.
  3. Elite endurance athletes overwhelmingly run roughly 80% or more of their volume below LT1, whether their overall pattern is labelled polarised or pyramidal; both beat threshold-dominant training on VO2max and performance, and the real failure mode is neither label but the grey zone between them.
  4. Jack Daniels' VDOT converts one honest race time into five training paces (E, M, T, I, R), anchored at roughly 70/84/88/98/105% of VDOT, with 70–80% of weekly mileage prescribed at E.
  5. Durability — how little your thresholds and economy degrade after hours of work, not how good they look fresh — is trained mainly by the same long, easy volume that builds the aerobic base in the first place, extended toward real event duration.
