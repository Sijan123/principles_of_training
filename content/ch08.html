A single muscle fibre can hold thousands of mitochondria, and they are not fixed furniture. Train it one way and the fibre grows more of them; train it another way and the ones it has grow better at their job; stop training and both reverse within weeks. Nothing else in the body is this visibly, measurably plastic in response to exercise — which is why the mitochondrion, not the heart or the lung, is where endurance training actually leaves its clearest fingerprint.

What A Mitochondrion Actually Does

Think of a mitochondrion as a small power station that takes delivered fuel — mostly fat, some carbohydrate — and burns it completely, the way a furnace burns gas rather than just scorching the surface of a log. The furnace itself is a folded inner membrane called the electron transport chain: a series of protein complexes that pass electrons stripped from fuel molecules from one to the next, a bit like a bucket brigade passing water down a line, each handoff releasing a little energy that gets used to pump hydrogen ions across the membrane. That pumping builds up a pressure of hydrogen ions on one side, and letting those ions flow back through one last protein (ATP synthase) spins a molecular turbine that bolts a phosphate onto ADP, making ATP.

The chain only keeps moving if something is waiting at the very end to accept the spent electrons. That something is oxygen — the final electron acceptor, converted to water as a by-product. No oxygen, no acceptor, no flow, no ATP from this route: which is exactly why the whole oxidative system (chapter 7) grinds to a halt without an adequate oxygen supply, and why the entire cardiovascular and respiratory apparatus in the next two chapters exists, in a sense, to serve this one membrane.

A mitochondrion is a hydroelectric dam, not a bonfire.

A bonfire releases all its energy at once, mostly as heat you can't capture. A dam instead uses a controlled, staged drop in water level — through several turbines in sequence — to generate usable electricity efficiently, with much less wasted as heat. The electron transport chain is the staged drop; the hydrogen-ion gradient is the water held back by the dam wall; ATP synthase is the final turbine. Oxygen is the river downstream that has to keep flowing for the water to keep moving through the system at all.

Mitochondrial Content And Quality

Two separate things change with training, and it is worth keeping them apart. Content is how much mitochondrial machinery a fibre holds — measured as the fraction of the fibre's volume taken up by mitochondria, or as the activity of marker enzymes like citrate synthase. In untrained muscle, mitochondria occupy roughly 3–5% of fibre volume; sustained endurance training can roughly double that figure over months B. Quality is a separate question — how efficiently the existing mitochondria couple fuel oxidation to ATP production, and how well they handle stress — and the two do not always move together.

Biogenesis: how new mitochondria get built

Making more mitochondria is called mitochondrial biogenesis, and it is coordinated by a single master switch: a protein called PGC-1α (peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma coactivator 1-alpha). PGC-1α does not build mitochondria itself; it is a coactivator that switches on the genes for mitochondrial proteins, including two transcription factors, NRF-1/2 (nuclear respiratory factors) and TFAM (mitochondrial transcription factor A), which together coordinate reading both the cell's own DNA and the small separate genome carried inside the mitochondria themselves. A fibre's PGC-1α levels can rise substantially within hours of a single hard session; repeated sessions keep nudging baseline mitochondrial content upward over weeks.

Several distinct signals converge on PGC-1α, which is why very different training styles can all stimulate biogenesis:

  1. Calcium (Ca2+)

    Every contraction floods the fibre with calcium. Repeated, moderate-duration contractions (classic steady endurance work) keep calcium elevated long enough to activate calcium-sensitive kinases that switch on PGC-1α.

  2. AMPK

    A falling ATP:AMP ratio — the energy-stress signal introduced in chapter 12 — activates AMPK, which both triggers and directly phosphorylates PGC-1α. High-intensity intervals, which deplete energy stores fast in a short time, lean heavily on this route.

  3. p38 MAPK

    A stress-activated kinase that rises with both volume and intensity and independently boosts PGC-1α transcription.

  4. ROS and nitric oxide (NO)

    Small amounts of reactive oxygen species and NO generated during exercise act as signals, not just as damage — part of why swamping them with high-dose antioxidant supplements can blunt the adaptation (see below).

There is no single "endurance pathway." Volume-dominant training (long, steady, aerobic) and intensity-dominant training (short, hard, intervals) both raise mitochondrial content — they just do it by leaning on different signals converging on the same PGC-1α switch. This is why both a marathoner's programme and a well-built HIIT block can produce comparable mitochondrial gains, and why "more is always better" and "harder is always better" are both oversimplifications of the same underlying biology.

Fuel Selection: The Crossover Concept

Mitochondria can burn fat or carbohydrate, but not in fixed proportion — the mix shifts predictably with intensity. At rest and during easy exercise, fat dominates: it is energy-dense and essentially unlimited in supply (even a lean athlete carries tens of thousands of kilocalories of it), but the fat-oxidation machinery delivers ATP relatively slowly per unit of oxygen consumed. As intensity rises, the body needs ATP faster than fat oxidation alone can supply it, and carbohydrate — which yields ATP more quickly per litre of oxygen, at the cost of a far smaller total store — takes up a growing share of the load. Above roughly 60% of VO2max, carbohydrate becomes the dominant fuel for most people, though this threshold shifts substantially with training status and fuelling practice B.

The point of maximum fat-burning rate is called FATmax, and it typically sits in the easy-to-moderate intensity zone. In absolute terms, FATmax in an untrained-to-moderately-trained adult is commonly 0.3–0.6 g of fat per minute; well-trained, fat-adapted endurance athletes have been measured oxidising more than 1.5 g per minute at their own FATmax intensity — roughly a threefold range driven by training history, muscle fibre type and recent diet B.

Why Glycogen Sparing Is The Real Prize

Glycogen — carbohydrate stored in muscle and liver as a branched chain, covered fully in chapter 28 — is a strictly limited store: roughly 400–500 g across a typical adult's muscle and liver combined, enough for perhaps 1,600–2,000 kcal. Fat stores, by contrast, are measured in tens of thousands of kilocalories even in lean athletes. In any event lasting much beyond 90 minutes, the athlete who can supply a larger share of their energy from fat at a given speed is the athlete who still has glycogen left in the tank when it matters — at the finish, on the final climb, in the closing kilometres of a marathon. This is the entire physiological case for aerobic base training in endurance sport: it is not really about raising VO2max, it is about teaching the mitochondria to lean harder on an almost limitless fuel so the limited one lasts longer. "Hitting the wall" is, in large part, glycogen depletion arriving before the finish line does.

Capillarisation: Getting Fuel And Oxygen To The Door

More mitochondria are only useful if they are supplied. Endurance training drives angiogenesis — growth of new capillaries, the smallest blood vessels, which run directly alongside muscle fibres and are where oxygen and fuel actually cross from blood into tissue. Sustained training can increase capillary density around working muscle by roughly 60% B, shortening the diffusion distance between blood and mitochondrion and giving each fibre more simultaneous delivery points. This process is itself substantially PGC-1α-dependent: the same signal that tells the fibre to build more power stations also tells the surrounding tissue to build more delivery roads to feed them, which is a tidy piece of biological coordination and one reason capillary and mitochondrial adaptations tend to track together over a training block, even though they are structurally separate changes.

Worked Example — Two Intensities, Two Fuel Bills

Priya, 29, competitive cyclist, 62 kg, glycogen store approximately 500 g (muscle + liver), riding a 3-hour event.
  1. At an easy endurance pace near her FATmax (roughly 55% VO2max), Priya oxidises fat at close to her peak rate and carbohydrate at a modest rate.Fat ~1.0 g/min, carbohydrate ~1.5 g/min at this intensity
  2. Over 60 minutes at this pace, her carbohydrate spend is comparatively small.1.5 g/min × 60 min = 90 g carbohydrate per hour
  3. At her 80% VO2max (threshold-adjacent) pace, fat oxidation falls well below its peak and carbohydrate becomes dominant.Fat ~0.3 g/min, carbohydrate ~3.5 g/min at this intensity
  4. Over 60 minutes at this harder pace, her carbohydrate spend roughly doubles.3.5 g/min × 60 min = 210 g carbohydrate per hour
  5. With a 500 g store and no fuelling during the ride, divide the store by the hourly spend to estimate how long each pace can be sustained before glycogen runs critically low.Easy pace: 500 g ÷ 90 g/h ≈ 5.5 h of store. Hard pace: 500 g ÷ 210 g/h ≈ 2.4 h of store
So: at the easier intensity Priya's glycogen would comfortably outlast a 3-hour event; at the harder intensity, run without any carbohydrate intake during the ride, her own stores would start running critically low with roughly 30–40 minutes still to race — which is exactly why both fuel-economy training (this chapter) and in-race carbohydrate intake (chapter 28) matter, and why they are not substitutes for each other.

How To Train Each — And How To Blunt It By Accident

Both mitochondrial content and capillarisation respond to volume and to intensity, through the different signalling routes described above, which is good practical news: there is more than one road to the same adaptation, and a programme does not have to choose only one.

A 2024 systematic review and meta-regression pooling several hundred training studies found that mitochondrial content rose by a broadly similar amount — roughly 20–30% — across endurance, high-intensity interval and sprint-interval training protocols, while capillary density gains were somewhat larger under high-volume endurance training than under high-intensity protocols B. The practical reading: total training load (intensity multiplied by volume) predicts the mitochondrial response better than either variable alone, and an athlete short on time can substitute intensity for volume with a reasonable expectation of a similar mitochondrial outcome, even though the capillary outcome may lag slightly behind.

Because ROS and NO are part of the signal that triggers PGC-1α and biogenesis, chronically swamping them with high-dose antioxidant supplements (particularly combined vitamin C and vitamin E at supra-dietary doses) has been shown in several controlled trials to blunt the mitochondrial and performance adaptations to endurance training, compared with training alone B. This is a case where a nutrient that seems obviously protective turns out to interfere with the very disturbance the training is meant to create — see the broader discussion of supplement evidence in chapter 33. Ordinary dietary antioxidant intake from food has not shown the same effect; the concern is specifically about isolated, high-dose supplementation taken close to training sessions.

A weekly structure that drives mitochondrial content and capillarisation through both available routes.

  • Volume base 3–5 sessions/week of continuous aerobic work at an easy, conversational effort, 30–90 min each, building toward 150+ min/week minimum.
  • Intensity top-up One session/week of 4–6 x 4 min at an effort you could not hold for much longer than 20 minutes, 2–3 min easy recovery between.
  • Fasted or low-glycogen easy rides Occasionally (not every session) start an easy aerobic session with reduced glycogen to bias fuel selection toward fat and reinforce FATmax — see chapter 30 for the trade-offs.
  • Avoid Routine high-dose antioxidant supplements taken immediately around training sessions, if the goal is to maximise the adaptation rather than just manage soreness.
  • On the bike 5 x 6 min at a steady, hard-but-controlled effort (roughly 85–90% of threshold power), 3 min easy spin between, once weekly, layered onto 3–4 easy endurance rides.
  • Signal You're doing it right when your heart rate at a fixed easy pace drops over 6–8 weeks and you can hold that pace while visibly sweating less hard — both track improving fat oxidation at the same absolute workload.

Five things to carry forward

  1. A mitochondrion is a staged energy converter, not a bonfire: electrons move down the electron transport chain to oxygen, and that controlled handoff is what makes ATP production efficient.
  2. Mitochondrial content (roughly 3–5% of fibre volume untrained, up to about double with training) and mitochondrial quality are separate variables, both coordinated by the master switch PGC-1α, which responds to calcium, AMPK, p38 MAPK, ROS and NO.
  3. Fuel selection shifts from fat-dominant at low intensity to carbohydrate-dominant above roughly 60% VO2max, and FATmax — the peak fat-burning rate — ranges from about 0.3 to over 1.5 g/min depending on training history.
  4. The real prize of a well-developed oxidative system is glycogen sparing: burning more fat at a given pace leaves the strictly limited carbohydrate store for later in a long event.
  5. Both training volume and training intensity drive mitochondrial and capillary adaptation through different signalling routes, but high-dose antioxidant supplementation around training sessions can blunt the very signal the session is meant to produce.
