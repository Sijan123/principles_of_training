Three muscles do almost all of the pushing a human body ever does — the chest, the front of the shoulder and the back of the upper arm — and between them they cover perhaps fifteen centimetres of real estate. The rest of the push, the part lifters rarely think about, is a thin sheet of muscle wrapped around the ribs and four small rotator-cuff muscles no bigger than a thumb, whose entire job is to keep the shoulder joint from flying apart while the big three do the obvious work. This chapter is built the way chapter 22 was: for each muscle, where it attaches, what it does, its fibre mix, where it is at long length — the joint position proven to grow muscle fastest, explained in full there — its best exercises, and how it typically fails.

Pectoralis Major — Two Heads, Two Jobs

The chest muscle is really two muscles sharing one tendon. The clavicular head runs from the collarbone down to the upper arm bone; the much larger sternal head runs from the breastbone and the cartilage of the upper ribs to the same tendon lower down. Both produce horizontal adduction — bringing the arm across the front of the body, the motion of a bench press or a cable fly — but only the clavicular head meaningfully flexes the shoulder, lifting the arm up and forward. That one anatomical difference is the entire reason incline and flat pressing feel and build differently.

Angle the bench up and the clavicular head is doing more of the work relative to the sternal head, because the bar path now runs closer to the line the clavicular fibres pull along. Lie flat, or decline the bench, and the sternal head dominates instead. High-density surface electromyography — EMG, a skin sensor that reads the electrical signal of a working muscle — confirms this directly: in one controlled comparison, the amplitude concentrated at the sternal head during flat bench press and shifted toward the clavicular region as the bench was inclined to 45° B. The same study tracked muscle thickness over a training block and found the regional pattern held for growth, not just for the moment-to-moment electrical signal: flat pressing built the sternal head more, inclined pressing built the clavicular head more. This is one of relatively few cases in exercise science where regional hypertrophy — growing one part of a muscle preferentially — is both measurable and programmable, rather than a gym myth.

Think of the pec as a fan of ropes anchored along a curved rail from the collarbone down to the breastbone, all converging on one handle at the upper arm. Pull the top ropes and the handle swings up and across; pull the bottom ropes and it swings down and across. A flat bench pulls mostly on the bottom ropes. Tilt the bench up and you start loading the top ropes instead — same muscle, same handle, a genuinely different set of ropes doing the work.

Both heads reach long length with the arm pulled behind the plane of the torso — the bottom of a dip, a deep bench descent, or especially a fly, where the elbow stays nearly straight and the whole range is spent under stretch rather than losing tension at the top the way a press does. A fly performed to a genuine stretch, not stopped early to protect the shoulder, is therefore not a secondary "pump" exercise; it is one of the few places pectoral training reaches a length a press structurally cannot.

The pec's most serious acute injury — a tear, usually at the junction between muscle and tendon — happens almost exclusively at this same long-length, high-tension position: the bottom of a heavy bench press or a wide-range dip, arm flared out and behind the torso, often on the last forced rep of a set taken past control. The injury is rare at moderate loads performed with control; it clusters around maximal or supra-maximal loads, poor technique, and a bounced rather than controlled bottom position. Training the stretch position deliberately, with a weight you control rather than one that controls you, is protective, not provocative.

Anterior & Lateral Deltoid — One Overtrained, One Ignored

The deltoid is one muscle with three functional regions, and push-focused programmes train them unevenly without meaning to. The anterior deltoid flexes the shoulder — raises the arm forward — and it is a synergist in every single pressing exercise that exists: bench press, overhead press, incline press, dip, push-up. It gets so much incidental work that direct anterior-raise training is rarely necessary and sometimes counter-productive, adding fatigue to a muscle that is already near its weekly tolerance from pressing alone.

The lateral deltoid does something different and is trained by almost nothing in a typical push-day. Its job is abduction — lifting the arm straight out to the side — and no pressing movement loads that action directly; a bench press and an overhead press both keep the arm close to the sagittal plane, the front-to-back plane of movement, rather than out to the side. The lateral deltoid is what gives the shoulder visual width, and it is trained almost exclusively by dedicated abduction work: a lateral raise, a cable raise, or a machine lateral raise. It reaches long length when the arm starts across the front of the body rather than hanging straight down, which is why a cable lateral raise from a low pulley, crossing the body before lifting, loads more of its length than a dumbbell raise started from the side.

Daniyar, 29, benches and overhead presses twice a week, has trained for two years, and feels his shoulders are "flat from the side" despite visibly bigger pecs and front delts.

Filling the lateral deltoid's gap without adding to an already well-loaded anterior deltoid.

  • Do Cable or dumbbell lateral raise, started with the arm slightly across the body for a longer stretch.
  • Load Light enough that the shoulder, not momentum, lifts the arm — strict form, no shrugging the trap to start the movement.
  • Dose 3 sets of 12–15 reps, 2–3 times weekly.
  • When After, not before, any heavy pressing — it is accessory work and should not compromise bar speed on the main lift.
  • Progress Add reps before load; the lateral deltoid responds to time under tension more forgivingly than to maximal weight.
  • On the bike No indoor-cycling session loads shoulder abduction; this is a gap only direct upper-body work closes.
  • Signal You're doing it right when the burn is felt at the point of the shoulder, not creeping up into the traps.

Triceps Brachii — Why the Long Head Needs the Arm Overhead

The triceps is three muscles named for where they start. The lateral and medial heads both begin on the upper arm bone itself and only cross the elbow, so they reach long length whenever the elbow is bent, regardless of what the shoulder is doing. The long head is different: it starts on the shoulder blade, crosses the shoulder joint as well as the elbow, and therefore only reaches true long length when both joints cooperate — elbow bent and shoulder flexed, arm overhead. A triceps pressdown, performed with the upper arm pinned to the torso, bends the elbow fully but leaves the shoulder in a neutral position; the long head never gets lengthened. An overhead extension bends the elbow with the arm raised overhead, stretching the long head across both joints at once.

This is not a theoretical distinction. A controlled within-subject trial trained one arm with overhead extensions and the other with a neutral, arm-down variant — same load, same volume, same person, twelve weeks — and measured each head's muscle volume directly. The long head grew roughly 28–29% overhead versus roughly 20% in the neutral position; the lateral and medial heads also grew more overhead, by a smaller margin B. Because the triceps makes up roughly two-thirds of upper-arm mass, and the long head is its largest of the three, this single joint-position choice is one of the highest-leverage decisions in an arm-training programme.

Maeo S, et al. Triceps brachii hypertrophy is substantially greater after elbow extension training performed in the overhead versus neutral arm position. Eur J Sport Sci 2023;23(5):781–790.
Who

21 untrained-to-recreationally-active adults.

What

Within-subject design: one arm trained elbow extensions overhead, the other in a neutral, arm-down position, 2×/week for 12 weeks, matched load and volume. Each triceps head's volume measured separately by MRI.

Found

All three heads grew more in the overhead condition; the long head's advantage was the largest, consistent with it being the only head that reaches a meaningfully longer length in that position.

Why it matters: a within-subject design controls for genetics, training history and diet almost perfectly, which makes this a cleaner test of joint position than most hypertrophy trials manage. Limitation: one trial, moderate sample, 12 weeks — it shows a direction and a plausible mechanism, not a guarantee of the exact percentages in every lifter.

None of this makes pressdowns useless. They allow heavier loading at the elbow with less shoulder strain and still train the lateral and medial heads well. The practical rule is to use both: a pressdown or close-grip press for heavy, short-length elbow work, and an overhead extension — cable, dumbbell or barbell, arm raised throughout — for the long head specifically.

Serratus Anterior & Scapular Mechanics

The shoulder blade is not bolted to the skeleton; it floats on muscle, anchored to the ribcage only by soft tissue. That gives the arm an enormous range of motion, but it means every pressing and overhead movement needs the shoulder blade to move in a precise, coordinated way underneath the arm — and the muscle most responsible for that coordination is serratus anterior, a broad sheet running from the upper eight ribs, around the side of the ribcage, to the inner edge of the shoulder blade. Its job is unglamorous and essential: it pulls the shoulder blade forward and rotates it upward as the arm lifts overhead.

That upward rotation matters because of a fixed relationship called scapulohumeral rhythm: for roughly every three degrees the arm lifts, about two degrees happen at the shoulder joint itself and one happens at the shoulder blade rotating underneath it. If serratus anterior is weak, the shoulder blade under-rotates, the bony arch above the shoulder joint fails to clear out of the way, and the tendons passing underneath it — chiefly the rotator cuff's supraspinatus — get mechanically pinched between the arm bone and that arch on every overhead repetition B. This is the actual mechanical story behind "poor scapular stability causes impingement," and it is also why coaches talk about "setting the shoulder blade" before a heavy press: it is not a cue about posture for its own sake, it is ensuring the floating bone the arm bone operates against is in the position where the rotator cuff has room to work.

"Scapular stability" is a loaded phrase that sounds like it means rigid or fixed. It means the opposite: a shoulder blade that moves through its full, coordinated range on demand, and that serratus anterior and the trapezius can control at any point in that range — not one glued in a single "correct" position. A shoulder blade that cannot upwardly rotate is just as much of a problem as one that cannot retract.

Serratus anterior is trained by any exercise that asks the shoulder blade to protract — reach forward around the ribcage — against resistance: a push-up performed to full lockout with a deliberate extra reach at the top (sometimes called a push-up plus), a landmine press, a straight-arm pullover, or simply a loaded overhead press performed with conscious upward rotation rather than a shrugged, elevated shoulder blade. It is a muscle almost nobody trains directly and almost everybody uses indirectly, which makes it a common quiet weak link behind otherwise well-trained shoulders.

The Rotator Cuff — Four Stabilisers, Not a Fifth Prime Mover

The rotator cuff is four separate muscles — supraspinatus, infraspinatus, teres minor and subscapularis — that wrap the ball of the upper arm bone from above, behind, behind-and-below and in front respectively, all running from the shoulder blade to the top of the arm bone. None of them is built to produce the arm's big, powerful movements; the deltoid and pec are vastly larger and do that work. The cuff's job is to hold the ball centred in a socket that is shallow by design — shallower than the hip, which is why the shoulder trades stability for the largest range of motion of any joint in the body — and to fine-tune rotation throughout every arm movement so the ball does not ride up, forward or back out of position under load.

That stabilising role, not a prime-mover role, is the reason the cuff is almost always trained at light loads with high control rather than heavy loads with maximal intent — and also the reason it is so often neglected, because light, unglamorous work is easy to skip. Supraspinatus assists the deltoid in starting abduction from the arm hanging at the side; infraspinatus and teres minor externally rotate the arm (turn it outward, as in cocking the arm back to throw); subscapularis internally rotates it. Direct training is simple: external rotation against a band or light cable for the posterior pair, and internal rotation work for subscapularis, both performed for higher reps at loads that feel almost too light, because the point is control through range, not force.

Shoulder Impingement & Why Heavy Pressing Is Not the Enemy

Shoulder impingement is the traditional name for pain attributed to the rotator cuff's tendons being pinched under the bony arch above the shoulder joint during overhead movement — the mechanical story described above. It is a genuinely common source of shoulder pain, but the name has aged badly, because it implies a simple structural cause (not enough space) with a simple structural fix (make more space, or avoid the movement that causes the pinching). Reviews of scapular and cuff muscle activity in people with and without shoulder pain complicate that picture considerably: the same structural narrowing is found on imaging in plenty of pain-free shoulders, and what reliably differs in people with pain is the pattern of muscle activity — reduced or mistimed activation of the lower trapezius and serratus anterior, with the upper trapezius working harder to compensate B. That is a motor-control and capacity story, not purely a "not enough room" story.

“Heavy overhead pressing damages healthy shoulders — it should be avoided or replaced with machines”

Common advice given to lifters with no shoulder history at all.

Healthy shoulders tolerate heavy pressing well

Shoulder pain in lifters correlates far more strongly with sudden volume spikes, poor scapular control, and training through an existing niggle than with the sheer fact of pressing heavy. A healthy shoulder, loaded progressively with normal scapular mechanics intact, is not living on borrowed time under a barbell overhead press. The muscles and movement described in this chapter — serratus anterior rotating the shoulder blade, the cuff centring the ball — are precisely what well-programmed heavy pressing also reinforces over time, provided it is built up rather than jumped into.

The practical takeaway is not that overhead pressing is risk-free regardless of history — a shoulder already in pain needs assessment, not a textbook reassurance — but that the correct response to a healthy shoulder is not avoidance. It is the same response as everywhere else in this book: progressive load, enough recovery between heavy sessions, and the serratus anterior and rotator cuff work in the tables above treated as a standing part of the programme rather than rehab reserved for when something already hurts.

Five things to carry forward

  1. The pectoral clavicular and sternal heads are both trained by pressing, but incline work shifts load toward the clavicular head and flat or decline work toward the sternal head — a measurable, programmable difference, not folklore.
  2. The lateral deltoid gives shoulders their width and is trained by almost nothing in a typical pressing session; it needs dedicated abduction work, while the anterior deltoid usually needs none.
  3. The triceps long head crosses the shoulder as well as the elbow, so it only reaches long length overhead — overhead extensions grow it measurably more than pressdowns, which should sit alongside pressdowns rather than replace them.
  4. Serratus anterior rotates the shoulder blade to clear space for the rotator cuff during overhead movement; weakness here, not a lack of physical room, is the usual story behind "impingement" pain.
  5. The rotator cuff is four stabilisers, not a fifth prime mover, and healthy shoulders tolerate heavy, progressively built pressing well — the muscles that go wrong in impingement are the same ones sensible pressing and direct cuff work both reinforce.
