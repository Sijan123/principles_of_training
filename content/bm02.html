Every technical term used in this book, defined in plain words. Where a typical value helps, it is given. Where a term is routinely misused — and several of the most common ones are — that is said too. Entries link to the chapter that explains the idea properly.

ABCDEFGHIJ–KLMNOPQ–RSTU–VW–Z

A

Actin
The thin filament inside a muscle fibre. Myosin heads grab it and pull, which is what shortening a muscle physically consists of. See chapter 4.
Active recovery
Very easy exercise done to aid recovery rather than to cause adaptation. Raises blood flow at almost no metabolic cost; the evidence for it speeding recovery is modest, but it reliably does no harm.
Adaptation
A lasting change in the body caused by repeated exposure to a stress. Training provides the stress; rest and food allow the adaptation. See chapter 13.
Adductors
The muscle group on the inner thigh that pulls the leg toward the midline. One of them, adductor magnus, is also a powerful hip extensor — it contributes substantially in a deep squat. See chapter 22.
Adenosine triphosphate (ATP)
The molecule that powers every muscle contraction — the body's energy currency. Stores are tiny, perhaps two to three seconds of maximal work, so the real question is always how fast you can remake it. See chapter 7.
Aerobic
Energy production that uses oxygen. Slower to start than anaerobic pathways but effectively unlimited in capacity, and the dominant supply for anything lasting more than about 90 seconds.
Afterburn
Popular name for EPOC. Real, but typically only 6–15% of the session's own energy cost — a few dozen calories, not a few hundred. See chapter 15.
Agonist
The muscle principally producing a movement. Also called the prime mover.
Altitude training
Living or training at reduced oxygen pressure to stimulate red-blood-cell production. See live high train low and chapter 37.
AMPK
An enzyme that switches on when a cell runs low on energy, acting as a fuel gauge. Endurance exercise activates it, and it drives mitochondrial building. It returns to baseline within about three hours — a timing fact that matters a great deal for concurrent training. See chapter 12.
Anabolic
Building up tissue. The opposite of catabolic (breaking down). Both happen continuously; what matters is the balance.
Anabolic window
The idea that protein must be eaten within 30 minutes of training. The genuinely sensitised period lasts roughly 24 hours, so daily total and distribution matter far more than minutes. Commonly overstated. See chapter 29.
Anaerobic
Energy production without oxygen — the phosphagen and glycolytic systems. Very fast, but limited in how long it can supply work.
Antagonist
A muscle that opposes the movement being made. Learning to relax antagonists is part of why early strength gains happen without muscle growth.
Anti-rotation / anti-extension
Core exercises that train the trunk to resist movement rather than produce it — a Pallof press, a plank, a suitcase carry. Closer to what the core does in sport than crunches are. See chapter 25.
Arteriovenous oxygen difference (a–vO₂ diff)
How much oxygen the muscles strip out of the blood as it passes through, in mL per 100 mL of blood. Roughly 14–15 untrained, 16–17 in elite athletes — it improves far less with training than stroke volume does. See chapter 9.
Autoregulation
Adjusting today's training based on how you actually are, using tools like RPE, reps in reserve or bar velocity, instead of following the written plan regardless. See chapter 21.

B

Ballistic
A movement in which you accelerate throughout and release or leave the ground, rather than decelerating at the end — a jump or a throw rather than a controlled lift.
Base training
A block emphasising high volumes of easy aerobic work to build mitochondria, capillaries and blood volume. Feels unproductive and is not. See chapter 14.
Beta-alanine
A supplement that raises muscle carnosine, which buffers acidity inside the cell. Roughly 3.2–6.4 g a day for 4–12 weeks, worth about 2–3% in efforts lasting 1–10 minutes. Useless for a 3-second sprint or a marathon. See chapter 33.
BCAAs
Branched-chain amino acids. Sold heavily, with no demonstrated benefit beyond eating enough complete protein — three amino acids cannot build a muscle that needs twenty.
Blood flow restriction (BFR)
Training with a cuff that partly restricts venous return, allowing light loads to produce hypertrophy comparable to heavy loads in some settings. Most valuable in rehabilitation, when heavy loading is not possible. See chapter 37.
Blood volume
Total circulating blood, roughly 70 mL per kg of body mass — about 5 litres in a 70 kg adult. Expands within days of starting training, which is one reason early fitness gains come so fast. See chapter 11.
Bohr effect
Haemoglobin releases oxygen more readily when blood is warmer, more acidic and higher in carbon dioxide. Convenient: exactly the conditions inside hard-working muscle. See chapter 10.
Bonking
The sudden collapse in effort when liver glycogen runs down and blood glucose falls, starving the brain. Distinct from "hitting the wall", which is local muscle glycogen depletion. See chapter 28.
Bone mineral density
How much mineral is packed into bone, and the standard proxy for bone strength. Responds to high-rate impact and heavy loading, not to volume of easy movement — which is why cycling builds almost none. See chapter 2.
Bone stress injury
A spectrum from stress reaction to full stress fracture, caused by loading accumulating faster than bone can remodel. The one injury category where relative rest genuinely is required. See chapter 42.
Bracing
Deliberately stiffening the trunk before a heavy lift by raising pressure inside the abdomen. Not "sucking in" — closer to preparing to be punched. See chapter 25.

C

Cadence
Steps per minute in running, or pedal revolutions per minute in cycling. Raising running cadence 5–10% shortens the stride and reduces load on several structures — the cheapest mechanical intervention available. See chapter 43.
Caffeine
The best-supported performance drug there is. Roughly 3–6 mg per kg, 45–60 minutes beforehand, worth about 2–4% in endurance. It works by blocking adenosine receptors, which lowers how hard the effort feels. See chapter 33.
Capillarisation
Growth of new capillaries in trained muscle, shortening the distance oxygen must diffuse. Endurance training can raise capillary density substantially. See chapter 8.
Carbohydrate loading
Deliberately over-filling glycogen stores before a long event. The modern version is 1–2 days at 10–12 g per kg of body mass — not the miserable depletion week of the 1970s. See chapter 28.
Cardiac output
Blood pumped per minute: heart rate × stroke volume. Around 5 L/min at rest, rising to 20–25 L/min in trained adults and above 35 L/min in elite endurance athletes. See chapter 9.
Cartilage
The smooth tissue covering joint surfaces. Almost no blood supply, so it heals very poorly — one of the slowest tissues in the body.
Catecholamines
Adrenaline and noradrenaline. Released during exercise to mobilise fuel, raise heart rate and sharpen attention.
Central fatigue
Fatigue arising from reduced drive out of the nervous system rather than from the muscle itself. Part of why a fresh mind can lift more than a tired one at identical muscle condition. See chapter 6.
Concentric
The shortening phase of a contraction — standing up out of a squat.
Concurrent training
Training endurance and strength in the same programme. Pooled evidence shows a small-to-moderate blunting of lower-body strength and power, with hypertrophy largely unaffected. See chapter 20.
Cortisol
A stress hormone that mobilises fuel. Routinely cast as the villain of training; in fact a normal and necessary part of the exercise response. Chronic elevation with poor recovery is the problem, not its presence.
Creatine monohydrate
The most robustly supported sports supplement available. 3–5 g a day, or 20 g a day for 5–7 days to load. Raises high-intensity work capacity and lean mass. Monohydrate only; no other form performs better. See chapter 33.
Critical power (CP)
The highest power output sustainable without fatigue becoming inexorable — the boundary of the severe-intensity domain. More physiologically grounded than FTP. See chapter 35.
Crossover concept
The observation that fuel use shifts from mostly fat at low intensity to mostly carbohydrate above roughly 60% of VO₂max. See chapter 8.
Cross-bridge cycle
The four-step sequence in which a myosin head attaches to actin, pivots to pull it, detaches and resets — spending one ATP each time round. Repeated billions of times per contraction. See chapter 4.

D

Deload
A planned easier week that dissipates fatigue while retaining fitness. Usually cut volume and keep intensity, not the reverse. See chapter 21.
Detraining
Loss of adaptation when training stops. Plasma volume goes within days, aerobic fitness over weeks, strength and muscle more slowly. Not a punishment — the same process that built you, running in reverse.
DIAAS
Digestible Indispensable Amino Acid Score — a measure of protein quality accounting for how well each amino acid is actually absorbed. Animal proteins generally score higher, which is why plant-based athletes need a bigger total intake. See chapter 29.
Diastole
The filling phase of the heartbeat. Trained hearts fill more completely and more easily, which is most of where extra stroke volume comes from.
DOMS
Delayed-onset muscle soreness: the stiffness peaking a day or two after unfamiliar exercise, caused mainly by eccentric loading. Not a measure of how good the session was — it tracks novelty, and it fades while adaptation continues. See chapter 36.
Dorsiflexion
Pulling the toes up toward the shin. The opposite of plantarflexion.
Dose–response
How much adaptation you get for how much training. Almost always a curve with diminishing returns, not a straight line — the central fact behind most programming decisions. See chapter 17.
Durability
How well your physiology holds up deep into a long effort — your threshold after three hours rather than when fresh. Increasingly seen as a better predictor of endurance performance than fresh laboratory values. See chapter 14.

E

Eccentric
The lengthening phase of a contraction — lowering into a squat, or the moment your calf absorbs landing. Produces more force than shortening, causes most muscle damage, and is where several injuries happen.
Economy
How much oxygen or energy a given pace costs you. Two runners with identical VO₂max can differ substantially in race performance because of it. Improved by strength work and plyometrics. See chapter 41.
Effect size
How big a difference is, in standardised units, so different studies can be compared. Roughly: 0.2 small, 0.5 moderate, 0.8 large. Far more informative than a p-value. See How to Read the Evidence.
EMG (electromyography)
Measurement of electrical activity in a muscle during exercise. Useful mechanistically, but a poor predictor of long-term growth — the exercise with the highest EMG is not reliably the one that builds the most muscle. See chapter 26.
Energy availability
Energy left for bodily function after exercise is paid for. Chronically low availability drives RED-S and is the most consequential nutrition problem in sport. See chapter 34.
EPOC
Excess post-exercise oxygen consumption — the elevated metabolic rate after exercise. Real, larger after intense work, and much smaller than popular claims suggest. See chapter 15.
Erector spinae
The columns of muscle running either side of the spine that keep you upright. Extraordinarily fatigue-resistant by design, and a major reason heavy deadlifts take so long to recover from. See chapter 25.
Erythropoietin (EPO)
A hormone from the kidneys that tells bone marrow to make red blood cells. Raised by altitude exposure, and also by the plasma-volume changes of heat acclimation.
Excitation–contraction coupling
The chain from a nerve signal arriving at a muscle to calcium release inside the fibre exposing the binding sites that let contraction begin. See chapter 4.

F

Fascia
Sheets of connective tissue wrapping and connecting muscles. Continuous throughout the body, which is why force does not travel only along the line you would draw between two attachment points. See chapter 3.
Fascicle
A bundle of muscle fibres. How fascicles are arranged — parallel or angled — largely determines whether a muscle is built for force or for speed and range.
FATmax
The exercise intensity at which you burn fat fastest in grams per minute — typically 0.3–0.6 g/min, higher in trained athletes. Not a fat-loss prescription: total energy balance over weeks decides fat loss, not the fuel mix during one session. See chapter 30.
Fibre type
See type I, type IIa, type IIx.
Fick equation
VO₂ = cardiac output × a–vO₂ difference. In words: oxygen use equals delivery multiplied by extraction. The single most useful equation in endurance physiology. See chapter 9.
Fitness–fatigue model
The idea that performance on any day is fitness minus fatigue. It explains why you often feel worst at the point you are adapting most, and why a taper works. See chapter 13.
Force–velocity relationship
The inverse trade-off between how much force a muscle makes and how fast it shortens. You cannot be maximally strong and maximally fast in the same instant; power peaks between the two. See chapter 18.
Fractional utilisation
What percentage of your VO₂max you can hold for a given race duration. Along with economy, it is why VO₂max alone predicts performance poorly.
FTP
Functional threshold power — the highest power a cyclist can hold in a quasi-steady state for roughly an hour, usually estimated as 95% of a 20-minute maximal effort. A convenient approximation, not a physiological constant. See chapter 44.
Functional
A word used so loosely in fitness marketing that it carries almost no information. Ask what specific quality is being transferred to what specific task, and the term usually dissolves.

G

Gastrocnemius
The visible, two-headed calf muscle. It crosses the knee, so it must be trained with the knee straight. See chapter 22.
Gluteus maximus
The largest muscle in the body; extends the hip and rotates it outward. Drives acceleration and sprinting. Hex-bar deadlifts and hip thrusts top the activation rankings, though squats and hip thrusts produce similar measured growth. See chapter 22.
Gluteus medius
The muscle on the side of the hip that stops the pelvis dropping when you stand on one leg. Weakness here is associated with patellofemoral pain and iliotibial band syndrome. See chapter 43.
Glycogen
The body's stored form of carbohydrate: roughly 400–500 g in muscle and 80–100 g in the liver. Muscle glycogen fuels the muscle it sits in; liver glycogen maintains blood glucose for the brain. See chapter 28.
Glycolysis
Breaking glucose down to pyruvate (and then lactate) without oxygen. Very fast, which is why it dominates efforts of roughly 15–60 seconds. See chapter 7.
Golgi tendon organ
A sensor in the tendon that measures tension and feeds it back to the spinal cord. Part of why you cannot voluntarily access your full theoretical force. See chapter 6.
Grey zone
Training that is too hard to accumulate volume in and too easy to stress maximal oxygen uptake. The commonest way for endurance athletes to plateau. See chapter 14.
Grip strength
Both the usual limiter in heavy pulling and one of the better simple predictors of all-cause mortality in population studies. See chapter 24.
Gut training
Progressively increasing carbohydrate intake during long sessions so the intestine adapts to absorb more. The reason trained athletes can tolerate 90–120 g per hour that would wreck an untrained gut. See chapter 27.

H

Haemoglobin
The iron-containing protein in red blood cells that carries about 98% of the oxygen in your blood. Low haemoglobin destroys endurance capacity.
Haemoglobin mass
The total amount of haemoglobin you have, as opposed to its concentration. A slow but powerful endurance adaptation, raised by altitude exposure and gated by iron status. See chapter 11.
Hamstrings
The three muscles on the back of the thigh. They both extend the hip and flex the knee, so they need training in both roles. Injured most often at long length in late swing phase of sprinting — which is what makes the Nordic curl so effective. See chapter 22.
Heart-rate reserve
The gap between resting and maximal heart rate. Used to set zones as a percentage of that range (the Karvonen method), which individualises better than percentage of maximum alone.
Heat acclimation
Adaptation to repeated heat exposure: plasma volume expands 6–10% within about five days, sweating begins earlier and becomes more dilute, and heart rate falls at the same workload. See chapter 37.
Heavy slow resistance (HSR)
Heavy lifting at a deliberate tempo, typically 3 seconds up and 3 seconds down for 6–8 reps. The best-supported loading protocol for tendon problems. See chapter 19.
Hepcidin
A hormone that blocks iron absorption, and which rises for several hours after hard exercise. It is the reason iron timing relative to training matters. See chapter 31.
Heritability
The proportion of variation in a trait across a population attributable to genetics. For VO₂max, estimates run roughly 0.43–0.72. Commonly misread as a ceiling on an individual's improvement, which it is not. See chapter 38.
HIIT
High-intensity interval training: repeated hard efforts, typically 1–8 minutes at 90–100% of maximal oxygen uptake, separated by recovery. Distinct from SIT. See chapter 15.
HRV
Heart-rate variability — variation in the interval between beats, used as a readiness marker. Weekly trends are more informative than daily readings, and one systematic review found resting HRV largely unaffected by overreaching. Treat claims cautiously. See chapter 35.
Hyponatraemia
Dangerously low blood sodium, usually from drinking too much low-sodium fluid over a long event. Rarer than dehydration and considerably more dangerous. See chapter 32.
Hypertrophy
Growth in the size of muscle fibres, driven primarily by mechanical tension. Achievable across a wide range of loads provided sets are taken reasonably close to failure. See chapter 17.

I

Iliotibial band
A reinforced strip of the fascia on the outside of the thigh, running from the hip to just below the knee. It cannot be stretched longer or "released" — it is dense fascia anchored to the femur. See chapter 43.
Iliotibial band syndrome
Lateral knee pain from compression of an innervated fat pad beneath the band, not from friction. The second commonest running injury. See chapter 43.
Insulin
The hormone that moves glucose and amino acids into cells after eating. Central to refuelling; not the villain of body composition that popular diet writing makes it.
Interference effect
The proposed blunting of strength adaptation by concurrent endurance training. Real but modest, and more dependent on modality, session spacing and total load than on molecular signalling. See chapter 20.
Intra-abdominal pressure
Pressure generated inside the abdomen by coordinating the diaphragm, abdominal wall and pelvic floor. It stiffens the trunk so force can transfer between legs and arms. See chapter 25.
Iron deficiency
Low iron stores, which impair oxygen transport and endurance. Common in endurance, female and plant-based athletes. Requires blood testing — ferritin, transferrin saturation and haemoglobin — because iron overload is also harmful. See chapter 31.
Isometric
A contraction with no change in muscle length — a plank, or holding a weight still. Heavy isometric holds often reduce tendon pain immediately, which makes them a useful entry point in rehabilitation. See chapter 42.

J–K

Joint
Where two bones meet. Synovial joints — knee, hip, shoulder — have a lubricated cavity and allow the most movement. See chapter 2.
Ketogenic diet
A very low carbohydrate diet that shifts metabolism heavily toward fat. Raises fat oxidation dramatically, but downregulates the enzymes needed to use carbohydrate at high intensity and tends to worsen economy at race pace. See chapter 30.

L

Lactate
A molecule produced during fast carbohydrate breakdown. A fuel, not a waste product, and not the cause of muscle burn or next-day soreness. It is shuttled between fibres and organs and burned for energy. Useful as a marker of intensity. See chapter 7.
Lactate threshold 1 (LT1)
The aerobic threshold — the intensity at which blood lactate first rises above resting, usually near 2 mmol/L. The practical ceiling of genuinely easy training. See chapter 14.
Lactate threshold 2 (LT2)
The anaerobic threshold or maximal lactate steady state — the highest intensity at which lactate production and clearance still balance, usually near 4 mmol/L. Roughly an hour's sustainable effort. See chapter 14.
Latissimus dorsi
The large fan-shaped back muscle that pulls the arm down and back. The biggest muscle of the upper body by area, and at long length overhead — which is why full-stretch pull-ups matter. See chapter 24.
Length–tension relationship
Muscle produces most force at an intermediate length where filament overlap is optimal, and less when very short or very stretched. It is why exercise choice and range of motion change which part of a muscle is challenged. See chapter 4.
Leucine threshold
The amount of the amino acid leucine needed in a meal to switch muscle protein synthesis fully on — roughly 2.5–3 g, usually from about 0.3–0.4 g of protein per kg of body mass. See chapter 29.
Lever / moment arm
The perpendicular distance from a joint to the line of force. A longer moment arm means more torque for the same muscle force — which is why an exercise is hardest at particular joint angles. See chapter 2.
Live high train low (LHTL)
Sleeping at altitude to stimulate red-cell production while training low enough to keep intensity high. Gains in haemoglobin mass are real but modest, and non-responders are common. See chapter 37.

M

Macrocycle
The longest planning unit — typically a season or year.
Mechanical tension
The force experienced by a muscle fibre, and the primary driver of growth. Sensed by proteins in the fibre's structure and converted into a chemical signal (mechanotransduction). See chapter 17.
Mesocycle
A training block of typically 3–6 weeks with a single emphasis.
Meta-analysis
A study that statistically pools the results of many studies. Stronger than any single trial — but pooling poor studies produces a precise, confident, wrong answer. See How to Read the Evidence.
Microcycle
Usually a week — the smallest repeating unit of a plan.
Mitochondrion
The structure inside cells that produces most ATP using oxygen. Occupies roughly 3–5% of untrained muscle volume and can approximately double with endurance training. See chapter 8.
Mobility
Usable range of motion with control, as distinct from flexibility, which is passive range. Mobility is what transfers to lifting and sport. See chapter 19.
Motor unit
One motor neuron plus every muscle fibre it controls — the smallest unit of voluntary force. Small units may control a handful of fibres; large ones over a thousand. See chapter 5.
mTORC1
The signalling hub that switches on muscle protein building, activated by mechanical tension, leucine and insulin. Stays elevated 18–24 hours after resistance training — much longer than AMPK's three hours. See chapter 12.
Muscle protein synthesis (MPS)
The rate at which muscle builds new protein. Growth happens when synthesis exceeds breakdown over time, not within a single session. See chapter 29.
Muscle spindle
A sensor inside muscle that detects length and rate of stretch, triggering the stretch reflex. See chapter 6.
Myofibril
The contractile strand inside a muscle fibre, made of sarcomeres end to end.
Myokine
A signalling molecule released by contracting muscle that affects other organs — including the brain. The mechanistic heart of "exercise is medicine". See chapter 12.
Myosin
The thick filament and its motor heads, which pull on actin to shorten a muscle. Which version of myosin a fibre contains is what defines its type. See chapter 4.

N

Neural adaptation
Improvement in how well the nervous system recruits and coordinates muscle. Accounts for most strength gain in the first 2–4 weeks, before any measurable growth. See chapter 16.
Nitrate (dietary)
Found in beetroot and leafy greens; converted to nitric oxide, improving efficiency. Roughly 6–8 mmol, 2–3 hours before. Benefit shrinks in highly trained athletes, and antibacterial mouthwash blocks it. See chapter 33.
Nordic hamstring curl
A bodyweight exercise lowering the torso slowly with the ankles fixed, loading the hamstrings eccentrically at long length. Including it in prevention programmes substantially reduces hamstring injury rates — one of the strongest findings in sports medicine. See chapter 41.

O

Osteogenic
Bone-building. Requires high-magnitude, high-rate loading — impacts above roughly 4 g — which is why cycling and swimming provide almost none. See chapter 2.
Overload (progressive)
The requirement that training demand increase over time for adaptation to continue. The only genuinely non-negotiable training principle. See chapter 13.
Overreaching
Short-term performance decline from heavy training. Functional if performance rebounds above baseline after rest; non-functional if it does not. See chapter 36.
Overtraining syndrome
A long-term state of impaired performance with systemic symptoms, requiring weeks to months of recovery. Genuinely rare, frequently self-diagnosed, and identifiable only in retrospect. See chapter 36.
Over-unders
Intervals alternating just above and just below threshold, training you to clear lactate while still working hard. See chapter 44.
Oxidative phosphorylation
The oxygen-using process in mitochondria that produces most of your ATP. Slow to ramp up, effectively unlimited in capacity.

P

Pennation angle
The angle at which muscle fibres attach to a tendon. Angled (pennate) muscles pack more fibres into a cross-section, producing more force but with less range and speed. See chapter 3.
Periodization
Organising training into phases with different emphases. Periodised programmes beat unstructured ones; no specific model clearly beats another when volume is equated. See chapter 21.
PGC-1α
The master switch for mitochondrial building, activated by calcium signalling, AMPK and other exercise signals. See chapter 8.
Phosphocreatine (PCr)
A high-energy store in muscle that regenerates ATP almost instantly. Powers maximal efforts of roughly 10 seconds, and is why creatine supplementation works. See chapter 7.
Physiological cross-sectional area (PCSA)
The total cross-section of all a muscle's fibres measured perpendicular to their direction. The best single structural predictor of how much force a muscle can make — better than its volume or length. See chapter 3.
Plantarflexion
Pointing the toes down — what the calf does at every push-off.
Plasma volume
The liquid part of blood. Expands 8–10% within days of starting training, raising stroke volume — the fastest meaningful endurance adaptation there is. See chapter 11.
Plyometrics
Jumping and bounding exercises that train the stretch-shortening cycle and raise tendon stiffness. Improve running economy. Dosed by number of landings, not by time. See chapter 18.
Polarised training
A distribution with roughly 75–90% of training below LT1, little in the middle, and 10–20% above LT2. Common in elite endurance athletes, along with the pyramidal pattern. See chapter 14.
Post-activation potentiation
A temporary boost in power following a heavy or explosive effort, usable in the last phase of a warm-up with the right rest interval. See chapter 40.
Preload
How much the heart's chamber is filled before it contracts. More preload means a bigger stroke volume — which is why extra plasma volume raises aerobic capacity.
Prime mover
The muscle doing most of the work in a movement. See also synergist and stabiliser, and chapter 26.
Proprioception
Your sense of where your body is and how fast it is moving, built from sensors in muscle, tendon and joints. See chapter 6.
Pyramidal training
A distribution with most volume easy, a moderate amount at threshold, and the least at high intensity. At least as common as polarised in elite endurance athletes, and the distinction between the two is often overstated. See chapter 14.

Q–R

Quadriceps
The four muscles on the front of the thigh that straighten the knee. One of them, rectus femoris, also crosses the hip, so it only reaches long length when the hip is extended — which changes how you must train it. See chapter 22.
RAMP
A warm-up framework: Raise, Activate, Mobilise, Potentiate. See chapter 40.
Range of motion
How far a joint travels in an exercise. Training through a full range, especially at long muscle lengths, generally produces more growth than a partial range. See chapter 17.
Rate coding
Increasing force by firing motor units faster rather than recruiting more of them. The main remaining route to force once the whole pool is already recruited. See chapter 5.
Rate of force development (RFD)
How quickly you can produce force. Often matters more than maximum force in sport, and is trained by intent to move fast rather than by heavy load alone. See chapter 16.
RED-S
Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport: a syndrome from chronic low energy availability impairing metabolic, hormonal, bone, cardiovascular and psychological function in athletes of both sexes. See chapter 34.
Repeated-bout effect
The way soreness and damage from an unfamiliar exercise fall sharply the second time you do it, even as adaptation continues — which is why soreness is a poor guide to stimulus. See chapter 36.
Reps in reserve (RIR)
How many more repetitions you could have done. 0 RIR is failure. Hypertrophy improves as sets end closer to failure; strength is largely indifferent to it. See chapter 17.
Reversibility
The principle that adaptations fade when the stimulus is removed, at rates specific to each tissue.
RPE
Rating of perceived exertion — a subjective 1–10 or 6–20 scale. Unfashionably simple and often outperforms wearables for monitoring load. See chapter 35.

S

SAID principle
Specific Adaptation to Imposed Demand: you get better at what you actually do, and transfer narrows as you become more trained. See chapter 13.
Sarcomere
The smallest contractile unit of muscle, running from one Z-disc to the next. Millions in series and parallel make up a muscle. See chapter 4.
Sarcopenia
Age-related loss of muscle mass and function. Substantially slowed by resistance training and adequate protein — which is why both matter more with age, not less. See chapter 38.
Satellite cell
A muscle stem cell that can fuse with a fibre and donate a nucleus, supporting growth and repair. Part of why previously trained muscle regains size quickly. See chapter 17.
SGLT1
The intestinal transporter for glucose. It saturates near 60 g per hour, which is precisely why glucose–fructose mixtures are needed to absorb more. See chapter 27.
SIT
Sprint interval training: very short all-out efforts, typically 10–30 seconds with long recoveries. Works through metabolic signalling rather than time spent near VO₂max. See chapter 15.
Size principle
Motor units are recruited smallest and slowest first, with larger, faster ones joining as force demand rises. The two practical exceptions are explosive intent and fatigue late in a set. See chapter 5.
Sliding filament theory
The explanation of contraction as thick and thin filaments sliding past one another, not shortening themselves. See chapter 4.
Sodium bicarbonate
Baking soda, used as a buffer outside the cell. Roughly 0.2–0.3 g per kg taken 60–180 minutes before, worth around 2% in severe-intensity efforts. Gastrointestinal distress is the limiting factor. See chapter 33.
Soleus
The deep calf muscle, up to 80–90% slow-twitch, which takes six to eight times body weight per running stride. It does not cross the knee, so it must be trained with the knee bent. Chronically under-trained. See chapter 41.
Specificity
See SAID principle.
Stabiliser
A muscle that holds a joint or the trunk steady so other muscles can produce movement. Much of what the body does during running and lifting is stabilising. See chapter 26.
Stretch tolerance
How much stretch sensation you will accept. The main mechanism by which range of motion improves with stretching — the muscle is not usually becoming longer. See chapter 19.
Stretch-shortening cycle (SSC)
Loading a muscle–tendon unit by stretching it immediately before shortening, so stored elastic energy and reflex activity add free force. Why a countermovement jump beats a jump from a held squat. See chapter 18.
Stroke volume
Blood ejected per heartbeat. Roughly 70–80 mL at maximum untrained, and 160–210 mL in elite endurance athletes — by far the most trainable term in the Fick equation. See chapter 9.
Supercompensation
The idea that after a stress and adequate recovery, fitness overshoots its previous level. Useful as a picture; the tidy curve usually drawn is a simplification of the fitness–fatigue model. See chapter 13.
Sweet spot
Cycling work at roughly 88–93% of FTP — high enough to drive threshold adaptations, low enough to accumulate a large volume and train again the next day. See chapter 44.
Synergist
A muscle assisting the prime mover in a movement.

T

Taper
Reducing training volume by 40–60% while keeping intensity, for one to three weeks before competition. The best-evidenced performance intervention in endurance sport, typically worth 2–3%. See chapter 21.
Tendinopathy
Painful tendon dysfunction. Not inflammation — a failed healing response with disorganised collagen, which is why rest alone does not fix it and progressive loading does. See chapter 42.
Tendon
The dense collagen structure connecting muscle to bone. It stiffens and thickens with heavy slow loading over 8–14 weeks — far slower than muscle, which is the root of most overuse injury. See chapter 19.
Titin
A giant spring-like protein running through the sarcomere that provides passive tension and keeps the filaments aligned.
Torque
Rotational force about a joint: muscle force multiplied by its moment arm. What actually moves a limb. See chapter 2.
Training intensity distribution (TID)
How training time is divided between easy, moderate and hard work. The single most consequential decision in endurance programming. See chapter 14.
Train low
Deliberately training with low glycogen to amplify the adaptive signal. Adaptation markers improve; performance outcomes are equivocal, and it impairs hard sessions. See chapter 28.
Transverse abdominis
The deepest abdominal muscle, wrapping the trunk like a corset and contributing to intra-abdominal pressure. Its role has been both overstated and over-isolated in rehabilitation fashion. See chapter 25.
Type I fibre
Slow-twitch: slow to contract, highly fatigue-resistant, rich in mitochondria. Recruited first and used in all easy activity. The soleus is up to 80–90% type I. See chapter 5.
Type IIa fibre
Fast oxidative: fast-contracting but reasonably fatigue-resistant. The middle ground, and the most adaptable fibre type.
Type IIx fibre
Fast glycolytic: fastest and most powerful, fatigues quickly, recruited only at high force or high speed. Shifts toward IIa with almost any training.

U–V

VDOT
Jack Daniels' single fitness number derived from a recent race time, from which training paces for Easy, Marathon, Threshold, Interval and Repetition running are read off. See chapter 14.
Velocity-based training
Using bar speed to set load and decide when a set should end, instead of fixed percentages. See chapter 35.
Ventilatory threshold
A breakpoint in breathing rate during a graded test, used as a non-invasive proxy for the lactate thresholds. See chapter 10.
VO₂max
The maximum rate at which you can take up and use oxygen, in mL per kg per minute. Roughly 35–45 in untrained adults, 70–85 in elite endurance athletes. The ceiling on aerobic performance — but not on its own a good predictor of race results. See chapter 9.
Volume
Total work done — weekly sets per muscle in lifting, or distance and time in endurance. Muscle growth rises with volume on a curve of diminishing returns; strength shows much stronger diminishing returns. See chapter 17.

W–Z

W′ (W-prime)
The finite, rechargeable amount of work available above critical power, measured in kilojoules — typically 15–25 kJ in trained amateurs. Your "battery" for attacks and sprints. See chapter 35.
Wingate test
A 30-second maximal cycling sprint against heavy resistance, used to measure anaerobic capacity and as the basis of classic sprint-interval protocols.
Wolff's law
The principle that bone remodels in response to the loads placed on it, laying down material where stress is highest. See chapter 2.
Work:rest ratio
The relationship between effort and recovery in interval training. It determines which energy system is stressed as much as the intensity does. See chapter 15.
Z-disc
The boundary at each end of a sarcomere, where thin filaments anchor. The repeating pattern of Z-discs is what makes skeletal muscle look striped.
Zone 2
Easy aerobic training at or just below LT1 — conversational, sustainable for hours, and the single biggest driver of mitochondrial and capillary adaptation per unit of fatigue. The most under-done intensity in amateur training. See chapter 14.

Two things worth knowing. First, where an entry says a term is commonly misused — lactate, the iliotibial band, EPOC, the anabolic window, FATmax, DOMS, "functional" — the misuse is usually the popular meaning, so expect to meet it everywhere. Second, a definition is a starting point, not an understanding: every entry here links to the chapter that shows the mechanism, because knowing what a word means and knowing what to do about it are different things.
