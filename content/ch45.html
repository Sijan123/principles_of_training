Stand on one leg on the edge of a kerb, rise onto your toes, and stop. Do nothing else. Nothing moves, your heart rate barely changes, and after a minute the only evidence of work is a hot, shaking calf. Yet inside that stillness the Achilles tendon is carrying several times your body weight, and it is being stretched by a few millimetres by a muscle that is working hard to look motionless. A runner's leg spends most of every stride doing exactly this, for a fraction of a second at a time. This chapter asks whether practising that stillness on purpose, in a few well-chosen positions, makes the spring in the leg stiffer, the muscle stronger and the running cheaper.

What An Isometric Contraction Actually Is

An isometric contraction is one where the muscle produces force but the joint does not move. The name means "same length", though that is only roughly true, as we will see. Every other kind of lifting changes the joint angle: a squat shortens the quadriceps on the way up and lengthens it on the way down. An isometric is a squat paused halfway and held.

There are two ways to do one, and they feel and train differently.

  • Yielding. You hold a load against gravity. The calf hold on a kerb is yielding: the force is set by the weight on your back, it is usually submaximal, and you stop when the muscle can no longer hold the position.
  • Overcoming. You push as hard as you can against something that will not move, such as a wall, a fixed bar or a strap. The force is set by your intent, climbs to a maximum within a second or two, and can be repeated for a few seconds at a time.

Two features make isometrics unusual. First, force can be very high with little fatigue and little soreness. Soreness mostly comes from lengthening a muscle under load, and an isometric does none of that, so a hard set of holds leaves far less damage than the same muscle's hardest eccentric work. That is a practical advantage in the middle of a running block, when the legs are already carrying quality sessions.

Second, "the joint does not move" is not the same as "nothing moves". The tendon is elastic. When the calf muscle tightens against a fixed ankle, the muscle fibres shorten slightly and the tendon stretches by the same amount, like a rubber band pulled taut while you hold your hand still. The muscle is not strictly isometric and the tendon is not strictly still. That small stretch is the useful part, because tendon adapts to the strain it is given.

Why Runners Should Care

Running is not a series of pushes. It is a series of bounces. Each foot is on the ground for roughly 0.2 to 0.3 seconds at ordinary distance speeds, and during that window the ankle and knee behave less like motors and more like a stiff spring. The muscle holds almost still while the tendon stretches, stores energy, and gives most of it back on the way out.

Think of a pogo stick. You do not power each bounce by pumping the spring. You stiffen your legs, the spring does the bouncing, and you only top up the energy it loses. If the spring is too soft it bottoms out and wastes the bounce. If your legs are too weak to hold it rigid, the spring folds.

In a runner, the muscle is the person holding the pogo stick firm, and the tendon is the spring. An isometric is practising the holding. Done in the right positions, it also gives the spring a firm, controlled stretch.

The muscle that does the holding at the ankle is the soleus, the broad calf muscle under the more visible gastrocnemius. Musculoskeletal modelling of running by Dorn and colleagues (2012) found that the soleus produced the largest force of any muscle in the running leg, which fits the figure of roughly six to eight times body weight through the Achilles tendon at speed that you will meet in chapter 41. Modelling is not measurement, so treat the rank order as strong and the exact multiples as approximate B.

So isometrics offer something specific. They let you load the soleus, Achilles and patellar tendon, glutes and adductors at very high intensity, in the exact joint angles of stance, without the impact of hopping and without the muscle damage of heavy eccentric work. They sit between mobility work (chapter 19) and the heavy lifting of chapter 41: a quiet, low-cost way to add stimulus to a week that is already full.

Tendon Stiffness And Running Economy

Stiffness is how much force it takes to stretch a tendon by a given amount. A stiffer tendon stretches less for the same load, so the muscle attached to it changes length less, and a muscle that changes length less is a muscle that spends less energy. That is the basic route from tendon to running economy, the oxygen you burn to hold a given pace.

What does it take to make a tendon stiffer? Arampatzis and colleagues (2007) trained the Achilles tendon with isometric contractions at two strain levels. The group that worked at high strain, around 90% of maximum, stiffened the tendon; the group that matched the total work at a lower strain did not. Magnitude of strain was the lever, not time under load. The pooled evidence agrees: Bohm and colleagues (2015) combined intervention studies in healthy adults and found that high-intensity loading, around 90% of maximal voluntary contraction (MVC, the most force you can produce in that position), with contractions of around three seconds or longer, drove the best tendon changes in stiffness and size A for tendon adaptation in principle, grade B for the exact dose.

Duration matters less than intensity but does matter. Kubo and colleagues (2001) compared long (about 20 second) and short (about 1 second) isometric quadriceps contractions in the same legs; long, sustained contractions raised tendon stiffness while short ones did not, at matched intensity. This is a small single study in young men and the result has not been replicated with the same design C, but it is part of why modern protocols use either a few seconds of high effort or several tens of seconds of moderate effort, not a flicker of force.

A small group of recreational distance runners, split into a training group and an untrained control group.

What

About 14 weeks of high-intensity isometric plantarflexion training (pushing the toes down against resistance), with tendon stiffness, calf strength and running economy measured before and after.

Found

The trained group had a stiffer Achilles tendon and a stronger calf, and their running economy improved by about 4%. The control group did not change.

Why it matters: it connects the dots in one trial: isometric loading, stiffer tendon, stronger muscle, cheaper running. Limitation: a small sample of young men running at one speed, no blinding possible, and no one has yet repeated it at scale; the economy gain is plausible rather than proven.

Be careful about what the evidence supports. That high-strain loading makes tendon stiffer is well replicated across training types A; that isometrics in particular do it is supported, but by fewer studies B. The finding that an isometric programme specifically makes runners more economical rests on a handful of small trials C. The broader claim that strength work helps endurance performance is much stronger, backed by the review of Beattie and colleagues (2014) and the work in chapter 41 A, but that evidence is mainly for heavy dynamic lifting. Isometrics are a good supplement to it. They are not shown to be a better version of it.

C Isometric training improving running economy directly: promising, few trials, small samples. Honest planning treats it as a likely bonus, not a guarantee.

Angle And Intent: Hold Where You Run

The most useful rule about isometrics is that strength gained is largest at the joint angle trained. If you only ever squat-hold at a deep knee angle, you get much stronger at that depth, a little stronger around it, and barely stronger at a shallow angle. Imagine strengthening a door hinge by pushing the door only when it is open at 90 degrees. The hinge is sturdier there, and not much sturdier when the door is almost shut.

The size of the window depends on how the training is done. Reviews of the literature (Oranchuk 2019; Lum and Barbosa 2019) and individual studies (Folland 2005; Noorkõiv 2014) agree on three points. Strength transfers some distance either side of the trained angle, roughly 15 to 20 degrees. Training at long muscle lengths, where the muscle is stretched and the joint is further along its range, transfers over a wider range of angles and tends to produce more of the muscle growth that comes with it. And training at several angles builds a flatter, broader strength curve than training at one. These findings are fairly consistent but come from short trials and mostly from knee extension and similar lab tasks B.

Intent is the second lever. Pushing as hard and as fast as possible, a ballistic or rapid isometric, trains the speed at which force rises, the rate of force development, more than a slow, sustained ramp does. Lum and Barbosa (2019) found that isometric training, with either approach, improved dynamic performance such as jumping and sprinting in a majority of studies. Sustained holds lean toward tendon and muscle size, rapid pushes lean toward speed B. For a runner who spends 0.2 seconds on the ground, rate of force development is arguably the more relevant quality, which is why the plan below includes a rapid "wall drive".

The practical conclusion is to choose the angles of running stance. At mid-stance the knee is flexed roughly 40 to 50 degrees and the ankle is dorsiflexed (the shin has tipped forward over the foot). Hip extension is just beginning. Those are the angles the holds in this chapter use.

“Isometrics only make you strong at one angle, so they are useless for running”

Since running moves through a range, a single static position cannot matter.

Strength is angle-specific, but running is not all angles

The specificity is real, with transfer of about 15–20 degrees, wider at long muscle lengths. But stance is a narrow set of angles and a runner can train those few directly. Tendon adaptation depends on strain, not on the angle. And isometrics supplement dynamic work; they do not replace it. Nobody needs a hold to do the work of a squat.

“Static holds make you slow”

Muscles trained without movement lose their snap.

No consistent evidence of that

The review by Lum and Barbosa found isometric training improved or maintained jumping and sprinting in most included studies, particularly with rapid intent. The slowing risk, if any, is from ignoring dynamic work altogether B.

Pain Relief And Other Honest Caveats

You may have read that isometric holds switch off tendon pain. The idea comes largely from a small study by Rio and colleagues (2015) in athletes with patellar tendinopathy: after a bout of isometric knee extensions, six volunteers reported much less pain on a single-leg decline squat, for about 45 minutes. It was a dramatic result in a sample of six C. A larger randomised crossover trial by Holden and colleagues (2020) tried to reproduce it and found isometrics were no better at reducing pain than isotonic (moving) exercise B. The honest reading is that isometrics are a tolerable way to keep a painful tendon loaded, not a proven analgesic. How to use them when you are actually injured is the subject of chapter 42, and this chapter is about the healthy runner.

Two further limits are worth stating plainly. Isometrics do not train the fast, elastic stretch-and-recoil of the stride itself, so they do not replace plyometrics (chapter 18). And they do not replace eccentric work such as Nordic hamstring curls, which load muscle at long length while it is lengthening under force (chapter 41). Think of them as a third tool next to hopping and heavy lifting, not a substitute for either.

Coaching cues for each hold

  • Bent-knee calf hold. Knee bent to about 90 degrees against a wall or in a half-squat, heel as high as comfortable; keep the ankle from rolling out.
  • Straight-knee calf hold. Tall, knee locked, rise on the ball of the foot, big toe pressed down; do not let the heel drift inward.
  • Split-squat hold. Front shin tipped forward, front heel flat, trunk tall; hold the knee over the second toe.
  • Spanish squat. Band anchored behind the knees, lean back into it, shins vertical, thighs about 60 to 70 degrees down; feel the quadriceps, not the back.
  • Single-leg glute bridge hold. Ribs down, pelvis level, drive through the heel; stop when the hips begin to twist.
  • Long-lever hamstring bridge. Heels placed far from the hips, lift until the body forms a straight line; press the heels into the floor.
  • Copenhagen plank. Forearm down, top foot or knee on the bench, hips lifted; build the lever from the knee to the foot before adding time.
  • A-stance wall drive. Hands on a wall, body leaning at about forty degrees, one knee driven up and the other leg pushing hard into the floor; hold five to eight seconds with maximal intent.
  • Side plank with hip abduction. Hips stacked, top leg lifted off the bottom one; keep the line from ear to ankle straight.
  • Tibialis anterior hold. Heels on a step, toes pulled toward the shin and held up; keep the ankle neutral, not turned in.

Slotting Holds Into A Running Week

Isometrics are cheap in fatigue, so they fit around running better than most strength work. A workable pattern is two or three short sessions a week, placed after easy runs or on hard-run days when the legs are already warm, and kept away from the day before a key long run or race. Ten to twenty minutes is enough. Pick four to six holds, not all ten.

There are two goals, with slightly different dose structures.

  • Tendon and stiffness. Either 4–5 sets of 3–5 seconds at near-maximal effort (about 90%), with 2 minutes or more of rest, or 4–5 sets of 30–45 seconds at about 70%, with 1–2 minutes of rest. This follows the intensity and duration logic of the tendon studies above B.
  • Strength at running angles. 3–5 sets of 5–10 second maximal overcoming efforts at the stance angles, with 2–3 minutes of rest. Add a rapid-onset version if speed is the aim C for the exact dose.

Progression, taper and what not to do

Progress by the slowest route first: more time, then more load, then more sets. When a hold is easy, change the lever or add weight in 5% steps, not several changes at once. If a tendon is sore the next morning, repeat the same dose rather than add to it.

In the last week before a race, do not stop. Keep one short session with two or three holds and about half the usual sets, done four or five days out, so that the stiffness gained is not lost by detraining; it can safely be dropped for the last two days. This is a judgement based on how fast strength is retained rather than on a tested taper C.

What not to do. Do not treat holds as a warm-up for fast sessions: a maximal hold before intervals can leave the calf stiff and flat. Do not push through sharp tendon pain, which is the territory of chapter 42. Do not hold your breath; breathe steadily and keep the face relaxed. And do not drop hopping, running drills or heavy lifting, which the broader plan in chapter 41 and the lower-body work in chapter 22 cover.

Five things to carry forward

  1. An isometric holds force without moving the joint, and the tendon still stretches, which is why it works as a low-fatigue way to load the spring in a runner’s leg.
  2. Tendon stiffness responds to the size of the strain, so near-maximal short holds or long moderate ones help, while light, brief holds do not.
  3. Strength gain is largest at the angle trained and carries about 15–20 degrees either side, so choose the knee, ankle and hip angles of running stance.
  4. The evidence for tendon adaptation is solid, but for isometrics improving running economy and for pain relief it is thin or contested, so treat them as a supplement.
  5. Two or three short sessions a week of four to six holds fit around easy runs, and tendon change takes eight to twelve weeks of steady work.
