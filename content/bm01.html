A study found that a supplement improved cycling performance by 3%. The study had eight participants, lasted four days, was funded by the manufacturer, measured time to exhaustion at a fixed workload, and reported p = 0.048. Every one of those facts changes what the 3% means, and none of them will appear in the headline. This chapter is the one that lets you audit the rest of this book — and everything else you will read about training.

The Hierarchy of Evidence, and Why It Is Only a Guide

Evidence is conventionally ranked by how well a study design controls for the things that could fool you. The ranking is useful. It is not a law.

From weakest to strongest — and what each kind of study can and cannot tell you
TypeWhat it can showWhat it cannot
Mechanism / cell cultureThat a pathway exists and could plausibly matterThat it matters in a living human, or by how much. Most things that work in a dish do not work in a person.
Animal studyMechanism in a whole organism, with doses and durations impossible in humansTransfer. Rodent muscle, metabolism and lifespan differ enough that dose and effect rarely carry across.
Case report / case seriesThat something is possible; a useful hypothesis generatorAnything about how often, in whom, or compared to what.
Cross-sectional studyThat two things occur together — e.g. that runners with lateral knee pain have weaker hip abductorsDirection. The weakness might be the cause, or the consequence of months of pain.
Prospective cohortThat a measured factor precedes an outcome — much better for causal reasoningFreedom from confounding. People who train more also sleep, eat and earn differently.
Randomised controlled trialThat an intervention caused a difference, within that population and durationGeneralisation. An eight-week trial in untrained undergraduates may say nothing about a trained athlete's third year.
Systematic reviewWhat the whole literature says, found by a stated, repeatable searchMore than the trials contain. Garbage in, garbage out.
Meta-analysisA pooled estimate of effect size, with a measure of how much the studies disagreeRescue of bad primary studies. Pooling twenty small biased trials produces a precise, confident, wrong number.

Think of the difference between statistical significance and effect size as the difference between a jury's verdict and a sentence.

The p-value is the jury: it answers one narrow question — "are we convinced that something happened at all, rather than this being chance?" A verdict of guilty tells you the jury was persuaded. It tells you nothing whatsoever about whether the crime was shoplifting or arson.

The effect size is the sentence: how big was it? And the confidence interval is the range of sentences consistent with the evidence — "somewhere between a fine and four years" is a very different state of knowledge from "between three and four years", even though both convicted.

A study can convict with total confidence and still be describing something far too small to matter to you. Most of the training literature's noisiest arguments are about verdicts when they should be about sentences.

The Specific Weaknesses of Sports Science

Exercise science is a genuinely difficult field to do well, and its characteristic problems are predictable enough to check for by name.

  • Tiny samples. A typical training study has 10–30 participants per group. With that many people, only large effects can be detected reliably, and the ones that are detected tend to be overestimated — a statistical artefact sometimes called the winner's curse.
  • Mostly young men. Historically a large majority of participants have been young, male, and recreationally active. Findings are then applied to women, to masters athletes and to children, usually without comment.
  • Short durations. Six to twelve weeks is normal. Many adaptations that matter — tendon stiffness, bone density, haemoglobin mass, the shape of a training career — operate on timescales no trial funds.
  • Untrained participants. Beginners improve on almost anything, so trials in untrained people are far better at showing that something works than at showing which thing works better. This is the single commonest reason a well-conducted study fails to generalise.
  • Surrogate outcomes. Measuring muscle growth over a year is expensive; measuring muscle activation this afternoon is cheap. So the literature is full of proxies — and proxies can be wrong (see §3 below).
  • Almost no elite athletes. There are very few of them, they will not be randomised into a control group, and their coaches will not let you change their training. Most of what we know about elite practice is descriptive — we watched what they do — which is valuable but cannot tell us what they should do.
  • Publication and funding bias. Positive results get published; null results sit in drawers. Industry-funded supplement trials are systematically more favourable than independent ones.
  • Blinding is often impossible. You cannot blind someone to whether they are doing intervals. Expectation effects are real and largely uncontrolled in training research.

Surrogate Outcomes: Where Most Bad Advice Comes From

A surrogate is a measurement that stands in for the thing you actually care about. They are not inherently bad — they are often all we can afford — but the gap between surrogate and outcome is where most confident, wrong training advice is manufactured. Three examples recur throughout this book.

  1. EMG activation does not predict muscle growth

    Electromyography measures electrical activity during an exercise this afternoon. It became the engine of a thousand "best exercise for your glutes" articles. But acute activation has repeatedly failed to predict long-term hypertrophy — and the clearest demonstration is that barbell hip thrusts produce substantially higher gluteal EMG than back squats, yet training studies find the two produce similar gluteal growth. The surrogate said one thing; the outcome said another. See chapter 26.

  2. Acute hormonal spikes do not predict hypertrophy

    Short-rest, high-volume training produces larger post-exercise growth hormone and testosterone responses, which generated a whole school of "hormone-optimising" programme design. When the long-term outcomes were measured, the correlation between those acute spikes and actual muscle growth was poor. See chapter 12.

  3. Molecular signalling does not guarantee adaptation

    A session that strongly activates AMPK or raises PGC-1α is doing something real. But signalling is a necessary step, not a sufficient one, and the magnitude of a signalling response does not translate proportionally into mitochondria, let alone into performance. This is the main reason "train low" looks so much better in its mechanism than in its race results. See chapter 28.

Whenever you meet a confident recommendation, ask what was actually measured. If the answer is activation, a hormone concentration, a signalling protein, a blood marker, or "time to exhaustion at a fixed workload", you are being shown a proxy. Proxies are evidence that a mechanism is operating. They are not evidence that the thing you want will happen.

Reading the Numbers

A real-shaped result: "Supplement X improved 20 km time-trial performance by 1.6% (p = 0.04, n = 11, Cohen's d = 0.28, 95% CI 0.1% to 3.1%)." What does that actually mean for you?
  1. Translate the percentage into your own terms. If your 20 km takes 35 minutes:35:00 × 0.016 = 34 s faster
  2. Now read the confidence interval, not the point estimate. The data are consistent with anything from 0.1% to 3.1%:0.1% = 2 s  |  3.1% = 65 s
  3. So the honest statement is "somewhere between trivially small and quite useful."2 s … 65 s, best guess 34 s
  4. Check the p-value against the sample size. With n = 11 and p = 0.04, this result sits close to the convention and would very plausibly not replicate.n = 11 → wide CI, low power, likely overestimated effect
  5. Check the effect size. Cohen's d = 0.28 is conventionally "small".d: 0.2 small · 0.5 moderate · 0.8 large
  6. Compare against the thing it must beat — the typical error of the test itself. If your own 20 km times vary by 2% week to week regardless:measurement noise 2% > estimated effect 1.6%
So: this is a real but small finding with wide uncertainty, from a sample too small to trust on its own, describing an effect smaller than your own week-to-week variation. It is worth filing and watching for replication. It is not worth reorganising your training around, and it absolutely does not support "proven to make you 1.6% faster."
p-value
The probability of seeing a result at least this extreme if the intervention did nothing. Low p means "probably not pure chance." It says nothing about size, importance, or whether the study was well designed. The 0.05 threshold is a convention, not a fact about nature.
Effect size (Cohen's d, standardised mean difference)
How big the difference is, expressed in standard deviations so that different studies can be compared. Rough conventions: 0.2 small, 0.5 moderate, 0.8 large — but in trained athletes a "small" effect can be the difference between winning and not.
Confidence interval
The range of true values consistent with the data. If it includes zero, the study cannot rule out "no effect". The width tells you how much the study actually learned — and this is usually more informative than the p-value.
Heterogeneity (I²)
In a meta-analysis, how much the included studies disagree. High heterogeneity (say above 60–75%) means the pooled average may be describing no one in particular.
Smallest worthwhile change
The smallest difference that would actually matter in practice. Compare it with the test's typical error before believing any individual result — yours included.
Meta-regression
A meta-analysis that models a dose–response rather than a yes/no answer. Much more useful for training questions, which are almost always about how much rather than whether. Several of the key findings in this book — on training volume and on proximity to failure — come from meta-regressions.

The Interaction Nobody Reports: Training Status

This deserves its own section because it explains more confusion than anything else in the field.

In an untrained person, almost any stimulus works. Three sets or six, heavy or light, intervals or steady — a sedentary beginner improves on all of them, and the differences between methods are swamped by the effect of simply starting. This is why the internet can supply a study supporting any position.

As training age rises, three things change. Adaptations get smaller. The differences between methods become visible, because there is no longer a large general effect masking them. And specificity tightens, so transfer between activities narrows. A protocol that produced a 20% VO2max gain in sedentary adults may produce 2% in trained athletes, and the mechanism with headroom may be a different one entirely.

The practical rule: before believing a finding applies to you, check who was in the study. If the participants were less trained than you, the effect you can expect is smaller — often much smaller — than reported. If they were more trained than you, it may not be the limiting factor in your training at all.

  • The mechanism trap. "Cold water immersion reduces inflammation, and inflammation is catabolic, therefore ice baths help you recover." Every link is plausible and the conclusion is still wrong — habitual post-lifting cold immersion blunts hypertrophy and strength adaptation, because that inflammatory signalling was part of the adaptation. Mechanistic reasoning without an outcome measurement is a hypothesis, not a finding.
  • The elite trap. "Elite marathoners run 180 km a week, so more volume is better." Elites are a survivor population: they are the people whose physiology and luck allowed them to tolerate that training. What they do is evidence about what works for people who can already do it.
  • The single-study trap. Any individual study, however well conducted, is one sample. The findings in this book that carry an A are ones where multiple independent groups found the same direction. Where only one striking study exists, it is graded C, however good it is.

The Grades Used in This Book

Every substantive claim in this book carries one of four grades. They are an honest signal of confidence, and they are the author's judgement — a readable shorthand, not a formal GRADE or Cochrane assessment.

What each grade means, and how much to lean on it
GradeMeansHow to treat it
AReplicated across good trials and supported by meta-analysis, with a consistent direction of effect and large enough total samples to be confident.Build your training on these. They are unlikely to reverse.
BReal but smaller, context-dependent, or shrinking in trained athletes. Often supported by several small studies pointing the same way, or by one good meta-analysis with high heterogeneity.Act on them, but hold them loosely and expect the magnitude to be revised.
CMechanistically plausible with thin, mixed or short-term evidence. Includes single striking studies and findings resting mainly on surrogate outcomes.Reasonable to try if cheap and low-risk. Do not reorganise a programme around them, and do not repeat them as fact.
DNo credible evidence of benefit, or evidence of harm to the adaptation you want.Stop spending time or money on it.

Two deliberate consequences of grading this way. First, you will find places where this book says "we don't know" — the menstrual cycle's effect on performance, the best training-intensity distribution for a given individual, whether train-low improves races as well as markers. That is not evasion; it is the actual state of the evidence, and a book that smoothed it over would be less useful. Second, where a popular belief is graded D, the disagreement is with the belief, not with you for having held it. Most of these ideas were taught in good faith by people working from the best available theory at the time — the friction model of iliotibial band syndrome in chapter 43 is the cleanest example in the book.

The Checklist

Ten questions. They take about ninety seconds and will correctly dismiss the great majority of training claims you encounter.

  1. Who was studied?

    How many, what training status, what sex, what age? Would you be eligible?

  2. What was actually measured?

    The outcome you care about, or a surrogate for it?

  3. Compared with what?

    No control group, a do-nothing control, or a credible alternative treatment? "Better than nothing" is a very low bar.

  4. How big was the effect?

    In units you can use — seconds, kilograms, watts, percent — not just "significant".

  5. How wide is the confidence interval?

    If it spans trivial to large, the study did not settle the question.

  6. How long did it run?

    Long enough for the tissue in question to actually adapt?

  7. Was the comparison fair?

    Were volume, total work, and energy intake equated? Many "method A beats method B" findings are really "more work beats less work".

  8. Has it replicated?

    One study is a hypothesis. Three independent groups is a finding.

  9. Who paid, and who benefits?

    Not disqualifying, but it shifts the burden of proof.

  10. Does the conclusion exceed the data?

    Check the abstract's final sentence against what was actually measured. The gap between them is where most misinformation is created — frequently by the press release rather than the researchers.

What

A formal analysis of how the probability that a published finding is true depends on study power, the number of relationships tested, the prior plausibility of the hypothesis, and bias.

Found

In fields characterised by small studies, small effects, many tested relationships and flexible analysis, the majority of published positive findings will be false — not through fraud, but as a mathematical consequence of how research is conducted and published.

Why it matters here

Sports science has every one of those characteristics: small samples, small effects, many measured variables, and strong incentives to publish positive results.

Why it matters: it reframes scepticism from cynicism into arithmetic. The appropriate response is not to distrust science but to weight replication far more heavily than novelty — which is exactly what the grading system in §6 tries to do. Limitation: it is a modelling argument about fields in aggregate, not a verdict on any particular study, and it is sometimes misused to dismiss findings the reader simply dislikes.

Five things to carry forward

  1. Statistical significance answers whether something happened; effect size and the confidence interval answer whether it matters — and the second question is almost always the one you care about.
  2. Ask what was actually measured: activation, hormone concentrations, signalling proteins and time-to-exhaustion are surrogates, and surrogates have repeatedly pointed the wrong way.
  3. Check the training status of the participants — in beginners almost everything works, which is why the literature can be quoted in support of nearly any position.
  4. Weight replication above novelty: one striking study is a hypothesis, and the characteristic conditions of sports science (small samples, small effects, publication bias) mean a good fraction of single positive findings will not hold.
  5. Where this book says "we don't know", that is the finding — and a recommendation that never admits uncertainty is telling you more about the author than about the evidence.
