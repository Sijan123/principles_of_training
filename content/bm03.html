This book is a synthesis, not original research. Everything in it rests on other people's work — often decades of it — and the honest thing to do is say exactly whose. What follows is the spine of the literature behind each part of the book: landmark primary studies, major reviews, meta-analyses and consensus statements. It is not exhaustive, and it is not a substitute for reading the originals.

How These Sources Were Chosen

Three criteria. Influence — papers that changed how the field thinks, even where later work has qualified them. Weight of evidence — meta-analyses, meta-regressions and consensus statements in preference to single trials, wherever they exist. Verifiability — everything listed here is a paper I am confident exists and have attributed to the right authors, journal and year.

Where I could not confirm an exact volume, issue or page range, I have given author, title, journal and year only, and left the rest off rather than guess at it. Where I could not confirm a paper existed at all, it is not in this list, however relevant it seemed.

Verify before you rely on it. This list was assembled by cross-checking against literature databases, but a reference list in a synthesis like this is a signpost, not a source. If a claim in this book matters to a decision you are making — a training block, an injury, a clinical question, a piece of your own writing — go and read the original paper. Three specific reasons:

  • Paraphrase loses nuance. Where this book compresses a finding into one sentence, the paper will carry caveats about population, dose, duration and measurement that the sentence cannot.
  • Citation errors propagate. Misattributed and drifting citations are endemic in popular sports-science writing, and a plausible-looking reference list is exactly how a wrong number travels. Assume this list could contain an error, and check the ones that matter to you.
  • The literature moves. Several findings in this book — training volume dose–response, proximity to failure, carbohydrate intake ceilings, the acute:chronic workload ratio — have shifted substantially in the last five years and will shift again.

Most of these papers can be found by pasting the title into PubMed or Google Scholar. Many are open access; where they are not, the abstract usually carries the finding.

Where to Follow the Research

Journals

  • Sports Medicine — the best source of high-quality systematic reviews and meta-regressions in the field.
  • Medicine & Science in Sports & Exercise — the ACSM journal; broad and influential.
  • Journal of Applied Physiology and The Journal of Physiology — mechanism, done properly.
  • International Journal of Sports Physiology and Performance — applied work with real athletes.
  • British Journal of Sports Medicine — injury, rehabilitation and consensus statements.
  • Scandinavian Journal of Medicine & Science in Sports — much of the endurance-training literature.
  • Journal of the International Society of Sports Nutrition — position stands; note the ISSN's industry links and read accordingly.

Preprints and synthesis

  • SportRxiv — preprints, often months ahead of publication. Not peer reviewed; read as provisional.
  • PubMed — the primary search tool. Free, and the abstract usually carries the finding.
  • Cochrane Library — where a relevant review exists, the most methodologically careful summary available.
  • Research-review services run by named researchers who publish their own work and show their reasoning are generally reliable; those that do not are generally not.

A great deal of fitness content cites papers it has not read, or cites the abstract's conclusion rather than its result. Two cheap checks: does the source link to the actual paper, and when you open it, does the population match the claim? Those two questions will catch most of it.

Roughly 90 sources sit behind this book, and the ones doing the most work are the meta-analyses and consensus statements rather than the individual trials — which is as it should be. If you read only five things from this list, read Seiler & Kjerland 2006 (how elite endurance athletes actually train), Helgerud 2007 (why intensity raises VO2max), Pelland & Remmert 2025 (the current state of the training-volume question), Morton 2018 (what protein intake actually does), and the 2023 IOC REDs consensus statement (the most consequential thing in sports nutrition). And where this book and a paper disagree, believe the paper.
