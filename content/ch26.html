A rowing eight has eight people pulling, but they are not doing the same job. One or two set the rhythm and produce most of the raw power; several more match that rhythm and add force in support; the coxswain adds no propulsive force at all but keeps the boat from veering off line. Every movement in this book recruits muscles the same way — a prime mover, a supporting cast, and a set of muscles whose entire job is to stop something unwanted from happening. This closing chapter of Part IV is the synthesis: the vocabulary for those roles, the limits of the tool (EMG) most often used to assign them, and a muscle-by-activity reference built to stay consistent with the interactive map in every chapter before it.

Prime Mover, Synergist, Stabiliser, Antagonist

Four roles cover almost every muscle in almost every movement. The prime mover is the muscle most directly responsible for producing the movement — the quadriceps straightening the knee in a squat's ascent. A synergist assists the prime mover, either by adding force in the same direction or by neutralising a side-effect the prime mover would otherwise cause; the hamstrings assisting hip extension in a deadlift while also controlling the knee are acting as synergists to the glutes. A stabiliser produces little or no movement itself but contracts to hold a joint or a body segment still so the prime mover has something fixed to pull against — gluteus medius holding the pelvis level while the opposite leg swings through in a running stride. The antagonist is the muscle on the opposite side of the joint from the prime mover, and its job is usually to relax and lengthen in a controlled way rather than to fight the movement — the triceps relaxing smoothly as the biceps flexes the elbow. Co-contraction of agonist and antagonist together, which stiffens a joint rather than moving it, is itself a stabilising strategy and becomes more prominent under heavy load or an unfamiliar skill.

These roles are not fixed properties of a muscle; they are properties of a muscle in a specific movement. Gluteus maximus is the prime mover in a hip thrust and a significant stabiliser during single-leg stance in running. The body-map widget used throughout this part colours a muscle by the role it plays in whichever movement is selected, for exactly this reason — the same muscle can appear in a different colour in the next chapter's example.

Picture that rowing eight again. The stroke seat sets the pace and pulls hardest — the prime mover. The next few seats match that rhythm and add real force toward the same goal — synergists. The coxswain shouts no stroke at all but keeps the rudder correcting so the boat runs straight rather than veering into the bank — a stabiliser, earning their seat in the boat without ever pulling an oar. And somewhere in the crew, a muscle that would pull the boat the wrong way if it fired has to stay quiet and let itself be stretched — the antagonist, doing its job by doing nothing.

What EMG Can — and Cannot — Tell You

Electromyography (EMG) reads the electrical signal a muscle produces as it contracts, usually through sensors taped to the skin. It is the tool behind most "this exercise activates muscle X by Y%" claims repeated across the fitness industry, and it is a genuinely useful tool for one narrow question: which muscles switch on, and roughly when, during a given movement. It answers that question badly, or not at all, for two much more commonly asked questions — how hard a muscle is working compared with a different muscle, and how much a muscle will grow over a training block — and conflating these three questions is the single most common misuse of EMG in popular writing about training.

The comparison problem is largely a normalisation one. Raw EMG amplitude depends heavily on factors that have nothing to do with how hard a muscle is actually working: how much fat and connective tissue sits between the sensor and the muscle, exactly where the sensor was placed relative to the muscle's fibres, and how large the muscle is to begin with. A detailed methodological review covering over twenty-five years of EMG research concluded that comparing raw activity between different muscles, or between different people, is not valid without correcting for these factors — and that the standard correction, scaling each reading against that same muscle's own maximum voluntary contraction, brings its own problems: it takes extra time, fatigues the muscle before the test movement even begins, and is often impractical in clinical or older populations who cannot produce a reliable maximum at all B. A bar chart ranking five exercises by "percent of MVC" is only as trustworthy as the weakest MVC test in that chart, a detail almost never reported alongside the ranking.

Limitation: a methods review, not new data on any specific exercise — it tells you how to read other papers' numbers, not what those numbers should be.

The second and larger problem is more fundamental: EMG amplitude measured during a single contraction tells you almost nothing directly about how much that muscle will grow over weeks of training. Chapter 22 showed this concretely — the hip thrust and hex-bar deadlift top EMG charts for gluteus maximus, yet a controlled trial found hip-thrust-focused and squat-focused training produced similar measured growth in all three regions of the muscle. Acute electrical activity and chronic structural adaptation are different biological processes, separated by weeks of signalling, protein synthesis and remodelling that a surface sensor reading today's workout cannot see into. EMG is a reasonable guide to which muscles a movement recruits and roughly when; it is a poor guide to how to rank exercises for long-term growth, which is why this book leans on controlled training trials, not activation charts, whenever the two disagree.

Running — Easy, Sprinting, Uphill

Running has one gait cycle but at least three distinct muscular stories, depending on pace and gradient, and each divides into the same two phases: stance, while the foot is on the ground, and swing, while the leg is moving through the air to the next step. Stance itself has three moments worth separating — early stance absorbs the impact of landing, mid-stance supports the body's weight over a single leg, and late stance (propulsion) drives the body up and forward into the next stride.

Easy running is dominated by support, not propulsion: the soleus (chapter 22) and gluteus medius work on almost every stride simply holding the body upright over one leg at a time, which is why both are built for endurance rather than peak force. Sprinting shifts the dominant story toward the hip: a detailed musculoskeletal-modelling study of running across a range of speeds found that as speed rises toward a sprint, the limiting strategy shifts from generating large support forces during ground contact toward swinging the limbs through the air faster — which puts far more demand on the hip flexors driving the thigh forward in early swing and the hamstrings decelerating the shin in late swing B. That late-swing deceleration, hamstring lengthening under load at the exact moment described in chapter 22, is where sprinting actually tears a hamstring. Uphill running changes the picture again: the grade adds knee- and hip-extension work throughout stance while reducing the impact force at landing, which is why hill running is often used to add strength-type loading with less of the impact-related injury risk that flat-ground speed work carries.

Cycling — A Fixed Path With No Eccentric Landing

A pedal stroke splits into sectors by clock position. From roughly 12 to 5 o'clock, the downstroke, the quadriceps and glutes extend the knee and hip and supply most of the propulsive work. From about 5 to 7 o'clock, the bottom sweep, the calf transfers force through the ankle. From 7 to 11 o'clock, the recovery, the leg contributes little net force unless a rider deliberately pulls up with the hip flexors C — cadence and pedal technique shift these boundaries, so treat them as a rough map, not a fixed rule.

This quadriceps dominance is why cycling is called concentric-dominant: the knee and hip are almost always extending under load, rarely lengthening under it the way they do absorbing a running footstrike. No pedal stroke asks a muscle to resist being stretched by an external force the way landing does. That absence of eccentric loading is the direct mechanical reason cycling produces far less muscle damage and soreness than running of equal duration, and why cyclists tolerate far higher weekly volumes before recovery becomes limiting (chapter 36). It is also why cycling alone supplies neither the impact loading bone needs (chapter 1) nor the eccentric, long-length loading that protects hamstrings and calves (chapter 22) — a low-damage stimulus is, by the same mechanism, an incomplete one.

Swimming & Rowing

Swimming, front crawl specifically, is an upper-body-led sport in a way runners and cyclists often underestimate: the lats, pecs and triceps pull the body through the water on every stroke, while the legs mostly stabilise body position and streamline drag rather than produce the majority of forward propulsion. This is part of why competitive swimmers carry noticeably more upper-body mass relative to their legs than runners or cyclists of similar overall fitness, and why dryland training for swimmers leans heavily on pulling strength rather than leg work.

Rowing is close to the most complete single-movement pattern in this book: one stroke contains a leg drive (quadriceps and glutes extending the knee and hip against the footplate), a hip hinge (erector spinae and hamstrings as the trunk swings back), and a horizontal pull (lats, biceps and the mid-trapezius finishing the stroke) — three major patterns most programmes train as separate exercises, sequenced into one continuous motion dozens of times a minute. That completeness is also rowing's main technical risk: the lumbar spine is loaded in a flexed position repeatedly at pace, and the usual rowing back injury comes from the hip hinge collapsing into spinal flexion under fatigue rather than staying hip-driven — the same controlled-versus-uncontrolled-flexion distinction from chapter 25.

The Big Lifts

Five lifts recur across more programmes than any others, and each has already been dissected by muscle earlier in this part; this section is the cross-reference. The back squat and deadlift are both hip-and-knee-extension patterns with the trunk resisting flexion throughout, but the squat keeps the trunk more upright and the quadriceps more dominant, while the deadlift starts from a greater hip-flexion angle and asks more of the glutes, hamstrings and erector spinae from the first inch of the pull (chapter 22, and Fig 22.1). The bench press is the pec, anterior deltoid and triceps working in series, legs and back serving only as a stable platform (chapter 23). The pull-up is the lat and biceps driving a vertical pull with the trunk preventing a swing (chapter 24). The overhead press, outside this chapter's activity data set, is the anterior deltoid as prime mover with the triceps locking out the elbow and serratus anterior and the rotator cuff doing the scapular and joint-centring work of chapter 23 — arguably the lift with the largest ratio of stabiliser to prime-mover effort, which is why it exposes a weak scapular setup fastest.

Choosing Cross-Training & Filling the Gaps Your Sport Leaves

Read across any row of the master table and a sport's blind spots become visible as a row of dashes. A runner's programme, read against the table, is dense with lower-leg and hip stabiliser work and almost empty across the entire upper-body block — no lats, no pecs, no biceps, no grip as anything but a non-entity. A cyclist's row is similar but worse for impact: cycling never asks bone for the loading it needs (chapter 1) and never asks the hamstrings or calf for the eccentric, long-length loading that protects against a sprinting strain, because the pedal stroke is concentric throughout (see above). A swimmer's row is the mirror image: dense upper-body involvement, sparse lower-body loading, and almost no axial bone-loading stimulus either, since the water supports body weight throughout.

A general method for choosing cross-training from the table rather than from habit.

  • Do List your sport's own column (or the nearest match) from the master table, and circle every muscle marked with a dash.
  • Load For each gap, pick one exercise where that muscle is a prime mover, not a stabiliser — a dash needs a P, not another St.
  • Dose 2 sessions a week covering the gaps is usually enough to maintain the capacity, even where the main sport cannot build it.
  • When Separate from, or at the end of, the main sport's hardest sessions, so the gap-filling work does not blunt the primary adaptation.
  • Progress Re-read the table each training block; a filled gap still needs maintenance, it does not become permanent.
  • On the bike If cycling is your sport, this is the chapter that tells you which gaps it specifically cannot close — impact loading and eccentric hamstring/calf work chief among them — rather than assuming more saddle time will eventually cover everything.
  • Signal You're doing it right when the muscle that used to be a blank row in your own training log has a real exercise next to it, not just an intention to "do more upper body sometime."

Five things to carry forward

  1. Prime mover, synergist, stabiliser and antagonist are roles a muscle plays in a specific movement, not fixed labels — the same muscle changes role from one exercise to the next.
  2. Raw EMG amplitude cannot be trusted to compare different muscles or different people without careful normalisation, and even correctly normalised acute activation is a poor predictor of which exercise will grow a muscle most over weeks of training.
  3. Running's muscular demands shift with speed and gradient: easy running is dominated by calf and hip-stabiliser endurance, sprinting shifts the load toward explosive hip flexion and hamstring deceleration, and uphill running adds extensor strength demand while cutting impact.
  4. Cycling's fixed, concentric-dominant pedal stroke makes it low-damage and highly repeatable, but the same mechanism means it cannot provide the impact loading bone needs or the eccentric, long-length loading that protects the hamstrings and calves.
  5. The master cross-reference table turns "I should probably train more" into a specific list of dashes — the muscles your sport leaves as a stabiliser at best, which is exactly what cross-training should be chosen to fix.
