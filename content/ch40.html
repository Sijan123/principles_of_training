Ten minutes before a hard session, almost everyone does something — a jog, a few lunges, a stretch held because it feels like the responsible thing to do. Very few people could say which part of that routine is doing anything, and which part is theatre. The honest answer is that a warm-up is a short, deliberate experiment in applied physiology: raise the tissue temperature, wake the nervous system, rehearse the movement, and leave the long static holds for later. Get the order right and the first interval of the session stops feeling like a shock. Get it wrong — in either direction — and you either walk onto the start line cold or you stretch away the power you came to use.

What A Warm-Up Actually Changes In You

Four things happen in the first ten to fifteen minutes of moving, and all four are measurable.

Muscle temperature rises. Active warm-up typically raises deep muscle temperature by roughly 1–3 °C, depending on intensity, clothing and ambient conditions B. That sounds small. It is not. Warmer muscle means faster cross-bridge cycling — the rate at which myosin heads attach, pull and release from actin, which is the entire mechanical basis of contraction (see chapter 4). In the classic work of Bergh and Ekblom, raising muscle temperature increased peak power output by roughly 2% per °C at slow contraction speeds and by as much as 10% per °C at fast, explosive speeds B. The faster the movement you are about to do, the more a cold muscle costs you.

Nerve conduction speeds up. Warming tissue increases the conduction velocity of peripheral nerves by roughly 1.5–2 m/s for every 1 °C B. A signal from your motor cortex to your calf arrives sooner, and the fine-grained timing that coordination depends on — which motor units fire, in what order, how fast — sharpens along with it.

Joint fluid thins. Synovial fluid, the lubricant inside your knee, hip and ankle capsules, is thixotropic: it is thicker at rest and thins as the joint moves, the same property that makes some paints and ketchup flow more easily once disturbed. A few minutes of movement lowers the viscosity you are moving against before you ever load the joint hard C — a real effect, but not one anyone has pinned to a precise number outside the laboratory.

Oxygen uptake gets a head start. At the onset of any hard effort there is a lag before your aerobic system catches up to the demand, called the oxygen deficit; that lag is filled by anaerobic metabolism, which means early fatigue and early lactate accumulation (see chapter 7). A gradual aerobic warm-up raises baseline oxygen uptake and blood flow to working muscle before the hard effort starts, shrinking that deficit B. This is the single most practical reason interval sessions and races are not run from a standing, cold start: without it, the first interval is run partly anaerobic by accident, not by design.

And finally, a warm-up is psychological. Ten minutes of purposeful movement is also ten minutes of mental rehearsal — narrowing attention, raising arousal to the right level for the task, and giving you something to do with pre-event nerves other than stand still and feel them C.

Think of muscle like engine oil. Cold oil is thick; it resists the engine's first few revolutions and the engine runs rough until it comes up to temperature. Warm oil flows, the moving parts slide past each other with less resistance, and the whole engine responds faster to the accelerator.

A warm-up is bringing your own oil up to temperature before you ask for peak output — and, unlike an engine, also loading the software: rehearsing the exact movement pattern the session is about to demand, so the first rep looks like the fiftieth.

The RAMP Framework

The clearest way to structure the ten minutes is a framework used widely in athletic preparation: Raise, Activate, Mobilise, Potentiate — RAMP. It is not a rigid script; it is an order of priorities that happens to map neatly onto the physiology above.

Static Versus Dynamic Stretching Before You Perform

This is the question that generates the most folklore, so it is worth being precise about what the evidence actually says, rather than the version that got simplified into "never stretch before you run."

The honest summary has three parts, and the dose is what matters:

  • Long static holds — more than roughly 60 seconds per muscle, especially stacked across several muscles — produce a reliable, if usually short-lived, reduction in subsequent maximal force and rate of force development A. The mechanism is debated (reduced muscle stiffness and altered neural drive both contribute) but the effect itself is one of the more consistently replicated findings in exercise science.
  • Short static holds — under about 30 seconds per muscle — have minimal effect on subsequent power, sprinting or jumping in most studies B. A quick 10–15 second hold on one tight spot is not the intervention the "never stretch before sport" rule was built to stop.
  • Dynamic stretching — moving a joint through range under control, repeatedly, rather than holding it — does not impair subsequent performance and in a meaningful number of studies improves it, likely through the raised temperature and rehearsed coordination described in §40.1 A.
Behm DG, Chaouachi A. A review of the acute effects of static and dynamic stretching on performance. European Journal of Applied Physiology 2011;111(11):2633–51.
Who

A synthesis of dozens of acute laboratory trials testing static and dynamic stretching protocols against strength, power, sprint and jump outcomes.

What

Compared static holds of varying duration with dynamic stretching and with no stretching, measured immediately before performance tasks.

Found

Static holds beyond roughly 60 seconds per muscle reliably reduced force and rate of force development; holds under about 30 seconds had little effect; dynamic stretching did not impair performance and often improved it, particularly when it raised temperature and rehearsed the movement.

Why it matters: it reframed the pre-performance warm-up question from "stretch or not" to "which kind, and for how long" — the distinction most locker-room advice still misses. Limitation: it synthesises heterogeneous lab protocols, many using isolated-joint strength tests rather than sport-specific tasks, and effect sizes vary considerably by muscle group, training status and exact hold duration.

“Stretching before I run prevents injury”

So a few minutes of static stretching is a sensible insurance policy before any session.

Not shown, and it can cost you power

Systematic reviews have not found that pre-exercise static stretching reduces injury risk in running or team-sport populations B. What it reliably does, at long hold durations, is transiently reduce the force and rate of force development you are about to need. The practical conclusion is about timing, not a ban: move the long holds to after training, where they have a real job to do (§40.4), and use dynamic movement to prepare for performance.

Where Static Stretching Does Belong

None of the above is an argument against static stretching. It is an argument about when. Held stretches, done after training or as a session in their own right, genuinely increase the range of motion you can reach over weeks of repetition A. The mechanism is worth stating plainly, because it is routinely misunderstood: the muscle-tendon unit itself barely changes length. What changes is stretch tolerance — your nervous system's willingness to let you reach further into a stretch before it calls a halt, mediated by reduced protective reflex activity and a shift in the point at which the stretch feels uncomfortable B. A small amount of genuine tissue adaptation in the tendon and the muscle-tendon junction may also contribute, but tolerance is doing most of the work.

This matters practically. If a hamstring stretch "worked" because your nervous system now tolerates more range, that adaptation needs repetition to maintain — which is why range of motion gained over a stretching block erodes within weeks of stopping, not because tissue "shortens back up" but because tolerance resets. It also means the goal of a post-training stretch session is consistency over months, not intensity in any one session.

Post-Activation Potentiation — Borrowing From The Next Rep

The last phase of RAMP, Potentiate, exploits a genuine and useful quirk of muscle physiology: a heavy or explosive effort can transiently raise the force and power available in the effort that follows it. This is post-activation potentiation (PAP), and it is the physiological reason a sprinter's last warm-up rep looks nothing like their first.

The mechanism runs through several overlapping routes: phosphorylation of the myosin regulatory light chain makes the contractile machinery more sensitive to calcium, higher-threshold motor units that were not previously active get recruited, and reflex excitability rises. All three make the next maximal effort marginally faster and more forceful — for a window that is real but brief, and that coexists with fatigue from the same conditioning effort that caused it.

That coexistence is the whole practical problem. Immediately after a heavy set, you are both potentiated and fatigued, and which effect dominates depends on how much rest you give it. Reviews of the literature describe an optimal window of roughly 3–12 minutes after a near-maximal conditioning activity, with large individual variation — some athletes potentiate fast and fade fast, others need longer for fatigue to clear before the potentiation shows up B. There is no single number that works for everyone; the only way to find yours is to test it.

Using post-activation potentiation in the final minutes of a warm-up, for a session that demands maximal speed, power or a heavy single.

  • Do A heavy near-maximal lift (e.g. 3 reps at 85–90% of what you will lift that day) or a ballistic effort (depth jumps, heavy sled push, bounding) that resembles the pattern you are about to perform.
  • Load Heavy enough to be a genuine near-maximal effort — light "activation" sets do not potentiate anything.
  • Dose 1–3 reps, one or two sets. This is a trigger, not a workout; doing more just adds fatigue.
  • When Rest 3–12 minutes before the maximal attempt or race effort you are preparing for, and use a practice session to find where you personally peak in that window.
  • On the bike A short, hard standing effort of 10–15 seconds, 5–8 minutes before a sprint finish or a maximal test effort, follows the same logic as a heavy lift.
  • Signal You're doing it right when the rehearsal rep or effort after the rest window feels crisper and faster than the one before the heavy trigger — not when you feel merely warmer.
B The phenomenon is well replicated; the optimal dose and rest window are highly individual and not reducible to one number.

Building The Ramp For Each Session

RAMP is a structure, not a fixed script — the content of each phase should match what the session is about to ask of you.

Running

An easy run needs almost nothing beyond its own first few minutes: easy running is itself the Raise phase, so walk or jog the first 5 minutes slower than target and let pace arrive naturally. An interval session or race needs the full sequence: 8–10 minutes of easy jogging, 2–3 minutes of mobility and activation drills, then 3–4 short accelerations building to close to race pace in the final minutes — rehearsing the exact speed the body is about to be asked to hold.

Lifting

The biggest warm-up mistake in a weights session is substituting drills for load. Five minutes of general raising and mobilising is enough; after that, the most effective warm-up for a heavy squat is a progressively heavier empty-bar-to-working-weight ramp — roughly 50%, 70%, 85% of the day's top set, for diminishing reps — not five more stretches. Each ramped set rehearses the exact bar path and bracing pattern the heavy set will need, which no amount of generic mobility work can substitute for.

Cycling

Indoors, a warm-up needs deliberate attention that outdoor riding gets for free. Start the fan before you need it — indoor riding produces far more sweat and heat stress than the equivalent outdoor effort because there is no airflow from motion, and an under-cooled rider fatigues faster regardless of fitness. Keep pedalling throughout: coasting on a stationary trainer does nothing, so treat every minute as active. Build over 10–15 minutes from very easy to just under threshold, with two or three 20–30 second fast-cadence efforts in the final few minutes if the session includes high-intensity work. See chapter 44 for full indoor-cycling session structures.

Cold or early morning

Cold tissue and a body that has been horizontal for eight hours both need a longer Raise phase — extend it to 8–10 minutes rather than 3–5, dress to retain heat until the last possible layer removal, and accept that the first few minutes of any cold-weather or early-morning session will feel heavier regardless of fitness. That feeling is accurate information, not a sign you are unfit.

Foam Rolling and Self-Massage

Foam rolling has acquired a vocabulary — "releasing," "breaking up adhesions," "unsticking fascia" — that outruns what pressure from a foam cylinder can plausibly do to dense connective tissue. The fascial sheets described in chapter 1 have tensile strength measured in hundreds of newtons; the sustained force needed to meaningfully and permanently deform that tissue is far beyond anything body weight on a roller generates B.

What rolling plausibly does is simpler and still worth having: a short-term increase in range of motion and a short-term reduction in the feeling of muscle soreness, most likely through altered pain perception and reduced protective muscle tone rather than any change in tissue structure B. Neither effect is adaptive — there is no evidence it changes tissue over time, reduces injury rate, or speeds the biological repair process that actually resolves delayed-onset muscle soreness (chapter 36).

“Foam rolling releases fascia and breaks up knots”

So fifteen minutes of aggressive rolling before and after every session is doing real tissue work.

It's a comfort tool, not a tissue treatment

There is no evidence that foam rolling changes the structure, length or stiffness of fascia. The short-term relief is real and worth using — before a session if it helps you feel ready, after one if it helps the soreness feel more bearable — but it belongs in the same category as a hot shower: a comfort measure, not a treatment, and not a reason to skip the loading that actually drives adaptation.

Used that way — a few minutes, where it feels useful, with no expectation of a lasting mechanical change — it is harmless and sometimes pleasant. Used as a substitute for the progressive strengthening in chapter 41, or rolled directly and aggressively onto an already irritated, compressed structure such as the iliotibial band, it can do more harm than good (chapter 43).

The Cool-Down

A cool-down — several minutes of easy movement after the main session, rather than stopping dead — does a short list of genuine things and is often credited with a longer list of things it does not do.

What it plausibly does: it eases the transition of heart rate and blood pressure back toward resting levels rather than dropping them abruptly, which matters most after very hard efforts where blood has been pooling in working limbs; it gives a structured few minutes to begin rehydrating and mentally close out the session; and continuing to move lightly can clear accumulated blood lactate somewhat faster than sitting still does, although lactate itself is a fuel, not the cause of next-day soreness, and clearing it faster has not been shown to change how you feel tomorrow C.

What it reliably does not do is prevent delayed-onset muscle soreness. Systematic reviews of cool-down protocols find no meaningful reduction in next-day soreness compared with simply stopping A. DOMS is driven by unfamiliar mechanical and eccentric loading and the repair process that follows it (chapter 36); a few minutes of easy jogging afterwards does not touch that process. If a cool-down feels good, do it for the parts that are real — the gentler return to rest, the ritual of closing out hard work — and stop expecting it to be an insurance policy against how your legs will feel tomorrow.

Five things to carry forward

  1. A warm-up does four measurable things — raises muscle temperature, speeds nerve conduction, thins synovial fluid and shrinks the early oxygen deficit — and all four matter more as the session gets faster.
  2. RAMP (Raise, Activate, Mobilise, Potentiate) sequences those effects sensibly in roughly 8–12 minutes, finishing with a rehearsal of the exact task ahead rather than more generic drills.
  3. Long static holds (over about 60 seconds per muscle) transiently reduce force and power and belong after training, not before it; short holds and dynamic stretching do not have this cost.
  4. Static stretching genuinely increases range of motion over weeks, mainly by raising stretch tolerance rather than lengthening the muscle itself — which is why it needs to be kept up, not performed once.
  5. Foam rolling and cool-downs both offer real short-term comfort and no adaptive or protective benefit — useful as comfort tools, not as substitutes for progressive loading or as insurance against next-day soreness.
