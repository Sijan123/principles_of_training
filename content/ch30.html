A lean adult carries roughly 80,000–100,000 kilocalories of fat on board, even at a modest body fat percentage — enough, in principle, to run for days without eating a single gram of food. Carbohydrate, by contrast, runs out in a few hours of hard effort. And yet elite endurance athletes do not run on fat; they spend their whole careers trying to use carbohydrate as efficiently as possible, calling on fat mainly to make that limited carbohydrate last longer. The reason is not that fat is a poor fuel — it is that fat is a slow one, and in a sport decided by how fast you can turn fuel into force, slow is sometimes the whole problem.

Fat As Fuel — Enormous Tank, Narrow Pipe

Fat is stored in two places that matter for exercise. The overwhelming majority sits in adipose tissue — dedicated fat cells distributed under the skin and around organs — which is energy-dense (about 9 kcal/g, more than double carbohydrate or protein's 4 kcal/g) and, for practical purposes, limitless as a fuel reserve even in lean athletes. A much smaller pool, intramuscular triglyceride (IMTG), is stored as fat droplets inside the muscle fibres themselves, close to the mitochondria that will burn it, and this local reserve is what actually fuels a chunk of steady, moderate-intensity exercise directly, replenished over hours and days between sessions.

The limitation is not supply, it is throughput. Using fat as fuel requires breaking triglyceride apart, transporting free fatty acids into the mitochondrion via a carrier system (carnitine palmitoyltransferase, met in chapter 8), and oxidising it through a longer biochemical pathway than carbohydrate requires. Each of those steps takes time and oxygen. Carbohydrate can be broken down anaerobically, in a hurry, when oxygen delivery can't keep pace; fat essentially cannot. That single constraint is why fat oxidation has a ceiling that shows up as a hard intensity limit, covered next, while carbohydrate oxidation can keep rising as intensity climbs toward maximal effort.

Think of adipose tissue as an oil tanker moored offshore, and intramuscular triglyceride as a small drum kept in the generator shed.

The tanker holds enough fuel to run the generator for weeks, but it can only unload through a narrow pipe at a fixed maximum flow rate — no amount of oil waiting offshore makes the pipe wider. The drum in the shed empties faster and refills between shifts. Carbohydrate, in this picture, is more like a tank of pre-mixed, instantly combustible fuel kept right next to the engine: smaller, but it can be burned at any rate the engine demands, including flat out. When the generator needs its absolute maximum output, it has to run almost entirely on the tank beside it, however much oil is sitting offshore.

FATmax And The Crossover Concept

Across a gradient from an easy walk to an all-out sprint, the proportion of energy coming from fat versus carbohydrate shifts continuously, and that shift can be measured directly from the ratio of carbon dioxide produced to oxygen consumed as someone breathes into a mask on a treadmill or bike. At very low intensities, fat supplies the majority of energy. As intensity rises, carbohydrate's share grows, and at some point the two lines cross — the crossover point — beyond which carbohydrate dominates and keeps climbing toward 100% of the energy mix at or near maximal effort.

The intensity at which the absolute rate of fat oxidation peaks, rather than its percentage share, is called FATmax, and it typically sits in the range of 0.3–0.6 g of fat per minute in untrained-to-recreationally-trained individuals, usually somewhere around 45–65% of VO2max B. Well-trained endurance athletes shift this curve up and to the right: higher peak fat-oxidation rates, occurring at a higher relative intensity, with some elite, highly fat-adapted endurance athletes measured oxidising fat at rates above 1.5 g/min — two to three times the typical FATmax of an untrained person C for the upper end of that range, since values this high are reported in small, highly selected samples rather than established as a routine training outcome.

Why Trained Athletes Burn More Fat — And What It Buys Them

Endurance training raises fat-oxidation capacity through several compounding adaptations covered more fully in chapter 8: more mitochondria, more of the specific enzymes and carrier proteins that shuttle fatty acids into them, more capillaries delivering the oxygen fat oxidation depends on, and better-regulated hormonal control of fat release from adipose tissue during exercise. The payoff is glycogen sparing: at any given sub-threshold pace, a trained athlete is pulling a larger fraction of the required energy from the large, slow fat tank and a smaller fraction from the small, fast carbohydrate tank, which means the same pace can be held for longer before carbohydrate stores described in chapter 28 run critically low.

Niamh, 41, trains for ultramarathons, holds a steady aid-station-to-aid-station pace at an intensity where fat oxidation is the dominant fuel. A focused training block raises her fat oxidation at that pace by 0.2 g/min — a realistic, measured improvement from several months of consistent aerobic volume.

So: a 0.2 g/min improvement in fat oxidation at race pace roughly doubles how long Niamh's muscle glycogen alone would last at that pace — which is exactly why ultra-endurance training emphasises fat-oxidation capacity even though the race is won on pace, not on burning fat for its own sake.

FATmax is not a fat-loss intensity, despite how often it is marketed that way. Training at the pace where the percentage of fat burned is highest does not mean more total body fat is lost over a day or a week — fat loss is decided by total energy balance (calories in versus calories out) over days and weeks, not by which fuel happened to be burned during one session. A short, hard session burning mostly carbohydrate can still produce a larger total calorie deficit, and therefore more fat loss over time, than a longer session spent carefully pacing inside the FATmax zone. Use FATmax training to build endurance-fuelling capacity, not as a weight-loss shortcut.

Essential Fatty Acids — Omega-3, Omega-6, And What The Evidence Actually Supports

Omega-3 and omega-6 fatty acids are called essential because the body cannot build them from scratch; they must come from food. The two long-chain omega-3s most relevant to training — EPA and DHA, found mainly in oily fish and algae — are incorporated into cell membranes throughout the body, including muscle, where membrane composition influences how cells respond to insulin and to the mechanical and metabolic signals that drive adaptation.

The evidence for omega-3 supplementation in athletes is genuinely interesting but not yet strong enough to oversell. Several trials report that higher omega-3 status is associated with a greater muscle protein synthesis response to a given protein dose — essentially sensitising the leucine threshold discussed in chapter 29 C. Other trials report modestly reduced markers of exercise-induced inflammation and muscle soreness with omega-3 supplementation C, and a smaller, less consistent body of work suggests possible benefits for recovery of muscle function after damaging exercise B for the general anti-inflammatory direction, C for whether it reliably changes performance outcomes. None of this amounts to a performance-enhancing supplement in the way creatine does; it is a plausible, modest, food-first recommendation.

The Ketogenic Diet And Performance — The Honest Picture

A ketogenic diet restricts carbohydrate severely enough (typically under 50 g/day) that the liver begins producing ketone bodies from fat as an alternative fuel, and after several weeks of adaptation, fat oxidation rises dramatically — well beyond even the elite endurance values discussed above. That part of the keto story is well established and genuinely impressive as a metabolic shift A.

What happens to performance on top of that shift is where the picture gets honest and complicated. The clearest, most carefully controlled test came from elite race walkers (this chapter's paper, below): keto adaptation sharply raised fat oxidation and modestly raised peak aerobic capacity, but it also downregulated pyruvate dehydrogenase — the enzyme that feeds carbohydrate into the mitochondrion — which blunted the athletes' ability to use carbohydrate even when it was available, impaired their exercise economy (more oxygen needed to hold the same race-relevant speed), and left high-intensity performance measurably worse than athletes on an equally well-controlled high-carbohydrate or carbohydrate-periodised diet B. Several other trials across different endurance disciplines report a similar pattern: good news for low-intensity fat oxidation, bad news for anything approaching race pace.

This is not a unanimous verdict in the field, and a fair account says so. Researchers associated with the earlier low-carbohydrate performance literature — notably Volek and Phinney — have argued that longer adaptation periods (months, not weeks), different training approaches, and selecting athletes who respond well to fat-adaptation may produce better outcomes than the race-walker trials show, and have published case series and smaller studies of ultra-endurance athletes performing well on long-term low-carbohydrate diets at genuinely low, steady intensities C. The disagreement has not been resolved by a single decisive trial; what both sides agree on is that keto's cost is concentrated at higher, race-relevant intensities, and its case is strongest at low, sustained intensities over very long durations — exactly where carbohydrate is least needed in the first place.

“Fat-adapted athletes have unlocked an unlimited fuel tank and don't bonk”

The appeal of keto for ultra-endurance sport, built on the genuinely enormous size of the fat tank.

Size of the tank was never the limiting factor — speed of delivery is

Keto-adapted athletes do avoid the specific bonk caused by carbohydrate depletion, because they are barely using carbohydrate. But the same downregulated carbohydrate machinery that prevents that bonk also caps how hard they can push: race-relevant speed and the ability to respond to a surge both suffer. It trades one failure mode for a lower ceiling, not for no ceiling.

Who might it nonetheless suit? Ultra-endurance athletes racing at low, steady intensities for many hours or days, where gut tolerance for carbohydrate at high rates is itself a limiting factor, or athletes with specific metabolic conditions managed under clinical supervision, are the main groups where the trade-off plausibly favours keto. It is a poor fit for anything involving repeated high-intensity efforts, sprint finishes, or races decided at or above threshold pace — which covers the large majority of competitive endurance sport.

Dietary Fat Intake — The Floor That Protects Hormones And Vitamins

Outside specific ketogenic contexts, there is no strong performance case for either a very high or a very low fat intake; the practical target for most athletes is roughly 20–35% of total energy intake, with the remainder split between the carbohydrate and protein targets set in the previous two chapters B. What matters more than hitting an exact percentage is not going too low for too long.

Dietary fat is not just energy; it is the raw material for steroid hormone production (including testosterone and oestrogen) and the vehicle for absorbing the fat-soluble vitamins A, D, E and K. Chronically restricting fat intake below roughly 15–20% of total energy, a pattern that shows up in some aggressive cutting diets, is associated with reduced testosterone in men and menstrual disruption in women, alongside reduced absorption of exactly the vitamins the rest of this book depends on for bone and immune health (chapter 31) B. A fat intake that is simply “adequate” protects this floor without needing to be high.

The Elite Test Case — What Happens When You Push Fat Oxidation To Its Limit

Niamh's 0.2 g/min improvement above is a realistic, modest training outcome. The ketogenic diet pushes the same variable — fat oxidation at a given pace — far further, which makes it a useful stress test of the whole chapter's argument: if more fat oxidation were simply, unconditionally good for endurance performance, pushing it to its maximum should produce the best performance of all. The most rigorous attempt to test that directly used elite athletes, tightly controlled diets, and race-relevant outcomes rather than lab markers alone.

Burke LM, Ross ML, Garvican-Lewis LA, et al. Low carbohydrate, high fat diet impairs exercise economy and negates the performance benefit from intensified training in elite race walkers. J Physiol 2017;595(9):2785–2807.
Who

Elite-level race walkers, world-class and Olympic-calibre athletes, studied over a 3-week intensified training and competition block.

What

Randomised to a high-carbohydrate diet, a periodised-carbohydrate diet, or a ketogenic low-carbohydrate high-fat diet, with race-walking economy, VO2peak and 10 km race performance measured before and after.

Found

The ketogenic group markedly increased fat oxidation and modestly raised VO2peak, but exercise economy worsened (more oxygen cost at race pace) and 10 km race-walk performance did not improve, while both carbohydrate-fed groups improved 10 km time by roughly 5–7%.

Why it matters: this is the most rigorous head-to-head test of ketogenic adaptation in genuinely elite endurance athletes, with race-relevant outcomes rather than only lab markers, and a since-published replication (“Crisis of confidence averted,” 2020) reproduced the core finding. Limitation: race walking is a highly technique-dependent, economy-sensitive discipline, and critics (including Lindseth et al., 2017) have questioned whether the training load was fully matched across groups — the debate over generalisability to other endurance disciplines and longer adaptation periods, outlined above, remains open.

Five things to carry forward

  1. Fat is energy-dense and its total store is effectively limitless even in lean athletes, but oxidising it is inherently slower than oxidising carbohydrate, which is why it cannot fully substitute for carbohydrate at high intensity.
  2. FATmax — the intensity of peak fat-oxidation rate, typically 0.3–0.6 g/min and higher in trained athletes — is a training-adaptation target, not a fat-loss intensity; total energy balance over days and weeks decides fat loss, not which fuel a single session burned.
  3. Training raises fat-oxidation capacity through more mitochondria, enzymes and capillaries, and the direct payoff is glycogen sparing — the same pace drains the smaller carbohydrate tank more slowly.
  4. Ketogenic adaptation genuinely raises fat oxidation but downregulates carbohydrate metabolism, impairs exercise economy and blunts high-intensity performance in the best-controlled elite trials; it suits a narrow band of very low, very long, steady-state endurance contexts and does not suit anything decided at or above threshold pace.
  5. Dietary fat below roughly 15–20% of total energy risks hormone production and fat-soluble vitamin absorption, so fat intake should sit at a sufficient floor (20–35% of energy) even during an aggressive cut.
