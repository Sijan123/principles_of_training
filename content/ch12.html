A single hard training session can nearly double circulating growth hormone and triple cortisol within half an hour — and tracking those exact spikes across a large group of lifters for three months tells you almost nothing about who will grow the most muscle. That gap between a dramatic, easily-measured acute response and the slow, invisible process it supposedly drives is the central lesson of this chapter. The real story of how a session becomes an adaptation runs mostly through local, inside-the-cell signalling that never shows up on a blood test at all.

Endocrine Basics — Gland, Hormone, Receptor

The endocrine system is the body's chemical messaging network. A gland — the adrenal glands, the pituitary, the testes and ovaries, among others — releases a hormone into the bloodstream, where it travels everywhere, reaching essentially every cell in the body. But a hormone only has an effect on a cell that has the matching receptor for it, a specific protein, usually on or inside the cell, shaped to bind that hormone the way a key fits a lock. No receptor, no effect, no matter how much hormone is circulating.

This single fact resolves most confusion about "boosting hormones" through training or supplements. The amount of a hormone circulating in blood is only half the story; the other half is receptor density (how many locks a cell has) and receptor sensitivity (how readily each lock turns once the key is in it). A tissue with few receptors, or desensitised receptors, will barely respond even to a large hormonal surge, while a tissue with abundant, sensitive receptors can respond strongly to a comparatively modest, steady level. Training changes receptor density and sensitivity over weeks; it changes acute circulating hormone levels over minutes. Confusing the two is the root of a great deal of marketing built around this system.

Think of a hormone as a radio broadcast and a receptor as a tuned-in radio.

Turning the broadcast volume up (more hormone in the blood) does nothing for a room with no radio in it, or a radio that's switched off. What actually matters for that room is whether it has a radio, and whether that radio is switched on and tuned to the right station. Training mostly works by installing more radios and keeping them tuned — not by shouting louder into the airwaves.

The Acute Hormonal Response To Exercise — And Why It Misleads

A hard training session reliably produces a predictable acute hormonal pattern. Catecholamines (adrenaline and noradrenaline) rise within seconds, mobilising fuel and raising heart rate and force output. Cortisol, often mislabelled purely a "stress hormone," rises over the course of a session and helps mobilise glucose and fatty acids for fuel — a normal, necessary part of meeting an energy demand, not evidence of damage. Growth hormone (GH) rises sharply after high-intensity or high-lactate work. Testosterone rises modestly, more so after large-muscle-mass compound lifting than isolation work.

For decades, the working assumption in strength and conditioning was that bigger acute spikes in GH and testosterone after a session meant more muscle growth down the line — which gave rise to training advice explicitly built around "maximising the hormonal response": short rests, high volume, specific set-and-rep schemes marketed as boosting these spikes. Careful longitudinal research has since dismantled that assumption. Studies that measured acute post-exercise hormone spikes and then tracked the same individuals' actual muscle growth over 12 or more weeks of training found little to no correlation between the size of someone's acute GH, testosterone or cortisol response and how much muscle or strength they gained B. Some of the same work found that intramuscular signals — markers of the actual local signalling machinery discussed in the next section — correlated far better with real hypertrophy than anything measured in blood.

“Chase the GH spike” / “this is a testosterone-boosting workout”

Set-and-rest schemes marketed specifically to maximise acute hormone spikes, on the theory that a bigger spike means more growth.

The spike is real; its growth relevance is not

The acute rises themselves genuinely happen and are easy to measure, which is exactly why they became a marketing hook. But controlled studies find the size of an individual's spike does not predict their long-term hypertrophy or strength gains B. Acute increases in testosterone, GH and IGF-1 after a session are neither necessary nor sufficient to drive muscle growth on their own.

“Cortisol is the enemy — keep sessions short to avoid it”

Long or hard sessions are sometimes avoided on the grounds that they will trigger "catabolic" cortisol and eat into muscle.

Cortisol is a normal fuel-mobilising response, not tissue destruction

Cortisol rising during and after a hard session is the same mechanism as it rising during any other physical demand — it liberates glucose and fatty acids for use. It is chronically, persistently elevated cortisol — from under-recovery, under-eating or severe life stress sustained over weeks — that is genuinely problematic, covered in chapter 36. An acute, hour-long rise after a good session is not that, and avoiding productive training purely to dodge a normal acute cortisol rise sacrifices real stimulus for an imagined risk.

The Signalling Pathways That Actually Matter

If circulating hormones are not where the real decision gets made, where is it made? Mostly inside the muscle fibre itself, through two molecular switches that detect completely different kinds of stress and point the cell in opposite directions.

  1. mTORC1 — the build signal

    mTORC1 (mechanistic target of rapamycin complex 1) is a protein complex that, when active, switches on muscle protein synthesis — the machinery that builds new contractile protein. It is activated by three things acting together: mechanical tension on the muscle fibre (heavy or fatiguing loading), the amino acid leucine arriving from protein in the diet, and insulin signalling that nutrients are available. All three need to be present for a strong response; mechanical tension without amino acids available to build with is a signal with no raw material to act on.

  2. AMPK — the energy-alarm signal

    AMPK (AMP-activated protein kinase) is activated when a cell's energy reserves are falling — specifically, when the ratio of AMP to ATP rises, which happens during sustained, demanding aerobic effort. Once active, AMPK's job is to conserve and generate energy: it promotes glucose uptake and fat oxidation and, importantly, drives PGC-1α, a master regulatory protein that switches on the genes for building new mitochondria (see chapter 8).

Both pathways are genuinely good news individually — mTORC1 builds the engine's size, AMPK via PGC-1α builds the engine's efficiency. The problem is timing, and it is the single most practically important fact in this chapter: AMPK activation decays within roughly three hours of a demanding aerobic session, while mTORC1 activation, once triggered by a resistance session, stays elevated for 18–24 hours B. Active AMPK has been shown to directly suppress mTORC1 signalling. So if a hard aerobic session is placed shortly before a resistance session, AMPK is still elevated and can blunt the mTORC1 response the lifting would otherwise produce; if the order is reversed, or enough time separates the two, the interference is much smaller. This asymmetric decay — one fast-fading, one long-lasting — is the entire molecular explanation for why concurrent training (doing both endurance and strength work) can blunt hypertrophy and strength gains, and why session order and spacing matter far more than most lifters assume. It is explored in full, with a practical scheduling framework, in chapter 20.

Insulin And The Fed/Fasted Switch

Insulin, released from the pancreas in response to rising blood glucose after eating, is not just a blood-sugar hormone in this context — it is one of the three inputs mTORC1 needs, and it independently suppresses protein breakdown, the ongoing process that is always removing some muscle protein alongside the synthesis that is always building it. Net muscle growth is the balance of synthesis minus breakdown, not synthesis alone. Training in a fasted state does not prevent muscle protein synthesis from rising — the mechanical-tension trigger still fires — but insulin being low means breakdown is not being suppressed as effectively, so the net balance is less favourable than the same session performed fed, all else equal. This is a modest, not dramatic, effect, and it is one of several reasons chapter 27 and chapter 29 recommend protein availability around training rather than training fasted as a default strategy.

Myokines — Muscle As An Endocrine Organ

Muscle is not merely a target of hormones; contracting muscle releases its own signalling molecules, called myokines, directly into the bloodstream — making skeletal muscle itself an endocrine gland in its own right. Three are particularly well characterised. Interleukin-6 (IL-6), released from contracting muscle, has wide-ranging anti-inflammatory and metabolic effects, including improved insulin sensitivity and fat mobilisation — a very different role from the pro-inflammatory IL-6 associated with chronic disease, illustrating that context (acute, exercise-induced versus chronic, disease-driven) changes what the same molecule does. Irisin, released particularly after endurance exercise, is implicated in prompting fat tissue to behave somewhat more like metabolically active brown fat. Brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF), released in response to exercise, supports neuron survival and growth, including in the hippocampus, and is one of the more credible mechanistic links between regular exercise and improved mood and cognitive function.

Myokines are, in a real sense, the mechanistic heart of the phrase "exercise is medicine." They explain how a muscle contraction in the leg can plausibly influence inflammation, insulin sensitivity and even brain function throughout the body — effects that used to be described only statistically, through population health data, and can now be traced to specific molecules with specific, identifiable actions.

What This Actually Means In Practice

Pulling the chapter together into a practical rule: you cannot usefully manipulate circulating hormones with clever set-and-rest schemes — the acute spikes you can engineer that way do not reliably translate into more adaptation, as the West & Phillips findings above show directly. What you can do, because the mechanisms above are real and timing-sensitive, is manage two things that are fully within your control: nutrition timing relative to training, and session order and spacing when combining different training types in the same day or week.

Finn, 27, lifter, trains at 6pm, wants to understand his own 24-hour signalling window after a heavy leg session.

If Finn had planned a hard 45-minute run, he schedules it the next afternoon, roughly 20+ hours after the lifting session, rather than that same evening or the next morning — letting the mTORC1 window run its full course with minimal AMPK interference, per the concurrent-training mechanism above.t ≥ 20 h: aerobic session placed outside the highest-interference zone
So: Finn's three evenly-spread protein feedings each land inside, or close to, the roughly 18–24 hour mTORC1 window his session opened, and pushing the run to the next afternoon keeps the fast-decaying AMPK signal from a same-day run away from the long-lived mTORC1 signal almost entirely.

Structuring nutrition and session order around the real signalling windows, rather than trying to manipulate hormone levels directly.

  • Do Protein-containing meals spaced through the 24 hours after a resistance session, rather than one large "post-workout" dose.
  • Load ~0.3–0.4 g/kg protein per feeding, each containing enough leucine to clear the per-meal synthesis threshold (chapter 29).
  • Dose 3–4 protein feedings across the day, roughly 3–5 hours apart.
  • When If combining with aerobic work, separate sessions by at least 6–24 hours where possible, or do the resistance session first if both must happen the same day.
  • On the bike If a hard bike session and a lifting session must share a day, ride first-thing and lift in the evening (or vice versa with the longest practical gap), rather than stacking an interval session immediately before or after heavy lower-body lifting.
  • Signal You're doing it right when strength and endurance both progress across a training block, rather than one visibly stalling while the other improves.
B The molecular mechanism and its timing are well established; the optimal practical gap for a given individual and session type is still being refined in the literature (see chapter 20).

Five things to carry forward

  1. A hormone's effect depends on receptor density and sensitivity in the target tissue, not just how much of it is circulating — which is why "boosting" a hormone acutely often changes little.
  2. Acute post-exercise spikes in growth hormone, testosterone and cortisol correlate poorly with long-term hypertrophy; cortisol's acute rise is a normal fuel-mobilising response, not tissue damage.
  3. mTORC1 (driven by mechanical tension, leucine and insulin) switches on muscle building and stays active for roughly 18–24 hours; AMPK (driven by energy stress) switches on fuel conservation and mitochondrial building but fades within about 3 hours.
  4. That asymmetric timing is the molecular reason concurrent endurance-and-strength training can blunt gains when sessions are poorly ordered or spaced, and the reason it usually doesn't when they aren't.
  5. Muscle itself is an endocrine organ via myokines such as IL-6, irisin and BDNF, which is the real mechanistic basis for "exercise is medicine" claims about inflammation, metabolism and brain health.
