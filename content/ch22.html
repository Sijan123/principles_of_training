Pick up any textbook of human muscles and you will find roughly 650 of them named, measured and drawn. You will train perhaps thirty on purpose. This chapter and the four that follow are a lookup atlas for those thirty — not a tour of anatomy for its own sake, but a working manual that answers one question for each muscle: what, exactly, makes it bigger, stronger or more resilient, and why. Part IV is built to be read out of order. Jump to the muscle you need.

How To Read This Atlas

Every entry in these five chapters answers the same six questions, because those six questions are what actually changes a programme. Where does the muscle attach, in plain words — which tells you what movement it can produce. What does it do when it shortens. What is its rough mix of fibre types — slow (type I, fatigue-resistant, low force) versus fast (type II, powerful, fatigues quickly) — covered properly in chapter 5. Where is it at long length: the joint position in which the muscle is most stretched. Which two to four exercises load it best, and why. And how does it typically fail or get hurt, plus the one habit that most often causes that.

The long-length question deserves its own paragraph, because it is the single most useful fact in this atlas and the one most programmes ignore. A muscle generates tension across a range of lengths, and the growth signal from mechanical tension — the stretch load inside the fibre, detailed in chapter 17 — is larger when that tension is applied at long muscle length than at short. This is not a minor refinement. Trials that compare training at long versus short length for the same muscle routinely find a meaningfully larger size gain from the long-length condition, sometimes close to double. Every entry below tells you the joint position that puts the muscle at long length, because that position is where you should be adding load, range or time under tension if growth is the goal.

Think of a muscle as a luggage strap wound around a suitcase. Pull the strap when it is already nearly at full stretch and you feel the tension everywhere along its length — the buckle, the middle, the far end. Pull it when it is already bunched up short and slack, and most of what you feel is at the buckle; the rest of the strap barely notices. Training at long muscle length is pulling the strap near full stretch. It is not a stretching exercise — the muscle is still working hard, often harder, because now every part of it is genuinely under load rather than resting bunched up.

The Quadriceps

The quadriceps are four muscles sharing one tendon: vastus lateralis, vastus medialis and vastus intermedius run from the upper thigh bone to the kneecap and do one job — straighten the knee. The fourth, rectus femoris, is the odd one out. It starts higher, on the pelvis, and crosses two joints: it straightens the knee and flexes the hip.

That second attachment matters more than it sounds. A muscle that crosses two joints can only reach long length when both joints cooperate. Rectus femoris is stretched by knee flexion and by hip extension at the same time. A back squat flexes the knee deeply but also flexes the hip — the two cancel out, so rectus femoris barely lengthens at the bottom of a squat, however deep you go. The vastii, which only cross the knee, do get a long-length stimulus from a deep squat; rectus femoris does not. To load rectus femoris at long length you need knee flexion with the hip extended — a lunge with a long stride, a leg extension performed with the torso reclined, or a reverse Nordic curl, where the hips stay straight while the knees bend under control.

The Hamstrings — Two Jobs, Not One

The hamstrings — biceps femoris, semitendinosus and semimembranosus, running from the sitting bone to the shin — cross both the hip and the knee, which means they do two unrelated-looking jobs: they extend the hip (drive the leg backward, as in the finish of a sprint stride or a deadlift) and they flex the knee (curl the heel toward the buttock). A muscle that does both is only at long length when the hip is flexed and the knee is straight at the same time — the position at the bottom of a Romanian deadlift, or at full stretch in a Nordic curl. A seated leg curl trains knee flexion with the hip already flexed and the thigh supported; a Romanian deadlift trains hip extension with the knee nearly straight. Neither alone covers both functions, which is why the two most common hamstring prescriptions — "just deadlift" and "just curl" — each leave half the muscle's job untrained.

This also explains where hamstrings get hurt. The classic sprinting strain happens in late swing phase, in the last instant before footstrike, when the hamstring is lengthening at the hip while contracting hard to decelerate the shin — an eccentric contraction at long length, the exact loading pattern a Nordic curl rehearses deliberately and safely. Trials of the Nordic curl in field-sport squads have found large reductions in hamstring injury incidence, among the stronger findings in all of sports medicine A.

Training both hamstring functions, at the length where sprinting actually injures the muscle.

  • Do Nordic hamstring curl (knee flexion, long length) paired with a Romanian deadlift or single-leg RDL (hip extension, long length).
  • Load Nordic curl: bodyweight, controlled by arms on the way down. RDL: a load that allows a full, controlled stretch without the lower back rounding.
  • Dose Nordic curl 2–3 sets of 3–6 reps; RDL 3 sets of 6–10 reps per leg. Twice a week covers both.
  • When Away from hard sprint or long-run days — the soreness from a first Nordic session is severe and genuine, not decorative.
  • Progress Nordic curl: slow the lowering phase before adding reps. RDL: add load once the stretch feels controlled through the full range.
  • On the bike A trainer loads the quadriceps and gluteus maximus concentrically with no impact, which makes it a useful supplement — low-cadence torque intervals (4 × 8 min at 85% FTP, 50–60 rpm) raise pedal force and recruit higher-threshold units. It will not load the hamstrings eccentrically or the calf at long length, so it substitutes for none of the exercises above.
  • Signal You're doing it right when the Nordic curl's hardest point shifts from near the top of the movement toward the bottom as eccentric strength improves.

Gluteus Maximus, Medius & Minimus

Gluteus maximus is the largest muscle in the human body, running from the back of the pelvis to the outer thigh bone, and its job is hip extension — driving the thigh backward, the motion of standing up from a squat or finishing a sprint stride. Smaller and deeper, gluteus medius and minimus run from the side of the pelvis to the top of the thigh bone and do a quieter but constant job: on every single stride, while one foot is on the ground, they stop the pelvis tipping sideways toward the unsupported leg. Weakness here shows up as visible hip drop and is linked to patellofemoral pain and iliotibial band syndrome (chapter 43 works through this in full).

Gluteus maximus is also the muscle that most clearly exposes the limits of electromyography (EMG) — a surface sensor reading the electrical signal of a contracting muscle — as a guide to what to actually programme.

Who

Resistance-trained adults, randomised to a hip-thrust-focused or back-squat-focused programme.

What

Equated training over several weeks; gluteus maximus (upper, middle, lower) and deadlift-transfer measured directly with MRI, not estimated from EMG.

Found

Both groups grew all three regions of the gluteus maximus by a similar amount. EMG amplitude during the lifts did not reliably predict which region grew more.

Why it matters: it directly tests the assumption built into Fig 22.1 — that a higher EMG reading means more growth — and finds it does not hold. Limitation: one training study, moderate sample, trained lifters only; it does not establish that EMG is never informative, only that acute activation is a poor stand-in for a multi-week growth outcome. See chapter 26 for why.

The practical reading is not "EMG is useless" but "EMG tells you what is working hard today, not what will be bigger in twelve weeks." Programme gluteus maximus with a hip-extension-dominant lift at deep hip flexion — hip thrust, deep squat, step-up or Romanian deadlift — and expect similar results from more than one of them, rather than chasing whichever currently tops an activation chart. Gluteus medius and minimus, by contrast, respond to a narrower and more specific stimulus: controlled single-leg loading that challenges pelvic levelling directly, which a squat or deadlift does only incidentally.

The Adductors

The adductor group runs from the pubic bone down the full length of the inner thigh bone, and its textbook job — pulling the leg toward the midline — undersells it badly. The largest member, adductor magnus, has a posterior portion that runs almost parallel to the hamstrings and acts as a genuine hip extensor: in a deep squat, it contributes roughly as much hip-extension torque as gluteus maximus does. Training "the adductors" as a stretching target and ignoring them as a strength target misses most of what the muscle actually does under load.

The adductors are also one of the most commonly strained muscle groups in field sport, particularly where a programme is heavy on forward-and-back running and light on frontal-plane (side-to-side) loading — a gap chapter 1 flagged as the commonest blind spot in athletic training generally.

Calves & Tibialis Anterior — The Knee-Angle Rule

The calf is two muscles with one tendon, and which one you train is decided entirely by the angle of the knee. Gastrocnemius starts above the knee, so it crosses both the knee and the ankle; it only reaches long length, and therefore only gets a full training stimulus, with the knee straight. Soleus starts below the knee and crosses the ankle alone; bending the knee takes gastrocnemius out of useful length and leaves soleus to do essentially all the work. A standing, straight-knee calf raise trains gastrocnemius. A seated, bent-knee calf raise trains soleus. There is no third option and no exercise that meaningfully loads both at once.

That force number is not decorative. Scott and Winter's biomechanical modelling of running put Achilles tendon load at several times body weight per stride, with soleus bearing the larger share because it works on every stride regardless of pace, while gastrocnemius contributes more as speed rises B. Soleus's fibre type explains why: a muscle that fires on every one of 1,500–1,800 strides per kilometre has to be built for endurance, not peak force, and a muscle built that way needs high volume — many reps, done often — to be trained properly, not a handful of heavy sets. Most lifters train calves like a fast-twitch muscle. Soleus is not one.

“Calves don't grow, mine are just stubborn”

Years of standing calf raises and nothing to show for it.

Usually one muscle, trained one way

Standing straight-knee raises load gastrocnemius only. A lifter who has only ever done these has trained perhaps half of the calf's mass, and has never put soleus — the larger of the two in many people — through a long-length, high-volume stimulus at all. Add seated or bent-knee work before concluding the tissue itself is unresponsive.

Tibialis anterior, on the front of the shin, works in the opposite phase to the calf: it lifts the foot during swing and then controls how fast the foot drops after heel contact, acting as a brake rather than a driver. It is almost never trained directly, and its weakness is a plausible contributor to shin pain in new or returning runners (chapter 42), because a muscle asked to brake a few hundred times a kilometre without ever being strengthened simply runs out of capacity before the rest of the leg does.

Common Lower-Body Programming Errors

Three mistakes account for most of the wasted effort this chapter has implicitly been arguing against. The first is training a two-joint muscle — rectus femoris, the hamstrings — as if a single-joint exercise covered it; it does not, because long length for a two-joint muscle requires a specific combination of both joint angles, not just a deep range at one of them. The second is reading an EMG ranking as a growth ranking; Fig 22.1 and the Plotkin paper above show directly why that substitution fails. The third is ignoring the knee-angle rule for the calf and wondering why half the complex never responds.

A fourth, quieter error is volume mismatch between muscles with very different fibre profiles. Soleus, at up to 90% type I, tolerates and needs far more weekly volume than a fast-twitch-leaning muscle like vastus lateralis; programming them identically under-serves one or overloads the other. The pattern repeats through chapter 23 and chapter 24 and is worth internalising once here rather than re-deriving every time.

Five things to carry forward

  1. A muscle's growth stimulus is strongest at its long-length position, and for two-joint muscles — rectus femoris, the hamstrings — that position needs both joints positioned correctly at once, not just one.
  2. The hamstrings have two separate jobs, hip extension and knee flexion, and a programme needs an exercise for each; training only one leaves the other half-developed and the muscle more injury-prone at the length sprinting actually strains it.
  3. Gluteus maximus tops EMG charts under hip thrusts and hex-bar deadlifts, but controlled trials find hip thrust and back squat training build similar amounts of gluteus muscle — acute activation is not a reliable proxy for weeks-long growth.
  4. Adductor magnus is a genuine hip extensor, not just a hip adductor, and the Copenhagen plank is the best-evidenced single exercise for preventing the groin strains this group is prone to in field sport.
  5. The calf is two muscles governed by one rule: straight knee trains gastrocnemius, bent knee trains soleus, and soleus additionally needs high volume because it is built, at up to 90% type I fibre, for endurance rather than peak force.
