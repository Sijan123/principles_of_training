Every other chapter in this book answers a version of the same question: what should one session do? This one answers a harder question: what should three hundred sessions, spread over a year, do together, in what order, so that the qualities they build don't cancel each other out and the body arrives at the day that matters already primed rather than merely tired. Programming is not a separate science from physiology — it is physiology with a calendar attached.

The Hierarchy — Session, Microcycle, Mesocycle, Macrocycle

Four nested units of time organise everything in this chapter, and almost every argument about programming is really a disagreement about which level a decision belongs at. A session is one workout. A microcycle is the short block that repeats — usually a week, sometimes ten days — within which hard and easy sessions are sequenced against each other. A mesocycle is a few microcycles, typically 3–6 weeks, built around a single training emphasis (a strength block, a base-building block, a peaking block) and usually ending in a lighter week to absorb the accumulated fatigue. A macrocycle is the whole arc toward a goal — a season, a competition, a year — built from a sequence of mesocycles.

The Models — And What The Evidence Says

Periodisation is simply the deliberate variation of training volume and intensity over time, instead of repeating the same workout indefinitely. Four named models cover most of what is actually used.

Four periodisation models

LinearVolume starts high and falls; intensity starts low and rises, across a single mesocycleClassic strength-to-peaking progression; simple to plan and coach
BlockSequential mesocycles, each concentrating on one or two qualities (e.g. accumulation → transmutation → realisation)Advanced athletes who no longer improve everything at once and need focused, sequential emphasis

The headline finding, replicated across several meta-analyses, is reassuringly simple: some periodisation beats none. Structured variation in volume and intensity over weeks reliably outperforms repeating an identical programme week after week, for both strength and hypertrophy outcomes. A The second finding is less tidy and more useful to know, because it punctures a lot of confident coaching rhetoric: once total training volume is equated between approaches, no single model has been shown to clearly and consistently outperform the others. A 2022 systematic review and meta-analysis of volume-equated resistance-training programmes found daily undulating periodisation produced slightly larger strength gains than linear periodisation in already-trained lifters specifically, but found no consistent difference for hypertrophy, and no consistent advantage for either model in previously untrained participants. B

Moesgaard L, Beck MM, Christiansen L, et al. Effects of periodization on strength and muscle hypertrophy in volume-equated resistance training programs: a systematic review and meta-analysis. Sports Med 2022;52(7):1647–66.
Who

Pooled trials comparing periodised against non-periodised, and linear against undulating, resistance-training programmes.

What

Meta-analysis of 1RM strength and muscle hypertrophy outcomes, restricted to studies where total training volume was matched between the compared groups — isolating the effect of structure from the effect of simply doing more work.

Found

Periodised training outperformed non-periodised training for strength. Undulating periodisation outperformed linear periodisation for strength gains in trained individuals, but not in untrained ones, and neither model reliably beat the other for hypertrophy.

Why it matters: by equating volume, it isolates what the pattern of training contributes on its own — and the answer is "some structure helps, but the specific brand of structure matters far less than coaches often claim." Limitation: most included trials run weeks to a few months, not the years over which real periodisation decisions compound, and "periodised" versus "non-periodised" labels are not applied identically across the underlying studies.

“Linear periodisation is outdated; undulating is simply better”

So any programme still built in a straight line is behind the evidence.

The advantage, where it exists, is narrow and specific

Undulating periodisation's edge over linear shows up for strength, in already-trained lifters, in volume-equated comparisons — not as a general rule for hypertrophy, for beginners, or for endurance qualities. For most people most of the time, the choice of model matters far less than whether any sensible variation in volume and intensity is present at all, and whether the programme actually gets followed consistently.

Autoregulation — Adjusting Today Without Abandoning The Plan

A written programme is a prediction made weeks in advance about how recovered you will feel on a specific Tuesday. It is sometimes wrong, because sleep, stress, illness and the cumulative cost of previous sessions all move around in ways no spreadsheet anticipates. Autoregulation is the set of tools for correcting that day's prediction without tearing up the plan behind it.

The simplest tool is RPE (rate of perceived exertion) and its close relative RIR (reps in reserve, introduced in chapter 17): instead of a fixed weight on the bar, the session specifies a target effort — "work up to a set of 5 at RPE 8" — and the actual load used that day is whatever achieves that feeling, which naturally comes out lighter on a bad day and heavier on a good one without the lifter having to decide consciously to deviate. Velocity-based training (VBT) does the same job with a number instead of a feeling: a sensor on the bar measures how fast it moves, and because bar speed at a given percentage of 1RM is remarkably consistent for an individual on a good day, a slower-than-expected bar speed at a prescribed load is an early, objective warning that today is not that day, well before the lifter would otherwise notice. Simple readiness checks — a morning resting heart rate, a brief jump or grip-strength test, even a subjective wellness questionnaire — serve the same purpose at a coarser grain, flagging a day worth adjusting before the warm-up set does it for you.

A written training plan is like a recipe written for an oven whose exact temperature you won't know until you turn it on. The recipe says "bake at 180°C for 25 minutes" because that is what usually works. Autoregulation is simply checking the actual oven — is it running hot today, running cold today — and adjusting the time, not abandoning the recipe. You are still making the same dish. You are just not pretending every oven on every day behaves identically.

The discipline autoregulation requires is knowing which numbers to adjust and which to leave alone. Adjusting load or reps achieved session to session, within the planned structure, is autoregulation working as intended. Abandoning the week's planned emphasis entirely because one session felt hard is not autoregulation; it is programme-hopping with extra steps, and it is the single most common way autoregulation gets misused in practice.

Deloads — Planned, Not Earned By Exhaustion

A deload is a short, deliberately reduced-load microcycle, usually inserted every third to sixth week of a mesocycle, that exists to let accumulated fatigue dissipate before it either stalls progress or tips over into overtraining (see chapter 36). The signs that one is overdue are reasonably consistent across lifters and endurance athletes alike: performance on key lifts or sessions has flattened or slipped for two or more consecutive sessions despite normal effort, resting heart rate is persistently elevated above its usual range, sleep quality has degraded, and motivation for sessions that were previously enjoyable has quietly dropped. None of these alone is diagnostic; two or three together, sustained for most of a week, usually is.

A deload's structure matters more than its mere existence. Cutting volume — fewer sets, shorter runs, less total work — by roughly 40–60% while keeping intensity close to normal and keeping skill practice (technique work, sprint drills, race-pace touches) largely intact preserves the neural and technical adaptations that would otherwise erode, while still giving the tissues that actually need the rest — the ones running on the slower clocks from chapter 1 and chapter 19 — the reduced load they need. Cutting intensity instead of volume, or simply skipping the week's training altogether, tends to produce a week that feels like a holiday but detrains skill and intensity tolerance faster than it resolves the underlying fatigue.

The Taper — The Best-Evidenced Intervention In This Book

If deloads are a routine maintenance tool used throughout a macrocycle, the taper is the single, deliberate reduction in training load used once, in the final one to three weeks before the performance that actually counts. It is, by some distance, the most consistently evidenced performance intervention described anywhere in endurance sport, with a far larger and more reliable literature behind its exact parameters than almost anything else in this book.

Bosquet and colleagues' meta-analysis, pooling studies across running, cycling and swimming, found that the most effective taper strategy reduces training volume by roughly 40–60%, over a period of one to three weeks, while largely preserving training intensity and frequency. Done this way, the typical performance gain is modest in absolute terms but meaningful at the level elite competition is decided — on the order of 2–3% — and reducing volume gradually (an exponential, progressively steepening taper) outperforms an abrupt step-down to the reduced load. B

Bosquet L, Montpetit J, Arvisais D, Mujika I. Effects of tapering on performance: a meta-analysis. Med Sci Sports Exerc 2007;39(8):1358–65.
Who

27 studies meeting inclusion criteria out of 182 screened, spanning running, cycling and swimming.

What

Meta-analysis of how taper duration, the degree of volume reduction, and how that reduction was patterned (step versus progressive) related to subsequent performance.

Found

A 2-week taper with an exponential, 41–60% reduction in volume, holding intensity and frequency close to normal, produced the most reliable performance improvement, typically in the low single digits of percentage — small by everyday standards, decisive at competition level.

Why it matters: it converts "rest before the race" from vague advice into specific, actionable numbers that have been repeatedly supported by later work. Limitation: pooled across different sports and distances, the optimal exact percentage and duration still varies by individual and event, and the meta-analysis cannot tell a given athlete their own number in advance — only the range worth starting from.

A standard taper template, adaptable to a lift, a race or a key match.

  • Do Keep the same sessions and the same key intensities; remove volume, not the sessions themselves.
  • Dose Cut weekly volume by 40–60% over 1–3 weeks, reducing progressively rather than in one step.
  • Keep Intensity and session frequency close to normal — a short, sharp effort at race pace or near-maximal load stays in the plan right up to a few days out.
  • Avoid Introducing anything new — a new exercise, an unfamiliar long effort, a change in diet — during the taper window itself.
  • On the bike For a cyclist, hold 2–3 short efforts a week at or near race intensity (e.g. 3×4 min at goal race power) while cutting total weekly volume by around half over the final 10–14 days — the taper transfers directly and is some of the best-supported advice in the entire sport.
  • Signal You're doing it right when you feel slightly impatient and under-trained in the final few days — that sensation, uncomfortable as it is, is what a correctly executed taper is supposed to feel like.

Building A Year

Across a full macrocycle, some qualities genuinely compete for the body's limited recovery resources and are best sequenced — led by one quality per block while others are held at a maintenance dose (a much-reduced volume that preserves rather than builds a quality, usually a fraction of the dose that originally built it). Maximal strength, peak power, and the top end of aerobic development are the usual candidates for sequencing, for the reasons covered in chapter 20 and earlier in this chapter. Other qualities genuinely can run in parallel for most of a year without much penalty: general aerobic base, moderate hypertrophy work, mobility and the tendon- and bone-loading work from chapter 19, and technical skill practice in any discipline, which rarely competes meaningfully with anything else on the calendar.

Monitoring — The Short List And The Long List

Wearable technology has made it possible to track dozens of numbers every day. Most of them do not change what you do on Tuesday, which is the only honest test of whether a metric is worth collecting.

What's worth tracking, and what usually isn't
Worth trackingWhy
Session load (volume × intensity, however roughly)The only way to know whether the plan on paper matches what actually happened
Key-lift or key-pace performanceThe direct signal of whether training is working, read over weeks not days
Resting heart rate or HRV trendA simple, cheap flag for accumulating fatigue, read as a trend over a week, not a single morning
Sleep durationOne of the few inputs with a direct, well-evidenced link to recovery capacity

Often over-trackedWhy it rarely changes a decision
Single-day body weightDominated by hydration and gut contents; only the multi-week trend means anything

The test for any metric worth adding to a programme is simple: name, in advance, the specific decision a given reading would change. If no such decision exists, the metric is entertainment, not monitoring — interesting, perhaps, but not part of the plan.

Five things to carry forward

  1. Every programming decision lives at one of four nested levels — session, microcycle, mesocycle, macrocycle — and most confusion comes from solving a problem at the wrong level.
  2. Periodised training beats unperiodised training, but once volume is equated, no single model (linear, block, undulating, conjugate) reliably beats the others — structure matters more than the specific brand of structure.
  3. Autoregulation (RPE, RIR, velocity-based training, readiness checks) adjusts today's load within the plan; it is not permission to abandon the week's planned emphasis because one session felt hard.
  4. Deloads cut volume by roughly 40–60% while keeping intensity and skill work largely intact; a taper does the same thing once, for 1–3 weeks before a key event, and reliably produces a small but decisive 2–3% performance gain.
  5. A monitoring system earns its place only if a specific reading would change a specific decision — everything else is data, not programming.
