Every stride a runner takes sends roughly six to eight times body weight through the soleus, the unglamorous muscle buried under the calf that almost nobody trains on purpose. Lift weights twice a week for three months and that runner does not get noticeably heavier, does not get noticeably more muscular to look at — and runs the same pace at a lower heart rate and a lower cost of oxygen. This chapter is about why that happens, which tissues the gain actually comes from, and exactly how heavy, how often and in what order to load them, for runners and for cyclists, whose problem is the mirror image.

Why Strength Training Makes You Faster Without Making You Heavier

Endurance performance at a given pace comes down to how much oxygen you burn to hold it — your running economy — and strength training improves that number by roughly 2–8% in trained runners A, without the body-composition change that the word "strength training" tends to imply. Three mechanisms explain most of it.

Tendon stiffness. A stiffer tendon returns more of the elastic energy stored in it during ground contact and loses less of that energy to stretching floppily before it can recoil — it behaves more like a good trampoline spring and less like an old one. Heavy resistance training measurably increases tendon stiffness over 8–14 weeks (see chapter 1), and a stiffer Achilles and patellar tendon means less muscular work is needed to maintain the same leg stiffness stride after stride.

Neuromuscular efficiency. Heavy lifting trains the nervous system to recruit motor units more efficiently and reduces wasteful co-contraction of opposing muscles — the brake and the accelerator firing at once. A more efficient contraction produces the same force for a lower metabolic cost, which is the direct definition of better economy (see chapter 6 and chapter 16).

Delayed fatigue and fewer injuries. Stronger muscle and tendon tolerate more strides before form degrades, which is why economy measured late in a long race or a long run degrades less in strength-trained athletes. And tissue that tolerates more load before it is damaged is, definitionally, less likely to become the next overuse injury — see chapter 39.

None of this requires adding muscle mass, and at the doses below it mostly will not: low-volume, low-rep training performed two or three times a week produces far less of a hypertrophy stimulus than the high-volume training that builds a bodybuilder's physique (chapter 17). The strength gain is real; the added bulk, at this dose, is not.

Think of your tendons as the springs in an old trampoline. A slack, worn spring absorbs your landing and gives almost nothing back — your legs have to do all the work of pushing you back up. A taut, well-maintained spring returns most of what you put into it, for free, on every bounce.

Strength training is tightening those springs. You are not building a bigger trampoline. You are making the existing one give more of your effort back to you, stride after stride, for the whole length of a race.

A large, consistent beneficial effect on running economy (standardised mean difference −1.42), with no meaningful increase in body mass across the pooled trials.

Limitation: the pooled programmes varied in exact load and exercise selection, the runners were already well trained, and the review cannot tell you which single exercise or rep range did the most work.

How Heavy, and Why Light Circuits Fail Runners

The dose that improves running economy is heavy and low-volume: loads of roughly 80% of one-rep max or above, for 3–6 reps, two or three times a week. The dose that does not reliably improve it is the one many runners reach for instead — a "muscular endurance" circuit of light dumbbells and bodyweight moves for 15–20 reps, done because it feels more aerobic and less likely to leave them sore.

The reason is not mysterious once you look at the mechanisms in §41.1. Tendon stiffness and neuromuscular recruitment efficiency both respond to mechanical tension — how hard the tissue is pulled — not to how many times a light load is repeated. A systematic review of 26 studies in well-trained endurance athletes found heavy, low-rep strength training reliably improved economy, velocity at VO2max and time-trial performance, while lighter circuit-style training produced far less consistent results B. A light circuit trains local muscular endurance — a real quality, but one that running itself already trains directly, every time you run far. It does almost nothing for the tendon stiffness and high-threshold motor-unit recruitment that running cannot train on its own, because running rarely demands near-maximal force from any single muscle in one contraction.

“Heavy lifting will make me stiff and bulky, which is bad for running”

So light weights and high reps are the safer choice for a distance runner.

Backwards on both counts

The muscle mass gain from a two-session, low-volume heavy programme is small to negligible (chapter 17), and the "stiffness" that heavy training builds is in the tendon, which is exactly the adaptation that improves economy. The light-circuit alternative is the one more likely to waste training time without moving the needle on performance.

Plyometrics For Runners

Heavy lifting builds the capacity for force; plyometrics teach the tendon to use it fast. The stretch-shortening cycle — a rapid stretch immediately followed by a shortening contraction, as in a bounding stride — relies on tendon stiffness and reflex timing that only elastic, high-speed loading rehearses properly.

The progression runs from short, stiff ground contacts to longer, more demanding ones:

  1. Pogo hops

    Nearly straight knees, minimal ground contact time, bouncing from the ankle alone. This is both the entry drill and a simple readiness test — if contacts are slow or loud, the calf and Achilles are not ready for more.

  2. Bounding and skipping

    A-skips and bounding strides add hip drive and longer airtime, rehearsing the running cycle itself at higher force and rate.

  3. Depth hops and single-leg hopping

    The highest-force stage: landing from a height or hopping for distance on one leg, demanding both the strength base from §41.3 and the elastic timing from the stages before it.

Dose this by ground contacts, not minutes. A sensible entry volume is 30–60 foot contacts in a session, two or three times a week, building toward 100–150 as tolerance allows — broadly consistent with the 50–60 weekly impact landings described as a baseline osteogenic dose in chapter 1, with plyometric sessions adding to, not replacing, the impact of running itself C.

Readiness matters more than enthusiasm here. Before adding bounding or depth hops, a runner should be able to perform a single-leg calf raise for 15–20 clean reps and a controlled single-leg landing without the knee collapsing inward. Skipping this check is the most common way plyometric work turns into the next overuse injury rather than the next improvement.

Fitting It In

Twice a week is the dose with the most consistent support, and timing matters almost as much as the dose itself. The safest sequencing, confirmed by the concurrent-training research in chapter 20, is to lift after a run on the same day, or to separate a strength session from a quality running session by at least six hours. Lifting on legs that are about to run a hard interval session blunts both sessions; lifting after an easy run or on a non-running day avoids the conflict entirely.

In race week, do not drop strength work altogether — drop its volume, not its intensity. A short session with the same loads but a single set of each exercise, 4–6 days before the race, maintains neural readiness and tendon stiffness without adding fatigue. Stopping strength work completely for two or three weeks before a goal race measurably erodes the neuromuscular gains that took eight weeks to build.

Once the 12-week block in §41.8 is complete and gains have plateaued, a maintenance dose of one session a week, at the same loads, holds the adaptation for the rest of a training cycle C. This is a programming judgement rather than a directly tested number, but it matches the general principle that strength is far easier to maintain than it is to build (chapter 13).

Cyclists: A Different Problem

Running loads the body with impact and with eccentric muscle contractions — the muscle lengthening under tension at footstrike, which is also the single strongest mechanical signal for building bone. Cycling has almost none of either. The pedal stroke is concentric-dominant, the bodyweight is supported by the saddle rather than the skeleton, and there is no impact at all.

This makes competitive cyclists a specific exception to the "exercise is good for bones" rule. Studies of cyclists repeatedly find lower bone mineral density than runners or the general population, concentrated at the hip and spine — the sites loaded by impact in other sports and loaded hardly at all on a bike B. The same absence of eccentric loading leaves the posterior chain — hamstrings, glutes, lower back — comparatively undertrained relative to the quadriceps-dominant pedal stroke, which is why cyclists so often present with a strong front and a weak back.

The case for strength training in cyclists is therefore not primarily about running economy — cycling economy responds to heavy strength training too, and by a similar mechanism of improved neuromuscular efficiency and tendon stiffness — but about correcting two deficits running never creates. Squats, deadlifts and step-ups load the posterior chain the pedal stroke does not, and deliberate impact work — skipping, pogo hops, jumps, even if only two or three short sessions a week — gives the skeleton the loading signal that thousands of pedal strokes never will B.

Low-cadence torque work — riding at 50–60 rpm against high resistance, so each pedal stroke requires near-maximal force — is sometimes proposed as an on-bike substitute for heavy lifting. It does raise muscular and tendon loading above normal cycling, and it is a reasonable addition to a block when the gym genuinely is not available. It is not a substitute for either the posterior-chain strengthening or the bone-loading signal above: ground reaction forces during cycling remain close to zero regardless of pedal force, so the one thing low-cadence work cannot deliver is the impact that bone specifically requires C.

Five things to carry forward

  1. Strength training improves running economy by roughly 2–8%, mainly through stiffer tendons and more efficient motor-unit recruitment, not by adding muscle mass.
  2. The running muscles that most need direct loading — soleus, gastrocnemius, hamstrings at long length, gluteus medius, tibialis anterior — are precisely the ones running itself trains the least.
  3. Heavy, low-rep training (roughly 80% of 1RM or above, 3–6 reps) improves economy; light, high-rep "muscular endurance" circuits do not, because the adaptations that matter respond to mechanical tension, not repetition count.
  4. Plyometrics progress from pogo hops to bounding to single-leg hopping, dosed by ground contacts rather than time, and depend on a strength base built first.
  5. Cyclists face the mirror-image problem to runners — little impact and almost no eccentric loading — which makes squats, deadlifts and deliberate impact work for bone non-negotiable rather than optional extras.
