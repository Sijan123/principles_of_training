In 1966, five healthy young men spent three weeks lying in bed. When they got up, their capacity to use oxygen had fallen by 27% — a larger loss than the next thirty years of ageing would cause. Then they trained for eight weeks and came back fitter than they had started. That single experiment contains the whole argument of this book: the human body is not a fixed machine you are issued at birth. It is a structure that is continuously demolished and rebuilt according to what you ask of it.

Seven Levels, One Body

Everything in the chapters that follow happens at one of seven scales. A training session is a demand placed on the whole organism; the adaptation that results is a change in molecules. Keeping the ladder straight in your head is the difference between understanding training and memorising it.

Think of your body as a city that rebuilds itself every night according to yesterday's traffic.

No building is permanent. Every road, every power line, every warehouse is quietly torn down and replaced on a rolling schedule — and the city planner's only information is how much traffic used each route yesterday. A road carrying heavy lorries gets rebuilt wider and thicker. A road nobody drives on gets narrowed, then abandoned, and eventually the materials are carted off and used somewhere busier.

Training is not "building" your body. It is sending traffic down the routes you want widened. Rest is when the construction actually happens. And detraining is not a mysterious loss of something you earned — it is the planner doing exactly its job on a road that went quiet.

The Four Fabrics

Every structure in you is woven from just four kinds of tissue. Knowing which is which tells you immediately how fast something will adapt, how well it will heal, and how it will fail.

That last row is the one that gets athletes hurt. Connective tissue is the slow tissue. Muscle can gain strength measurably in a fortnight; the tendon it pulls on, the bone it inserts into and the cartilage it compresses are working on a schedule of months. Almost every overuse injury in this book is the same story in a different location: a muscular and nervous system that improved faster than the connective tissue holding them together.

Fitness and robustness are not the same adaptation and they do not arrive together. Your engine can be ready for a training load that your tendons, bones and cartilage are still six weeks away from tolerating. This mismatch — not bad luck, and rarely bad technique — is the single commonest cause of injury in people who are training sensibly in every other respect.

Connective tissue is one continuous fabric

It is tempting to picture muscles as separate parts bolted to bones, the way an engine diagram looks. The reality is closer to a single garment. The sheath around a whole muscle (epimysium) is continuous with the sheath around each bundle of fibres (perimysium), which is continuous with the wrapping of each fibre (endomysium), which is continuous with the tendon, which is continuous with the outer layer of the bone. Collagen runs through all of it without a seam.

This has a practical consequence worth holding on to: force does not travel only along the line you would draw between two attachment points. A meaningful fraction is transmitted sideways, into neighbouring tissue. It is part of why an isolated exercise never isolates as cleanly as the name suggests, and why pain so often appears somewhere other than the tissue that is actually overloaded.

Why Training Works At All

Your body defends a narrow internal range. Core temperature near 37 °C. Blood pH between 7.35 and 7.45. Blood glucose around 4–6 mmol/L. This defence is called homeostasis, and left alone it is profoundly conservative: it exists to keep things as they are.

Exercise is interesting precisely because it breaks homeostasis. A hard interval raises muscle temperature, drops pH, empties fuel stores, strains cell membranes and floods the muscle with calcium and metabolites. None of that is the adaptation. The adaptation is what happens next: the disturbance is detected by sensors inside the cell, which switch on genes, which build more of whatever would have made the disturbance smaller. Build enough mitochondria and the same interval no longer empties the fuel store. Build a bigger ventricle and the same oxygen demand no longer requires a frantic heart rate.

  1. Stimulus

    A session disturbs the internal environment — mechanical tension on a membrane, a falling ATP:AMP ratio, calcium flooding a fibre, a shear force on a vessel wall.

  2. Sensing

    Molecular sensors read the disturbance. Mechanical tension is read by proteins in the fibre's skeleton; low energy is read by AMPK; calcium is read by calcium-dependent kinases. These are not metaphors — they are specific proteins you will meet again in chapter 12.

  3. Signalling

    Signals travel to the nucleus and change which genes are being read. This begins within minutes and runs for hours to a day or more.

  4. Construction

    New proteins are built — contractile proteins, enzymes, mitochondrial components, collagen, transporters. This is the part that needs raw material (food) and time (rest).

  5. New baseline

    The next identical session causes a smaller disturbance. That is fitness. It is also why the same session eventually stops working, and why load must keep rising — the subject of chapter 13.

Who

Five healthy men, aged 19–21, studied in 1966 and traced again at age 50.

What

Three weeks of strict bed rest, then eight weeks of endurance training. VO2max, cardiac output and stroke volume measured throughout.

Found

Bed rest cut VO2max by 27% — mostly through a fall in stroke volume. Training restored it and then exceeded baseline. Three weeks of lying down cost more aerobic capacity than the following three decades of ageing.

Why it matters: it established, in the same five people, that aerobic capacity is dominated by a plastic variable — how much blood the heart moves per beat — and that the direction of change follows the direction of demand, in both directions, fast. Limitation: five young men, no control group, and strict bed rest is a far harsher stimulus than ordinary inactivity. It demonstrates plasticity vividly; it does not quantify what two weeks off training will cost you.

Everything Adapts — At Wildly Different Speeds

If you take one practical idea from this chapter, take this one. The reason training programmes are structured in weeks and blocks rather than days is that the tissues you are trying to change run on clocks that differ by a factor of a hundred.

Notice how much of the early improvement in any new training programme comes from the top three bars. In the first fortnight of lifting, you get much stronger without getting measurably bigger — because what changed was your nervous system's ability to recruit and coordinate, not the amount of contractile protein. In the first fortnight of endurance training, a good deal of the improvement is extra plasma, which makes each heartbeat move more blood. Both are real. Neither is the durable adaptation you are ultimately training for, and mistaking fast feedback for finished adaptation is how people get hurt in week six.

Sam, 34, 80 kg, sedentary for two years, starting training on Monday. What is actually available to change?
  1. Skeletal muscle is roughly 40% of body mass in an untrained man.80 kg × 0.40 = 32 kg of muscle
  2. Mitochondria occupy about 3–5% of the volume of untrained muscle, and endurance training can roughly double that.4% → 8% of 32 kg ≈ 1.3 kg → 2.6 kg of mitochondria
  3. Blood volume is about 70 mL/kg, and the first two weeks of training expand plasma by 8–10%.80 × 70 = 5,600 mL → +450–560 mL in a fortnight
  4. Stroke volume, untrained, is around 70–80 mL at maximum; well-trained endurance athletes reach 160–200 mL.75 mL → potentially 130–150 mL over years
  5. Realistic muscle gain in a first year of sensible lifting with adequate protein is 20–40% of a beginner's rate per month, i.e. roughly 0.5–1.0% of body mass per month early on, slowing steadily.≈ 5–9 kg in year one, then far less
  6. Achilles tendon stiffness responds to heavy loading over 8–14 weeks — long after Sam's runs feel easy.12 weeks before the tendon has caught up with the engine
So: within a fortnight Sam will feel dramatically fitter, and almost all of that will be extra plasma and better neural coordination. The mitochondria arrive over 6–12 weeks, the muscle over months, and the tendons and bones over a year. The programme should be paced by the last number, not the first.

Three conventions make the rest of the book readable. All anatomical description assumes the anatomical position: standing upright, facing forward, arms at the sides, palms forward.

The minimum weekly dose that sends traffic down every route in this chapter — the baseline the rest of the book builds on.

  • Aerobic 150 min/week of easy work you can hold a conversation through — walk, jog, cycle, row. Builds mitochondria, capillaries, plasma volume, stroke volume.
  • Intensity One session a week with 6–10 hard efforts of 1–4 min. Reaches the oxygen-delivery ceiling that easy work never touches.
  • Strength Two full-body sessions: one squat pattern, one hinge, one push, one pull, one carry. 2–3 sets each, stopping 2–3 reps short of failure.
  • Impact 50–60 landings a week above roughly 4 g — skipping, pogo hops, or simply running. This is the osteogenic signal; bone needs it specifically, and nothing else substitutes.
  • On the bike If joints, weather or time rule out running, an indoor trainer covers the aerobic and intensity lines entirely: 2–3 easy rides of 45–90 min plus one session of 4 × 4 min hard. It cannot cover the impact line — cycling builds almost no bone — so keep the landings separate (see chapter 44).
  • Range 10 min, 3×/week in the frontal and transverse planes (see Ch 40) — the planes your sport ignores.
  • Signal You're doing it right when the same easy pace drops your heart rate by 5–10 beats over six weeks, and the same weights feel lighter before they look different.

The Two Ways People Misread Their Own Body

“I'm either a fast-twitch person or a slow-twitch person — genetics decides what I can be”

So there is no point training the thing I'm not built for.

Partly true, badly applied

Fibre-type proportion is substantially inherited, and the heritability of VO2max is estimated at roughly 0.43–0.72 — genuinely large. But heritability describes the spread of outcomes in a population, not a ceiling on your improvement. Everyone studied improves with appropriate training; what varies is the starting point and the rate. Genetics sets the handicap, not the finishing line.

“Soreness means it worked; no soreness means it didn't”

If I'm not wrecked, I wasted the session.

Soreness tracks novelty, not stimulus

Delayed-onset muscle soreness is driven mostly by unfamiliar eccentric loading. It fades rapidly as you repeat a movement — while the adaptation keeps accruing. A trained lifter gaining muscle steadily is usually not sore at all. Chasing soreness selects for constantly changing exercises, which is precisely the habit that prevents progressive overload. See chapter 36.

Five things to carry forward

  1. Your body is continuously demolished and rebuilt, and the only instruction it receives is the load you placed on it recently — which is why detraining is not a punishment but the same process running in reverse.
  2. Adaptation is a response to disturbance: a session's job is to break homeostasis, and rest and food are when the rebuilding actually happens.
  3. Connective tissue — tendon, bone, cartilage, fascia — adapts an order of magnitude more slowly than muscle and nerve, and that mismatch causes most overuse injury in otherwise sensible training.
  4. Early gains come mostly from the nervous system and from extra plasma; mistaking that fast feedback for a finished adaptation is how people get hurt in week six.
  5. Almost all common training is sagittal-plane; the frontal and transverse planes are where most athletes are weakest and where they most often break down.
