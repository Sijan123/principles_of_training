Stand a sprinter and a marathoner next to each other at the finish and ask which one "ran out of energy" first. Trick question: both of them had ATP left over. What actually happened is that each one's fastest way of remaking ATP could no longer keep pace with how fast they were spending it, and a slower, less powerful system had to take over the load. Every event in sport, from a 100 m sprint to a six-hour ride, is a negotiation between three systems that remake the same molecule at wildly different speeds — and almost nothing is run on just one of them.

ATP Is The Only Currency

Muscle does not burn carbohydrate or fat directly. Every contraction is paid for by a single molecule, adenosine triphosphate (ATP) — think of it as the only currency the muscle's cash register accepts. Fat, glucose and creatine phosphate are just different ways of topping up the till. When ATP loses one of its three phosphate groups, energy is released and the cross-bridge in the sarcomere can cycle (see chapter 4); what is left is ADP, and the whole problem of "energy systems" is really the problem of how fast you can turn ADP back into ATP.

The surprising number is how little ATP is actually sitting in the muscle at any moment. A working muscle fibre holds enough to fuel maybe 2–3 seconds of all-out contraction before it would run dry if nothing topped it up. That is roughly one hard jump, or two strides out of the blocks. So the question a physiologist asks is never "how much ATP do you have" — it is always "how fast can you remake it, and for how long can you sustain that rate?" Three systems answer that question on three completely different timescales, and understanding training means understanding the handover between them, not memorising one in isolation.

Imagine a till with almost no float in the drawer, backed by three suppliers.

Supplier one is a runner standing right next to the till with a single pre-packed bag of exact change — instant, but the bag is small and once it's gone it takes minutes to repack. Supplier two is a worker in the stockroom next door who can keep bringing float through fast, using whatever is lying around (no need for the delivery van), but he's burning through the stockroom's own stock and will eventually have cleared the shelves nearest the door. Supplier three is a delivery van that brings in a near-endless supply from the warehouse across town — but it has to be loaded, driven over and unloaded, and that takes time to get rolling.

A 100 m sprint uses almost nothing but the runner with the bag. A 10 km race is almost entirely the van. Nearly everything interesting in training happens in the overlap between all three.

The Phosphagen System — Instant, Brief, Clean

The fastest supplier is the phosphagen system (also called the ATP–PCr system). Muscle stores a second high-energy molecule, creatine phosphate (PCr), purely as a buffer: an enzyme called creatine kinase instantly hands PCr's phosphate group to ADP, remaking ATP in a single chemical step. No oxygen is needed, no multi-step pathway, no waste product worth worrying about — just creatine and phosphate, both reused.

Its ceiling is set by how much PCr the muscle holds, and that store is small. Combined with the resting ATP already in the fibre, the phosphagen system can sustain maximal effort for roughly 8–10 seconds before its contribution falls away sharply A. This is why a 100 m sprinter does not actually slow down from fatigue in the way a distance runner does — elite sprinters are still accelerating or holding peak velocity past 10 seconds partly on momentum and partly because glycolysis is already contributing hard by then. The phosphagen system is also what refills fastest: roughly half of it is restored within 30 seconds of rest, and it is largely topped back up within 3–5 minutes — the physiological reason rest periods for maximal-power work are measured in minutes, not seconds.

Glycolysis — Fast, Powerful, Self-Limiting

Once PCr starts running low, the second supplier takes over: glycolysis, the breakdown of glucose (from blood or from stored muscle glycogen) into a three-carbon molecule called pyruvate. Done without oxygen, this is fast and can produce ATP at a very high rate — high enough to power most of a 400 m race or a minute-long maximal effort on a bike. Pyruvate's fate then splits: if the cell's oxidative machinery can keep up, pyruvate is shuttled into the mitochondria and burned fully (see chapter 8); if demand is outrunning that capacity, pyruvate is instead converted to lactate.

The acidosis story needs correcting

For decades, lactate itself was blamed for the burning legs and fading power of a hard effort — "lactic acid build-up." That account does not survive close reading of the chemistry. The step that produces lactate also consumes a hydrogen ion, and the enzyme that follows it (lactate dehydrogenase) is, on balance, acid-neutral. The fall in muscle pH during hard exercise is real and it does impair contraction — but it comes from the accumulation of hydrogen ions (H+) released elsewhere in rapid ATP turnover, not from lactate production itself B. Lactate is better described as a marker that travels alongside acidosis, not its cause. Other contributors to the fatigue of a maximal 60–120 second effort include potassium shifts across the muscle membrane, inorganic phosphate accumulation, and the central nervous system's own protective pacing — the whole picture is still debated and nobody should present fatigue at this duration as a single-cause problem C.

What lactate actually is: a fuel, and a signal. The biochemist George Brooks showed that lactate produced in one place — a hard-working glycolytic fibre, for instance — is routinely shuttled through the blood to be oxidised elsewhere: in neighbouring slow-twitch fibres, in the heart, in the liver (where it is recycled back to glucose), even in the brain. This is the lactate shuttle: lactate as a mobile energy currency that connects tissues making it faster than they can use it to tissues that can burn it immediately. Far from being metabolic litter, lactate may be the single most actively recycled fuel molecule in the body during exercise.

Oxidative Phosphorylation — Slow To Start, Almost Unlimited

The third supplier is the mitochondrion, running oxidative phosphorylation: burning pyruvate and fat fully down to carbon dioxide and water, using oxygen as the final step, and yielding far more ATP per fuel molecule than glycolysis alone. The catch is startup time. Oxygen has to be delivered by the lungs and heart, diffused into the fibre and used by enzyme machinery that takes tens of seconds to ramp up to a new demand — which is why the first minute of any hard effort always feels disproportionately difficult: the oxidative system hasn't caught up yet, and the first two suppliers are covering the gap. The full mechanism is the subject of chapter 8; the point for this chapter is capacity, not mechanism. Fuelled mostly by fat with carbohydrate as back-up, and limited chiefly by oxygen delivery and fuel availability rather than any store running dry in seconds, oxidative phosphorylation can in practice sustain output for hours — its capacity is, for training purposes, effectively unlimited.

The Systems Overlap Continuously

No real effort uses one system and then switches to the next like gears in a car. All three contribute from the first second; what changes with duration is the proportion. At the very start of any effort, the phosphagen system supplies almost everything simply because it is the only one that can respond instantly. By 30 seconds, glycolysis dominates. By roughly 75 seconds, anaerobic sources (phosphagen plus glycolysis combined) and aerobic sources are each contributing about half — the so-called 50/50 crossover point, identified from maximal-exercise studies and widely cited as a practical anchor B. Past that point the aerobic share keeps climbing, and by 4–5 minutes of maximal effort it has become dominant.

Lactate As A Window, Not A Verdict

Because lactate reflects the balance between production and clearance rather than "how much poison is in the muscle," blood lactate concentration measured during a graded test is one of the most useful single numbers in endurance testing (fully explored in chapter 35). At low intensities, lactate stays near resting levels because whatever is produced is cleared just as fast. As intensity rises, two inflection points appear: LT1, where lactate first rises detectably above baseline, and LT2 (sometimes called the lactate threshold or OBLA), where it begins rising sharply because production is now outrunning clearance. Training shifts both points rightward — the same lactate concentration is reached at a higher speed or power — without necessarily changing peak lactate at all.

Worked Example — What Actually Fuels An 800 m

Noah, 24, club-level 800 m runner, personal best 2:10 (130 seconds) for the two laps.
  1. At 130 seconds of maximal effort, Noah is well past the ~75-second crossover point, so aerobic supply is already the majority contributor — but nowhere near all of it.Using Gastin's pooled curve: roughly 60% aerobic, 40% anaerobic over the whole race
  2. The anaerobic 40% is itself split: the phosphagen system is essentially spent in the first 10–15 seconds (largely the first bend), so almost all of the remaining anaerobic contribution is glycolytic.~10% phosphagen (first 10–15 s) + ~30% glycolytic (spread across the race, heaviest in the first lap and the final sprint)
  3. That means Noah's 800 m is run on a split of roughly 10% phosphagen, 30% glycolytic, 60% oxidative — which is why the event is physiologically brutal: it needs sprint-level anaerobic power and a well-developed aerobic engine, more of both than either the 400 m or the 1500 m demands.
  4. The training implication follows directly: a session that only does long intervals at threshold pace develops the 60% but leaves 40% of race demand untouched; a session of only short sprints does the opposite.
So: Noah's weekly plan needs at least one session that stresses the glycolytic system directly — repeats of 200–400 m at or faster than race pace with incomplete recovery — alongside the aerobic volume, because the race is won or lost on a near-even split between "how much speed can you buy with lactate" and "how much oxygen can you deliver," and training only one half leaves the other half exactly where it started.

What This Means For Training And Testing

Every event taxes the three systems in a different ratio, and the practical skill of programming endurance and speed work is matching the session to the ratio the event actually demands — not to the ratio that feels satisfying to train.

This is also how to read a testing battery sensibly. A countermovement jump or a 6-second sprint test interrogates the phosphagen system almost exclusively. A 30-second Wingate test is built specifically to exhaust glycolytic capacity. A graded lactate-threshold test or a VO2max ramp interrogates the oxidative system and its delivery chain (chapter 9, chapter 35). None of these tests measures "fitness" in general — each measures one supplier, and a complete athletic profile needs results from more than one.

Three sessions, each targeting one system specifically — the backbone of any speed-and-power block.

  • Phosphagen 8–10 reps of 6–10 s maximal effort (hill sprints, sled push, flying sprints), full recovery of 3–5 min between reps.
  • Glycolytic 4–8 reps of 30–60 s at maximal sustainable effort, recovery roughly 3–5× the work time, so the next rep starts only partially recovered.
  • Oxidative Continuous or long-interval work of 8 min or more at an intensity you could sustain, with discipline, for an hour — see chapter 14 for the full framework.
  • Dose One phosphagen and one glycolytic session a week is enough stimulus for most athletes; stacking both in the same week as a hard oxidative session risks swamping recovery.
  • On the bike Phosphagen: 8 x 8 s all-out seated sprint, full gear, 4 min easy spin between. Glycolytic: 6 x 45 s at a pace you could not hold for 90 s, 3 min easy between. Both transfer the same stimulus indoors with less fall risk than track sprinting.
  • Signal You're doing phosphagen work correctly if peak power on the last rep is within 5% of the first; if it drops further than that, recovery was too short for that system.

Five things to carry forward

  1. Muscle ATP alone lasts only 2–3 seconds; every "energy system" is really just a different speed of remaking it, not a different fuel tank.
  2. The phosphagen system is instant and oxygen-free but exhausted in about 10 seconds; it also refills fastest, within 3–5 minutes of rest.
  3. Lactate is not the cause of exercise acidosis and is not a waste product — it is a actively shuttled fuel, and the burning-leg sensation comes mainly from accumulating hydrogen ions and other factors, not lactate itself.
  4. All three systems contribute from the first second of any effort; the widely cited 50/50 anaerobic/aerobic crossover sits at roughly 75 seconds of maximal exercise, not at a hard switch.
  5. Every event has a characteristic energy-system ratio, and a training plan or a test that stresses only one system leaves the others — and therefore part of race performance — untouched.
