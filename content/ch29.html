Muscle is never simply gained. It is won, meal by meal, in a tug-of-war that runs continuously whether you are training or asleep: protein being built on one side, protein being broken down on the other. A single resistance session does not create new muscle directly — it tips that balance, for about a day, in favour of building. Miss the window with too little protein, spread too thin across too few meals, and the session's signal arrives at a construction site with nobody there to receive the delivery. This chapter is about sizing that delivery correctly: how much protein, how often, from what, and what actually changes when the goal is to lose fat without losing the muscle underneath it.

Muscle Protein Synthesis And Breakdown — The Balance That Decides Everything

Muscle protein synthesis (MPS) is the process of building new muscle protein from amino acids; muscle protein breakdown (MPB) is the process of dismantling existing protein back into amino acids. Both run continuously, all day, whether or not you train. Net muscle growth is not a mysterious separate process — it is simply what happens when synthesis outpaces breakdown by enough, often enough, for long enough.

A resistance training session does something specific and time-limited to this balance: it raises MPS for roughly 24–48 hours afterwards, making the muscle acutely sensitive to protein — more responsive to the same dose of amino acids than it would be on a rest day A. Feed that sensitised muscle enough protein, spaced out across that window, and synthesis wins. Feed it too little, or bunch the whole day's protein into one meal while the rest of the day sits at near-zero, and breakdown claws back a larger share of what training achieved. The training creates the opportunity; the diet decides whether it is taken.

Picture a renovation site that is only staffed properly for about a day after each delivery of materials.

Training is the job that tells the foreman “get the crew in, there's building to do here.” For the next day or so, the site is primed — extra workers on call, ready to use whatever turns up. If the materials (amino acids) arrive steadily through that window, the crew builds. If no materials show up, or they all arrive at once in a single overloaded delivery while the rest of the day is empty, the crew stands around, the window closes, and the site reverts to ordinary wear and tear until the next job.

Daily Dose — Where The Plateau Actually Sits

The most robust number in this chapter comes from pooling dozens of trials. Across healthy adults doing resistance training, protein supplementation stops producing extra gains in muscle mass once total daily intake reaches roughly 1.6 g/kg/day — a meta-analytic plateau identified by Morton and colleagues across 49 randomised trials and detailed in this chapter's paper, below A. Below that intake, more protein reliably helps. Above it, the average extra benefit across a population of trial participants is small.

“Small on average” is not “zero for everyone,” and the honest caveat matters here. The confidence interval around that 1.6 g/kg figure is wide enough that some individuals — larger athletes, those doing very high training volumes, older trainees fighting age-related anabolic resistance, or anyone in an energy deficit (below) — plausibly continue to benefit from intakes up to roughly 2.0–2.2 g/kg/day B. Beyond about 2.2 g/kg/day, the evidence for any further muscle-building return essentially disappears, though the extra protein is not wasted in any harmful sense — it is simply oxidised for energy or, if total calories allow, stored, exactly like any other macronutrient eaten beyond what is being used.

Per-Meal Dose And The Leucine Threshold

Total daily protein is only half the picture; how it is divided across the day matters almost as much, and the reason is a single amino acid acting as a switch. Leucine is a branched-chain amino acid that, independent of its role as a building block, directly activates mTOR (mechanistic target of rapamycin), the master signalling pathway that switches muscle protein synthesis on. Below a certain leucine dose, mTOR barely responds; above it, synthesis rises sharply. This is sometimes called the leucine threshold, and it sits at roughly 2.5–3 g of leucine per meal for most adults — which in practice means a protein dose of about 0.3–0.4 g/kg of body weight, since leucine makes up roughly 8–11% of high-quality animal protein by mass B.

This explains why 3–5 feedings a day reliably outperform 1–2 at the same total daily protein: each feeding is a separate opportunity to clear the leucine threshold and switch MPS back on, and MPS does not stay switched on indefinitely after one large dose — it rises, peaks around 90 minutes, and subsides over a few hours even if amino acids are still circulating, a phenomenon researchers call the muscle full effect. One enormous 100 g protein meal a day clears the threshold once; four 30–35 g meals clear it four times. For most athletes that means roughly 25–40 g of good-quality protein per meal, taken four times across the day, rather than concentrating the day's total into breakfast and dinner alone.

Timing — The Window Is Hours, Not Minutes

The “anabolic window” — the idea that protein must be consumed within 30–45 minutes of finishing a session or the benefit is largely lost — is one of the most over-stated claims in sports nutrition. The sensitisation to protein described above lasts on the order of a day, not 45 minutes, and trials comparing immediate post-exercise feeding against the same protein eaten a few hours later, with total daily intake matched, find the difference is small to negligible B. Total daily protein and even distribution across the day are both far larger levers than the precise minute a shake is consumed.

That does not mean timing is irrelevant everywhere. Pre-sleep casein — roughly 30–40 g of a slow-digesting protein taken shortly before bed — measurably raises overnight muscle protein synthesis compared with no pre-sleep protein, because it keeps amino acids trickling into the blood through the long overnight fast when MPS would otherwise have nothing to work with for seven or eight hours B. This is one of the few timing recommendations in this book with decent mechanistic and trial support, precisely because it targets a genuinely long gap in intake, not a brief post-training moment.

Protein Quality — DIAAS, Leucine, And What It Means For Plant-Based Athletes

DIAAS (Digestible Indispensable Amino Acid Score) is the current standard for ranking protein quality: it measures how completely and how digestibly a protein supplies the specific amino acids the body cannot make itself. Whey, milk, eggs and meat score close to or above 1.0 — essentially complete. Soy sits around 0.9. Most grains and single plant sources score markedly lower, typically 0.4–0.6, both because they are lower in one or more essential amino acids (often lysine) and because plant cell walls make the protein somewhat less digestible than animal protein.

The practical consequence for athletes eating no animal products is threefold, and all three are manageable rather than disqualifying. First, total daily intake needs to run higher — often 10–20% above the targets in the table above — to compensate for lower digestibility. Second, blending sources (grains with legumes, for instance rice with lentils, or wheat with soy) covers each source's amino acid gaps with the other's strengths, producing a combined amino acid profile close to complete even though neither source alone is. Third, because plant proteins are generally lower in leucine by mass, per-meal doses need to be larger — often 35–40 g of a plant protein rather than 25–30 g of whey — to clear the same 2.5–3 g leucine threshold. None of this makes a well-planned plant-based diet inferior for building muscle; it makes the planning less forgiving of small portions.

Protein quality does not change the goal, it changes the arithmetic needed to hit it: a plant-based athlete reaches the same leucine threshold and the same daily total as an omnivore, just with larger portions and more deliberate source-blending.

Protein In An Energy Deficit — Raising The Target, Not Lowering It

Cutting calories is, from muscle's perspective, a second signal pulling the MPS/MPB balance toward breakdown, stacked on top of whatever training is doing to pull it the other way. The body in a calorie shortfall is actively looking for tissue to break down for fuel, and without a strong enough protein signal and enough resistance training stimulus, some of that tissue will be the muscle an athlete is trying to keep. The fix is not to eat less protein to save calories — it is to raise protein specifically, to roughly 2.0–2.4 g/kg/day, while cutting calories primarily from carbohydrate and fat B. Protein is also the most satiating macronutrient gram for gram, which makes a higher protein target easier to sustain through a deficit, not harder.

Protein For Endurance Athletes — The Neglected Macronutrient

Protein is usually the last thing an endurance athlete thinks about, and the gap in attention is a mistake. Long aerobic training increases amino acid oxidation during exercise (protein can supply a modest but real fraction of fuel in long efforts), drives meaningful mitochondrial protein turnover — the very adaptation most endurance training is trying to build — and places ongoing demand on collagen synthesis in tendon, ligament and bone matrix that absorb the repetitive load of running or cycling for hours at a time. None of this is primarily about building bigger muscles; it is about keeping pace with turnover in tissues that are working hard but rarely visibly growing.

The practical target, roughly 1.4–1.8 g/kg/day, is lower than a strength athlete's but meaningfully higher than the general-population figure many endurance athletes default to while focused on carbohydrate B. An athlete running 90 km a week who eats like a sedentary person protein-wise is quietly under-fuelling the repair of the tendons and mitochondria that the running itself depends on — the connection explored further in chapter 27 and chapter 19.

Endurance athletes chronically under-fuelling protein while also under-fuelling total calories are at risk of the same lean-mass loss and connective-tissue fragility covered under energy availability in chapter 34. If training volume is high and recovery feels slow despite adequate sleep, protein intake is one of the first things worth auditing honestly before assuming the problem is purely a training-load issue.

Building a day's protein around the leucine threshold rather than just a daily total.

  • Eat 25–40 g of good-quality protein per meal, 3–5 times across the day, rather than loading it all into one or two sittings.
  • Dose Daily total 1.6–2.2 g/kg if strength-training, 1.4–1.8 g/kg if endurance-training, 2.0–2.4 g/kg in an energy deficit.
  • Sources Any mix that clears ~2.5–3 g leucine per meal works; plant-only athletes should run per-meal doses 30–40% higher and blend sources (grain + legume) to cover amino acid gaps.
  • Timing Don't panic about the post-training minute; do prioritise a 30–40 g slow-digesting (casein or a mixed meal) dose before bed on training days.
  • On the bike Protein is not a session variable, but long indoor rides are where athletes most often under-eat it: a 3-hour trainer session burns through carbohydrate while quietly raising amino-acid oxidation too. For rides over 2 h, add 15–20 g of protein to your during-ride fuelling, and make the post-ride feeding a full 0.3–0.4 g/kg dose rather than a carbohydrate-only recovery drink.
  • Signal You're dosing correctly when strength or lean mass holds steady through a cut, and when easy-day fatigue and tendon niggles don't creep up through a high-volume endurance block.

Who

1,863 participants pooled across 49 randomised controlled trials of resistance training with and without protein supplementation.

What

Meta-analysis and meta-regression of total daily protein intake against gains in fat-free mass and strength, across training durations of six weeks or more.

Found

Protein supplementation significantly increased gains in muscle mass and strength overall; the meta-regression located the point of diminishing additional benefit at roughly 1.6 g/kg/day of total protein intake, with the regression model's upper confidence bound extending toward 2.2 g/kg/day.

Why it matters: this is the most heavily cited single figure in protein dosing for exactly the reason it deserves to be — a large pooled sample with a formal regression, not one small trial's result. Limitation: it pools healthy, mostly young trainees across widely varying training programmes and protein sources; it identifies a population-level plateau, not a hard individual ceiling, which is exactly why the honest range given in this chapter extends above 1.6 g/kg for specific contexts.

Five things to carry forward

  1. Training does not build muscle directly; it raises muscle protein synthesis for roughly 24–48 hours, and diet during that window decides whether synthesis actually outpaces breakdown.
  2. Daily protein intake shows diminishing returns above roughly 1.6 g/kg/day on average, with reasonable evidence that some individuals continue to benefit up to about 2.0–2.2 g/kg/day.
  3. Per-meal dose matters as much as the daily total: roughly 0.3–0.4 g/kg (about 2.5–3 g of leucine) is needed to fully switch on muscle protein synthesis, which is why 3–5 feedings a day outperform 1–2 at the same daily total.
  4. The “anabolic window” is hours wide, not minutes; total daily protein and even distribution across meals explain far more of the outcome than the exact post-training minute, though pre-sleep casein genuinely helps by covering the long overnight gap.
  5. Needs rise above the general target in three specific situations — an energy deficit (2.0–2.4 g/kg to protect lean mass), endurance training (1.4–1.8 g/kg for mitochondrial and connective-tissue turnover), and plant-based diets (higher totals and larger per-meal doses to offset lower protein quality).
