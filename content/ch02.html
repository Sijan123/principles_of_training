A skeleton sounds like the one part of you that should hold still. It does not. Every one of your 206 bones is being quietly dismantled and rebuilt right now, cell by cell, on a schedule that depends on how hard you have been leaning on it. A hip bone loaded by regular running is measurably denser within a year; the same bone in a limb kept in a cast for six weeks loses density fast enough to show up on a scan. The skeleton is not scaffolding. It is a lever system built from living tissue that reads its own workload and adjusts the hardware accordingly.

Bone Is a Living Tissue

Cut a cross-section of bone and it looks like stone: hard, white, inert. Under a microscope it looks like a construction site. Two cell types work the site in opposition. Osteoclasts are the demolition crew — they dock onto the bone surface, seal a small chamber against it, and pump out acid and enzymes that dissolve mineral and digest collagen, carving out a microscopic pit. Osteoblasts are the construction crew — they move into the pit the osteoclasts leave, lay down fresh collagen (osteoid), and mineralise it with calcium and phosphate crystals over the following weeks. A single one of these resorb-then-rebuild cycles, run by a local team of cells called a basic multicellular unit, takes roughly three to six months from start to finish. Multiply that by millions of sites happening on a rolling, staggered schedule across your skeleton, and the result is that something like a tenth of adult bone tissue is replaced every year — which is the arithmetic behind the often-quoted figure that your entire skeleton turns over roughly once a decade.

Think of a bridge that is repaired a rivet at a time while traffic keeps crossing it — never closed, never finished, always slightly different from the bridge it was last year.

Engineers do not repair every rivet equally. They watch which parts of the structure carry the heaviest loads and reinforce those first, while a walkway nobody uses gets patched minimally or left to rust. Your skeleton runs the same logic. The demolition-and-rebuild crews are not working from a blueprint of what your bones should look like; they are working from a live readout of what each bit of bone is actually being asked to carry.

That readout is the basis of Wolff's law, named after the 19th-century German anatomist Julius Wolff, who observed that bone architecture reorganises itself along the lines of mechanical stress it habitually experiences — thicker where load is high, thinner where it is not. A century later the orthopaedic surgeon Harold Frost reframed the same idea as the mechanostat: bone behaves like a thermostat for mechanical strain rather than temperature. Below a certain strain threshold, bone is allowed to be lost (disuse resorption dominates). Above a higher threshold, new bone is laid down (modelling and remodelling favour construction). In between, bone simply maintains itself. Where exactly those thresholds sit varies by bone, age and measurement method, so treat the specific strain values in older papers as illustrative rather than exact C — but the shape of the idea, a dial rather than a switch, is well supported.

This has a direct training consequence covered properly in chapter 13: because a full cycle takes months, bone cannot respond to this week's training the way muscle protein synthesis responds to this week's training. The signal has to repeat for a long time before the rebuilding crews outpace the demolition crews by a visible margin.

Joints: The Body's Hinges, Sockets and Gliders

A skeleton of rigid bone is only useful where the bones meet, and how they meet determines what the body can do there. Most joints that matter for movement are synovial joints: a fibrous capsule encloses the joint, lined on the inside by a thin synovial membrane that secretes synovial fluid, a viscous, egg-white-like lubricant. The bone ends themselves are capped in hyaline cartilage, a smooth, low-friction surface with roughly a tenth the friction coefficient of ice on ice.

Cartilage has one property that explains most of the orthopaedic bad news in this book: it is avascular — it has no blood supply of its own, and no nerves or lymphatics either. Every other tissue heals by the same basic sequence: a blood clot forms, immune cells arrive through the bloodstream, and a repair scaffold is laid down and gradually replaced with proper tissue. Cartilage cannot run that sequence because the blood never gets there. Its cells (chondrocytes) are fed by slow diffusion of nutrients out of the synovial fluid, through the cartilage matrix, which is why cartilage is so slow to turn over and why a significant cartilage injury does not scar over with more cartilage — it typically fills, if anything, with mechanically inferior fibrocartilage, and often does not fill at all.

Movement itself is what feeds cartilage. Diffusion alone is slow; joint motion repeatedly compresses and releases the cartilage like a sponge, pumping synovial fluid, and its dissolved nutrients and oxygen, into the tissue. A joint that is iced, braced rigid or simply never moved is a joint whose cartilage is being starved, not protected — which is the physiological reason a graded return to movement beats total rest after most joint injuries, and why a warm-up that actually moves a joint through range does more for that joint than static stretching alone.

Synovial fluid also changes its own physical properties with use. At rest it is relatively thick (viscous); as a joint begins to move, shear forces make it thin and flow more easily — a property called thixotropy, shared with paint and ketchup. This is the mechanical half of why a short general warm-up genuinely reduces joint stiffness rather than just feeling like it does: moving a cold joint literally changes the lubricant's behaviour within a couple of minutes, on top of the temperature-driven changes in muscle and tendon covered in chapter 19.

Levers and Moment Arms

Every joint that a muscle crosses is a lever. A lever has three points: a fulcrum (the pivot — the joint), an effort (the force applied — the muscle pulling on its tendon), and a load (the resistance being moved — gravity, a barbell, the ground). What a lever changes is not the amount of work done, but the relationship between force and distance moved, and the dial that sets that relationship is the moment arm: the perpendicular distance from the fulcrum to the line along which a force acts. Multiply a force by its moment arm and you get torque — the twisting effect around the joint, measured in newton-metres. A joint is in balance, or moving in a given direction, according to which side has more torque, not which side has more force.

This is the single idea worth carrying out of this section: the same muscular force produces more torque at a longer moment arm. A door is easy to push open near the handle (far from the hinge, long moment arm) and nearly impossible to budge by pushing right next to the hinge (short moment arm), even though your pushing force hasn't changed at all. Your joints work by the same rule, except the hinge is fixed by your skeleton and the "handle" position is set by where a tendon inserts and where the load is held.

The jaw is a useful place to see moment arms change outcome within a single lever. The temporomandibular joint is the fulcrum; the masseter muscle, inserting near the angle of the jaw, supplies the effort. Bite down on something between your molars and the load sits close to the fulcrum — short load-arm, large mechanical advantage, enormous crushing force. Bite down on the same object between your front teeth and the load arm roughly doubles or triples — the same muscular effort now produces much less force at the teeth, which is exactly why molars crack nuts and incisors do not, and why you instinctively move food toward your molars to chew anything tough.

The short effort arm that makes the calf such an efficient force amplifier is the same short effort arm that multiplies the load on the Achilles tendon — which is one reason Achilles tendinopathy is one of the commonest overuse injuries in running populations.

Why This Changes Your Training

Moment arms are not a side detail of anatomy — they explain several things that otherwise look mysterious in the gym.

Sticking points are a geometry problem, not just a muscle problem

A muscle's own force output already changes through a lift, for the length-dependent reasons covered in chapter 4. On top of that, the muscle's moment arm around the joint, and the external load's moment arm against gravity, both change continuously as the joint angle changes — because the geometry of where a tendon crosses a joint is almost never constant through the full range of motion. A "sticking point" in a squat or bench press is usually the joint angle where the combination of muscle force available and the mechanical leverage of the bar against you is at its worst simultaneously — not a mysterious weak spot, but the point in the range where biomechanics is working hardest against you.

Limb length changes the deal

A taller lifter with a longer femur has a longer moment arm between the hip joint and the barbell in a back squat, for the same depth and the same bar position. A longer moment arm on the load side means more hip torque is required to lift an identical bar weight — which is a mechanical, not a motivational, reason that long-femured lifters often squat less relative to their body weight than short-femured lifters of similar strength, and why limb-length-adjusted comparisons matter more than raw totals when judging someone's squat. The same logic runs in reverse for deadlifts and rows, where a longer torso or arm can shorten or lengthen the relevant moment arm depending on the exercise.

Cables, machines and free weights load the joint differently through the same range

A free weight's resistance is gravity: constant in size, fixed in direction (straight down), at all times. Whether that feels easy or brutal at any joint angle depends entirely on how long your moment arm is at that angle — which is why a dumbbell lateral raise is nearly weightless at the bottom and punishing near shoulder height, even though the dumbbell never changes. A cable or a well-designed cam machine can route the line of pull through a pulley or a shaped cam, which lets an engineer deliberately change the effective resistance through the range, often to track the muscle's own strength curve more closely and remove the "free" moment at the easiest joint angle. Neither approach is superior in general; they simply load the same muscle differently through the same range, which is a legitimate reason to use both rather than treat one as a substitute for the other.

A free weight is an honest, unhelpful training partner: it always weighs the same, wherever your joint happens to be.

A cable or cam machine is a training partner who adjusts their resistance to roughly match how strong you are at each point in the movement. Neither one is cheating. They are just two different tests of the same muscle, and a well-rounded programme usually wants both.

Loading Bone: Why Impact Beats Volume

If bone responds to mechanical strain, the obvious next question is which kind of strain works best. The answer, backed by both animal and human loading studies, is that magnitude and rate matter far more than total volume. A small number of high-intensity impacts produces more osteogenic (bone-building) signal than a large number of gentle ones, and beyond a certain point, adding more repetitions of the same gentle load barely moves the needle at all.

This threshold-and-saturation pattern was first mapped cleanly in animal experiments, where loading could be precisely controlled, and the general shape has since been echoed in human trials of jumping and resistance training. In practice it means that running a few extra kilometres a week does very little for bone density once a runner is already running regularly, because the strain magnitude of each footstrike barely changes. What does move bone density is adding a small number of much higher-magnitude impacts — jumping, hopping, change-of-direction work — or heavy resistance training, where the muscular pull on the bone itself, not just body weight, drives strain well above habitual levels.

Umemura Y, Ishiko T, Yamauchi T, Kurono M, Mashiko S. Five jumps per day increase bone mass and breaking force in rats. J Bone Miner Res 1997;12(9):1480–5.
Who

Growing rats, loaded via a controlled jumping apparatus, compared against sedentary controls.

What

Groups performed different numbers of jumps per day (from a handful up to many dozens), for eight weeks, with the ground-reaction acceleration of each jump controlled and measured.

Found

As few as five jumps a day, each well above a threshold acceleration, measurably increased bone mass and the force needed to break the bone. Beyond roughly 40–60 jumps a day, adding more produced little further gain — the response saturated.

Why it matters: it is one of the cleanest demonstrations that bone's response to loading depends on strain magnitude crossing a threshold, not on cumulative volume — a small number of hard impacts beat a large number of gentle ones. Limitation: rat bone and human bone do not remodel on identical timelines or thresholds, and these figures should be read as evidence of the pattern, not as a literal human prescription.

Resistance training raises bone mineral density through a related but distinct route: rather than ground-reaction impact, it is the tendon's pull on the bone at its attachment sites, under heavy, progressively increasing load, that provides the osteogenic signal. The evidence for resistance training improving or preserving bone mineral density in adults, particularly at the hip and spine, is consistent enough across trials to be graded B, though the typical effect size is modest — a few percentage points over a year — rather than dramatic, and is more reliably about preventing age-related loss than building bone far above a young adult's natural peak.

The minimum osteogenic dose — the loading pattern that actually reaches bone, as distinct from everything else in a session that trains muscle, heart and lungs instead.

  • Do Hopping, skipping, bounding, or any sport with jumping and sharp changes of direction.
  • Load Impacts that are clearly harder than a normal walking footstrike — a comfortable jog barely clears the threshold on its own.
  • Dose 50–60 good impacts, most days of the week; two or three short bouts of skipping cover this in a few minutes.
  • Add Two sessions a week of heavy, progressively loaded resistance training for the hips and spine — squats, deadlifts, loaded carries — which load bone through tendon pull rather than impact.
  • On the bike Cycling is the wrong tool for this one, and it is worth knowing why: it is non-weight-bearing and generates almost no high-rate impact, so it provides essentially no osteogenic stimulus. Cyclists who train only indoors must add hopping, skipping or running and heavy lower-body lifting deliberately.
  • Signal You're doing it right when the dose feels like "a bit of a jolt," not like background cardio; a jolt your joints tolerate well is exactly the stimulus bone needs.
B Impact loading and resistance training are each independently supported for bone; a cyclist's riding itself provides essentially none of this signal, because pedalling is low-impact and much of body weight is supported by the saddle — the impact dose above has to be added separately rather than assumed to come from time in the saddle.

Osteoporosis, Stress Fractures and the Female Athlete

Osteoporosis is a reduction in bone mineral density severe enough to raise fracture risk substantially, defined clinically by a bone density scan (DEXA) at least 2.5 standard deviations below the average young adult (a T-score of −2.5 or lower). It is the long-run consequence of the remodelling balance in section one tipping toward net resorption for years — through ageing, inactivity, low body weight, or hormonal change, most importantly the fall in oestrogen at menopause, since oestrogen normally restrains osteoclast activity.

A stress fracture is a different timescale of the same imbalance: not years of net bone loss, but weeks of loading that outpaces a single bone's capacity to remodel in response. Ordinary training causes microscopic damage to bone constantly; the remodelling cycle in section one is partly how that damage gets cleared and repaired. Increase training load faster than the repair crews can keep up — a sudden jump in running mileage is the classic trigger — and microdamage accumulates faster than it clears, eventually propagating into a visible fracture line, usually at a site of concentrated bending stress such as the tibia, metatarsals or femoral neck.

“Swimming and cycling are great for bone health — they're just as good as running, with less impact”

Less joint stress should mean the same benefit for less risk.

Low-impact, non-weight-bearing exercise does little to raise bone density

Bone needs the mechanical strain of impact or heavy resistance to trigger net building, from section one and five. Cycling and swimming are both excellent for the cardiovascular system and joint-friendly for rehabilitation, but surveys of competitive cyclists and swimmers repeatedly find bone density at the hip and spine no higher than, and sometimes below, sedentary controls — because the sport simply never delivers the strain signal. This is not an argument against those sports; it is an argument for adding a separate, deliberately impactful or resistance-loaded component alongside them.

Young female athletes carry a specific, compounding version of this risk, formalised by sports medicine bodies as Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport (RED-S), an expanded successor to the older "female athlete triad." The chain runs: insufficient energy intake relative to training load (sometimes unintentional, sometimes driven by pressure around body composition) suppresses reproductive hormone signalling, which reduces oestrogen, which removes its protective brake on osteoclast activity, which lowers bone density — stacking a hormonal driver of bone loss on top of whatever training load is already present. The practical warning signs are irregular or absent menstrual cycles, recurrent stress fractures, and persistent underperformance despite consistent training, and any combination of them in a young athlete deserves attention rather than being read simply as "training hard." The condition is recognised to also affect male athletes through analogous energy-availability pathways, though the evidence base there is thinner C.

Pain that is sharp, localised to one spot on a bone, and gets worse with continued loading rather than warming up out of it is the classic presentation of an early stress fracture — and the classic coaching mistake is to treat it as a muscular niggle and keep training through it. Because the remodelling cycle runs on a timescale of months, "a few more days" of continued loading on a bone already losing the microdamage race can be the difference between a short rest and a fracture that takes it out of action for a season. See chapter 39 for how training load itself is tracked to prevent this.

Five things to carry forward

  1. Bone is continuously demolished by osteoclasts and rebuilt by osteoblasts on a three-to-six-month cycle, and which direction wins at any site is set by how much mechanical strain that site has recently carried.
  2. Cartilage has no blood supply of its own and is fed by diffusion from synovial fluid driven by joint movement, which is why it adapts and heals far more slowly than almost any other tissue in the body.
  3. Torque, not force alone, decides what happens at a joint — the same muscular force produces more turning effect at a longer moment arm, which is why sticking points, limb-length effects on lifts, and the different feel of cables versus free weights are all the same underlying idea.
  4. Bone responds to the magnitude and rate of loading far more than to its volume: a handful of high-impact landings a day does more for bone density than hours of low-impact cardio ever will.
  5. Osteoporosis and stress fracture are the same imbalance — resorption outpacing formation — playing out on different timescales, and in young female athletes that imbalance can be driven as much by insufficient energy intake as by training load itself.
